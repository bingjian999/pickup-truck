Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests Overview IFRS 10 Consolidated Financial Statements sets out the principles for the presentation and preparation of consolidated financial statements when an entity controls one or more other entities.
# Chapter 7 discusses the requirements of IFRS 10 relating to the concepts underlying control of an entity (a
subsidiary) by its parent, the requirement to prepare consolidated financial statements and what subsidiaries are to be consolidated within a set of consolidated financial statements. It also covers the definition of an investment entity and the exception from consolidating particular subsidiaries of an investment entity. The development, objective, and scope of IFRS 10 are dealt with in Chapter 7 at sections 1.2 and 2. This chapter deals with the accounting requirements of IFRS 10 relating to the preparation of consolidated financial statements. In particular, it covers topics such as: consolidation procedures, when to commence or cease consolidation, how to account for the acquisition of a subsidiary that is not a business (business combinations are addressed in Chapter 10), how to account for a loss of control (including deemed disposals and distributions in kind), how to account for changes in ownership interest without a loss of control, and how to account for non-controlling interests (NCI). The chapter also covers the accounting for put and call options over NCI (‘NCI puts and calls’). Finally, the chapter includes a section on future developments being considered by the IASB or the IFRS IC. What you need to know • Consolidated financial statements represent the financial statements of a group (i.e., the parent and its subsidiaries) in which the assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses and cash flows of the parent and its subsidiaries are presented as those of a single economic entity. • Investment entities do not consolidate particular subsidiaries, instead including them at fair value through profit or loss. IFRS 10 provides specific guidance on the definition of and accounting for investment entities. • If the subsidiary is not wholly-owned, the profit or loss and each component of other comprehensive income (OCI) of the subsidiary, and the equity of the subsidiary, are attributed to the parent’s interest and to NCI. This allocation is usually done on the basis of existing ownership interests. IFRS 3 Business Combinations addresses the initial measurement of NCI arising from a business combination. However, IFRS accounting standards do not address the initial measurement of NCI arising from an acquisition that is not a business and different approaches have developed. • A parent consolidates a subsidiary from the date on which the parent first obtains control, and ceases consolidating that subsidiary on the date on which the parent loses control. • On a loss of control of a subsidiary, the group deconsolidates the assets and liabilities and any NCI relating to a former subsidiary. Any retained interest in the former subsidiary is measured at fair value. A gain or loss is recognised in profit or loss equal to the fair values of the consideration received and retained interest less the carrying amounts of the assets and liabilities and any NCI in the subsidiary that is deconsolidated. Certain elements of OCI relating to the former subsidiary are required to be reclassified to profit or loss on a loss of control. • Where a subsidiary is sold (or contributed) to an associate or joint venture accounted using the equity method, conflicts between IFRS 10 (which requires a full gain or loss to be recognised in profit or loss on loss of control of the subsidiary) and IAS 28 Investments in Associates and Joint Ventures (which requires a partial gain or loss only to be recognised in a ‘downstream transaction’) have led to diversity in accounting approaches. The IASB and IFRS IC have also discussed other situations involving the loss of control of a subsidiary where it was unclear whether the requirements of IFRS 10 and/or other IFRS accounting standards applied. • Increases or decreases in the ownership interest in a subsidiary that do not involve a loss of control are accounted for in equity. There is no change to the assets (including goodwill) and liabilities recognised in the consolidated financial statements but there may be reallocations in equity between the parent’s interest and NCI in the subsidiary. • IFRS 10 does not directly address the accounting for NCI puts and calls. IAS 32 Financial Instruments: Presentation requires that a financial liability is recognised for the present value of the redemption price for an NCI put. There is diversity of accounting approaches in accounting for NCI puts and this issue has been addressed in the IASB’s Financial Instruments with the Characteristics of Equity Project. An Exposure Draft Financial Instruments with the Characteristics of Equity was published in November 2023.

For example, a parent may purchase shares in a subsidiary held by NCI. By acquiring some (or all) of the NCI, the parent will be allocated a greater proportion of the profits or losses of the subsidiary in periods after the additional interest is acquired. [IFRS 10.BCZ175]. Therefore, the profit or loss and OCI of the subsidiary for the part of the reporting period prior to the transaction are attributed to the owners of the parent and the NCI based on their ownership interests prior to the transaction. Following the transaction, the profit or loss and OCI of the subsidiary are attributed to the owners of the parent and the NCI based on their new ownership interests after the transaction. 2.3 Consolidating foreign operations IFRS 10 does not specifically address how to consolidate subsidiaries that are foreign operations. As explained in IAS 21 The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates, an entity is permitted to present its financial statements in any currency (or currencies). If the presentation currency differs from the entity’s functional currency, it needs to translate its results and financial position into the presentation currency. Therefore, when a group contains individual entities with different functional currencies, the results and financial position of each entity are translated into the presentation currency of the consolidated financial statements. [IAS 21.38]. The requirements of IAS 21 in respect of this translation process are explained in Chapter 16 at section 6. A reporting entity comprising a group with intermediate holding companies is permitted to adopt either the ‘direct method’ or the ‘step-by-step method’ of consolidation. IFRIC 16 Hedges of a Net Investment in a Foreign Operation refers to these methods as follows: • Direct method - The financial statements of the foreign operation are translated directly into the functional currency of the ultimate parent. • Step-by-step method - The financial statements of the foreign operation are first translated into the functional currency of any intermediate parent(s) and then translated into the functional currency of the ultimate parent (or the presentation currency, if different). An entity has an accounting policy choice of which method to use, which it must apply consistently for all foreign operations. [IFRIC 16.17]. Both methods will result in the same amounts in the presentation currency for the statement of financial position. However, this does not necessarily hold true for income and expense items particularly if an indirectly held foreign operation is disposed of (as the different methods impact the amount of cumulative retranslation reserve subsequently reclassified to profit or loss). [IFRIC 16.BC36]. Differences will also arise between the two methods if an average rate is used, although these are likely to be insignificant. See Chapter 16 at sections 6.1.1, 6.1.4 and 6.6.3 for further discussion and a comparison of the effect of the two approaches on accounting for a disposal of an indirectly held foreign operation. IFRIC 16 also provides guidance on what does and does not constitute a valid hedge of a net investment in a foreign operation, and on how an entity determines the amounts to be reclassified from equity to profit or loss for both the hedging instrument and the hedged item, where the foreign operation is disposed of. It notes that in a disposal of a subsidiary by an intermediate parent, the use of the ‘step-by-step method’ of consolidation may result in the reclassification to profit or loss of a different amount from that used to determine hedge effectiveness. An entity can eliminate this difference by determining the amount relating to that foreign operation that would have arisen if the entity had used the ‘direct method’ of consolidation. However, IAS 21 does not require an entity to make this adjustment. Instead, it is an accounting policy choice that should be followed consistently for all net investments. [IFRIC 16.17]. IFRIC 16 is discussed in more detail in Chapter 16 at sections 6.1.4 and 6.6.3 and Chapter 49 at section 5.3.

Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests 2.5 Non-coterminous accounting periods The financial statements of the parent and its subsidiaries used in the preparation of the consolidated financial statements shall have the same reporting date. If the end of the reporting period of the parent is different from that of a subsidiary, the subsidiary must prepare, for consolidation purposes, additional financial information as of the same date as the financial statements of the parent, unless it is impracticable to do so. [IFRS 10.21, IFRS 10.B92]. ‘Impracticable’ presumably means when the entity cannot apply the requirement after making every reasonable effort to do so. [IAS 1.7]. If it is impracticable for the subsidiary to prepare such additional financial information, then the parent consolidates the financial information of the subsidiary using the most recent financial statements of the subsidiary. These must be adjusted for the effects of significant transactions or events that occur between the date of those financial statements and the date of the consolidated financial statements. [IFRS 10.21, IFRS 10.B93]. How we see it IFRS 10 does not give examples of ‘significant transactions or events’ that must be adjusted. However, there will commonly be items that will need to be eliminated as part of the consolidation process such as distributions from the subsidiary to the parent, cash transfers or settlement of intragroup balances. Care is needed to ensure such transactions are properly eliminated in order to avoid double counting effects, e.g., where assets transferred intragroup might otherwise be reported by both the subsidiary and parent. Significant transactions could also include significant acquisitions or disposals, impairments or developments on contingencies or provisions in the intervening period between the dates of the subsidiary and parent financial statements. The difference between the date of the subsidiary’s financial statements and that of the consolidated financial statements must not be more than three months. The length of the reporting periods and any difference between the dates of the financial statements must be the same from period to period. [IFRS 10.21, IFRS 10.B93]. It is not necessary, as in some national GAAPs, for the subsidiary’s reporting period to end before that of its parent. How we see it The requirements relating to coterminous accounting periods seem to imply that, where a subsidiary that was previously consolidated using non-coterminous financial statements is now consolidated using coterminous financial statements (i.e., the subsidiary changed the end of its reporting period), comparative information is restated so that financial information of the subsidiary is included in the consolidated financial statements for an equivalent period in each period presented. However, other approaches not involving restatement of comparatives might sometimes be acceptable (as discussed below). Other approaches not involving restatement of comparatives might be acceptable, particularly where the comparative information had already reflected the effects of significant transactions or events during the period between the date of the subsidiary’s financial statements and the date of the consolidated financial statements. Where comparatives are not restated, additional disclosures might be needed about the treatment adopted and the impact on the current period of including information for the subsidiary for a period different from that of the parent. IAS 21 addresses what exchange rate is used in translating the assets and liabilities of a foreign operation that is consolidated on the basis of financial statements made up to a different date to the reporting date used for the reporting entity’s financial statements. [IAS 21.46]. This issue is discussed further in Chapter 16 at section 6.4. 2.6 Consistent accounting policies IFRS 10 requires the use of consistent accounting policies by the group in consolidated financial statements. Accordingly, if a member of the group uses accounting policies that differ from those adopted in the consolidated financial statements for like transactions and events in similar circumstances, appropriate adjustments are made to that group member’s financial statements in preparing the consolidated financial statements to ensure conformity with the group’s accounting policies. [IFRS 10.21, IFRS 10.B87].
Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests Changes in control 3.1 Commencement and cessation of consolidation A parent consolidates a subsidiary from the date on which the parent first obtains control, and ceases consolidating that subsidiary on the date on which the parent loses control. [IFRS 10.20, IFRS 10.21, IFRS 10.B88]. IFRS 3 defines the acquisition date, which is the date on which the acquirer obtains control of the acquiree, [IFRS 3.8, IFRS 3 Appendix A], (see Chapter 10 at section 4.2). The requirement to continue consolidating (albeit in a modified form) also applies to a subsidiary held for sale accounted for under IFRS 5 Non-current Assets Held for Sale and Discontinued Operations (see Chapter 5). 3.1.1 Acquisition of a subsidiary that is not a business The same basic requirement to consolidate a subsidiary (see section 3.1 above) also applies when a parent acquires a controlling interest in an entity that is not a business, as defined in IFRS 3 (see Chapter 10 at section 3.2). [IFRS 3.3, IFRS 3 Appendix A, IFRS 3.B7-B12D]. As required by IFRS 10, the parent consolidates all investees that it controls, not just those that are businesses, and recognises any NCI in the subsidiary (see section 5 below). IFRS 3 states that when an entity acquires an asset or group of assets that is not a business, the acquirer allocates the cost of the group between the individual identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed based on their relative fair values at the date of purchase. Such a transaction or event does not give rise to goodwill. [IFRS 3.2(b)]. The cost of the group of assets is the sum of all consideration given, any NCI recognised, and transaction costs incurred, if any. The allocation of the transaction price (or cost) to the identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed is discussed further in Chapter 10 at section 2.2.2. However, neither IFRS 10 nor IFRS 3 address how NCI in the entity is initially measured, where the entity is not a business. How we see it In our view, if the NCI has a present ownership interest and is entitled to a proportionate share of net assets upon liquidation, the acquirer of a subsidiary that is not a business has a choice to recognise the NCI on initial measurement in one of the following ways:
(a) Its proportionate share of the fair values of the entity’s identifiable net assets
(b) Its fair value (measured in accordance with IFRS 13 Fair Value Measurement)
(c) Its proportionate share of the consolidated book values of the net assets, including transaction costs. The
basis for including transaction costs is that these are included in the consolidated book values of the subsidiary’s net assets Or
(d) Its proportionate share of the consolidated book values of the net assets, excluding transaction costs. The

Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests 3.1.2 Step acquisition of an associate or joint venture (equity accounted) that is not a business and becomes a subsidiary An entity that has an interest in an associate or joint venture that does not constitute a business may obtain control and so become a parent of a subsidiary that does not constitute a business. Current IFRS accounting standards do not address the accounting treatment for an acquisition in stages from associate or joint venture to a subsidiary that is considered as an asset acquisition rather than a business combination. This raises questions as to how to measure the previously held interest and the NCI on acquisition. 3.1.2.A Measuring the previously held interest As discussed in section 3.1.1 above, the cost of the group of assets is the sum of all the consideration given (including transaction costs incurred, if any) and any NCI recognised. It is therefore necessary to determine the ‘cost’ of the previously held interest, being part of the consideration given for the group of assets acquired. [IFRS 3.2(b)]. In January 2019, the IFRS IC issued an agenda decision regarding a different, but relevant, example of the step acquisition of an investment in a subsidiary accounted for at cost in the separate financial statements of the parent. The IFRS IC concluded that a reasonable reading of the requirements in IFRS accounting standards could result in the application of either one of two approaches to determine ‘cost’: the ‘fair value as deemed cost approach’ or the ‘accumulated cost approach’.3 See
# Chapter 9 at section 2.1.1.C.
How we see it Given the IFRS IC agenda decision relating to a step acquisition of a subsidiary in separate financial statements, in our view, the following approaches are both acceptable methods of measuring the previously held interest in the associate or joint venture: • Approach 1 Fair value as deemed cost • Approach 2 Equity-accounted carrying amount as accumulated cost Consistent with that agenda decision, any adjustment to remeasure the previously held interest to its fair value meets the definition of income or expense in the Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting and is, therefore, recognised in profit or loss. Approach 1 Fair value as deemed cost Where a ‘fair value as deemed cost approach’ is applied, the fair value of the previously held interest at the date that control is obtained is deemed to be the cost for the purposes of accounting for the acquisition of the subsidiary. Under this approach, the transaction is viewed as if the entity is exchanging its initial interest (plus consideration for the additional interest) for a controlling interest in the investee (‘exchange view’). As noted above, any adjustment to remeasure the previously held interest to its fair value is recognised in profit or loss. Approach 2 Equity-accounted carrying amount as accumulated cost Following an ‘accumulated cost approach’, the carrying amount of the associate or joint venture could be considered the relevant cost in accounting for the acquisition of the subsidiary. Although there is a change in status, it is not appropriate to remeasure the existing ownership interest as the subsidiary is not a business. Accordingly, no gain or loss is recorded on the acquisition of the subsidiary, assuming that the carrying amount of the new subsidiary is recoverable. In certain circumstances, it might be acceptable to take a third approach and consider the cost of the previously held interest to be the accumulated historical cost consideration for the investment in the associate or joint venture. Given the agenda decision of the IFRS IC mentioned above, any difference arising between the previous carrying amount and the accumulated historical costs will be recognised in profit or loss. However, caution needs to be taken with using this approach as it does not always provide relevant information. If the accumulated historical cost consideration was lower than the equity-accounted carrying amount of the previous associate or joint venture, there would be a loss recognised, even though there is no economic loss arising from the transaction. Where the accumulated historical cost consideration exceeds the equity-accounted carrying amount of the previous associate or joint venture, care is needed when using this approach to ensure that the subsidiary is not carried at above its recoverable amount.

Where a transaction falls in scope of both IFRS 10.25 and IAS 28.28, we believe that there is a choice of which accounting standards takes precedence, to be applied consistently. Where a transaction falls outside the scope of both standards, an appropriate accounting policy must be determined, with regard to the requirements of IAS 8.

Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests As set out in section 3.2 above, in accounting for a loss of control of a subsidiary, a parent is required, among other matters, to: • Derecognise the assets and liabilities of the subsidiary • Recognise any investment retained in the former subsidiary at fair value at the date when control is lost • Recognise any resulting gain or loss in profit or loss However, it is unclear how these requirements are to be applied when the retained interest is in the assets and liabilities of a joint operation. One view is that the retained interest is remeasured at fair value. Another view is that the retained interest is not derecognised or remeasured at fair value, but continues to be recognised and measured at its carrying amount. In July 2016, the IFRS IC discussed whether an entity should remeasure its retained interest in the assets and liabilities of a joint operation when the entity loses control of a business, or an asset or group of assets that is not a business. In the transaction discussed, the entity either retains joint control of a joint operation or is a party to a joint operation (with rights to assets and obligations for liabilities) after the transaction. The IFRS IC noted that IFRS 11.B34-B35 specify that an entity recognises gains or losses on the sale or contribution of assets to a joint operation only to the extent of the other parties’ interests in the joint operation. [IFRS 11.22, IFRS 11.B34, IFRS 11.B35]. The requirements in these paragraphs could be viewed as conflicting with the requirements in IFRS 10, which specify that an entity remeasures any retained interest when it loses control of a subsidiary. The IFRS IC observed that the IASB had issued amendments to IFRS 10 and IAS 28 in September 2014 to address the accounting for the sale or contribution of assets to an associate or a joint venture. Those amendments (which are discussed in section 3.3.2.B above and section 7.1 below) address a similar conflict that exists between the requirements in IFRS 10 and IAS 28. The IASB decided to defer the effective date of the amendments to IFRS 10 and IAS 28 and further consider a number of related issues at a later date. Because of the similarity between the transaction discussed by the IFRS IC and a sale or contribution of assets to an associate or a joint venture (see section 3.3.2 above), the IFRS IC concluded that the accounting for the two types of transactions should be considered concurrently by the IASB. Consequently, the IFRS IC decided not to add this issue to its agenda but, instead, to recommend that the IASB consider the issue at the same time that it further considers the accounting for the sale or contribution of assets to an associate or a joint venture.25 The interaction of IFRS 10 and IAS 28 has subsequently been considered as part of the IASB’s equity method project (see section 7.1 below). How we see it Where an entity loses control of a former subsidiary, and retains an interest in a joint operation, there is a conflict between the requirements of IFRS 10 and IFRS 11 which means it is unclear whether remeasurement of the retained interest to its fair value is required or allowed. Until the IASB issues further guidance, we believe that, where a parent loses control over a subsidiary but retains an interest in a joint operation that is a business, entities have an accounting policy choice as to whether to remeasure the retained interest at fair value or not. 3.4 Loss of control in multiple arrangements IFRS 10 only allows a parent to recognise a gain or loss on disposal of a subsidiary when the parent loses control over it. In addition, IFRS 10 has the following guidance: [IFRS 10.26, IFRS 10.B97] Extract from IFRS 10 B97 A parent might lose control of a subsidiary in two or more arrangements (transactions). However, sometimes circumstances indicate that the multiple arrangements should be accounted for as a single transaction. In determining whether to account for the arrangements as a single transaction, a parent shall consider all the terms and conditions of the arrangements and their economic effects. One or more of the following indicate that the parent should account for the multiple arrangements as a single transaction: (a) They are entered into at the same time or in contemplation of each other. (b) They form a single transaction designed to achieve an overall commercial effect. (c) The occurrence of one arrangement is dependent on the occurrence of at least one other arrangement. (d) One arrangement considered on its own is not economically justified, but it is economically justified when considered together with other arrangements. An example is when a disposal of shares is priced below market and is compensated for by a subsequent disposal priced above market. These indicators clarify that arrangements that are part of a package are accounted for as a single transaction. How we see it Judgement is needed in determining whether the circumstances really do support multiple arrangements being accounted as a single transaction since there is a risk that an entity might end up accounting for a transaction that is truly separate as part of the transaction in which the loss of control occurred. IFRS 10 is silent on how an entity accounts for multiple arrangements that are part of a single transaction. Depending on the facts and circumstances, the parent accounts for these transactions as either an advance payment or as an immediate disposal. Advance payment - If the parent does not lose control over the subsidiary and access to the benefits associated with ownership until later steps in the transaction, then it accounts for the first step of the transaction as an advance receipt of consideration and continues to consolidate the subsidiary until the later date. In many cases, the assets and liabilities of the consolidated subsidiary could qualify as a disposal group held for sale under IFRS 5 (see Chapter 5 at section 2.1). Immediate disposal - If the parent loses control and access to benefits associated on the first step of the transaction, then it ceases to consolidate the former subsidiary immediately, recognises a gain or loss on disposal, and accounts for the consideration due in the second step as deferred consideration receivable. [IFRS 10.26, IFRS 10.B98(c), IFRS 10.B99]. Therefore:
(a) If a revaluation surplus previously recognised in OCI would be transferred directly to retained earnings
on the disposal of the asset, the parent transfers the revaluation surplus directly to retained earnings when it loses control of the subsidiary. [IFRS 10.26, IFRS 10.B99].
(b) Remeasurement gains or losses on a defined benefit plan recognised in OCI would not be reclassified
to profit or loss when the parent loses control of the subsidiary, but may be transferred within equity. [IAS 19.122].

How we see it There are two different interpretations of how to treat OCI accumulated in equity (other than exchange differences related to the net investment in foreign operations) that would be reclassified to profit or loss on the disposal of the related assets or liabilities, both of which are acceptable: • Approach 1 Reclassification of OCI related to parent interest only • Approach 2 Reclassification of OCI related to parent and the NCI These approaches for the treatment of OCI accumulated in equity (other than exchange differences related to the net investment in foreign operations) are described below. Approach 1 Reclassification of OCI related to parent interest only IFRS 10 requires derecognition of the NCI (including any components of OCI attributable to them) at the date when control is lost, which implies derecognition of the NCI without any need for reclassification. [IFRS 10.26, IFRS 10.B98(a)]. In addition, IFRS 10 requires recognition of a gain or loss in profit or loss to be attributable to the parent, [IFRS 10.26, IFRS 10.B98(d)], which again implies that there is no reclassification of OCI in respect of the NCI. Approach 1 is more consistent with the treatment of exchange differences relating to the net investment in foreign operations, as described at (c) above. Approach 2 Reclassification of OCI related to parent and the NCI IFRS 10 specifically requires that “if a gain or loss previously recognised in other comprehensive income would be reclassified to profit or loss on the disposal of the related assets or liabilities, the parent shall reclassify the gain or loss from equity to profit or loss (as a reclassification adjustment) when it loses control of the subsidiary.” [IFRS 10.26, IFRS 10.B98(c), IFRS 10.B99]. That would clearly require reclassification of the entire balance of OCI accumulated within equity. However, where this is done, the portion of the reclassification adjustment attributable to the NCI is included as part of the profit or loss attributable to the NCI, not as part of the profit or loss attributable to the parent.

4.2 Goodwill attributable to NCI It is not clear under IFRS 10 what happens to the NCI’s share of goodwill, when accounting for transactions with NCI. How we see it We believe that when there are changes in their relative ownership interests, the parent is required to reallocate a proportion of the goodwill between the controlling interest and NCI. However, the amount of goodwill, if any, to be allocated may depend on whether NCI was recognised at fair value or not on initial acquisition, and on whether there is an increase or decrease in ownership of the NCI without a loss on control. If an appropriate amount of goodwill is not reallocated, the loss recognised upon loss of control (see section 3.2 above), or goodwill impairment would not reflect the ownership interest in the subsidiary applicable to that NCI (see Chapter 21 at section 9). The issues arising include: • Calculation of the ‘gross up’ of the carrying amount of goodwill (for the purposes of the impairment test) because the NCI is measured at its proportionate share of net identifiable assets and hence its share of goodwill is not recognised • The allocation of impairment losses between the parent and NCI • Reallocation of goodwill between NCI and the controlling interest after a change in a parent’s ownership interest in a subsidiary that does not result in loss of control Under IFRS 3, the proportion of goodwill that is attributable to NCI is not necessarily equal to the ownership percentage. The most common situation where this occurs is when the parent recognised the NCI at its proportionate share of the acquiree’s identifiable net assets and therefore does not recognise any goodwill for the NCI (see Chapter 10 at sections 5.1 and 8.2). This situation might also occur because goodwill has been recognised for both the parent’s interest and the NCI, but the parent’s goodwill reflects a control premium that was paid upon acquisition (see Chapter 10 at section 8.1).

4.3 Non-cash acquisition of NCI One issue considered by the IFRS IC is the accounting for the purchase of NCI by the controlling shareholder when the consideration includes non-cash items, such as an item of property, plant and equipment. More specifically, the submitter asked the IFRS IC to clarify whether the difference between the fair value of the consideration given and the carrying amount of such consideration should be recognised in equity or in profit or loss. The submitter asserted that, according to the requirements of the then IAS 27 Consolidated and Separate Financial Statements (now reflected in IFRS 10), the difference described should be recognised in equity, whereas applying IFRIC 17 by analogy, the difference should be recognised in profit or loss (see section 3.7 above). The IFRS IC noted that the requirements of the then IAS 27 (now reflected in IFRS 10), deal solely with the difference between the carrying amount of the NCI and the fair value of the consideration given; this difference is required to be recognised in equity. These requirements do not deal with the difference between the fair value of the consideration given and the carrying amount of such consideration. [IFRS 10.24, IFRS 10.B96]. The difference between the fair value of the assets transferred and their carrying amount arises from the derecognition of those assets. Since IFRS accounting standards generally require an entity to recognise, in profit or loss, any gain or loss arising from the derecognition of an asset,28 this difference has to be recognised in profit or loss. 4.4 Transaction costs Although IFRS 10 is clear that changes in a parent’s ownership interest in a subsidiary that do not result in the parent losing control of the subsidiary are equity transactions (i.e., transactions with owners in their capacity as owners), [IFRS 10.23], it does not specifically address how to account for related transaction costs. Only incremental costs directly attributable to the equity transaction that otherwise would have been avoided qualify as transaction costs. [IAS 32.37]. How we see it In our view, any incremental directly attributable transaction costs incurred to acquire an outstanding NCI in a subsidiary, or to sell an NCI in a subsidiary without loss of control, are deducted from equity. [IFRS 10 Appendix A]. The principle underlying accounting for NCI is that all residual economic interest holders of any part of the consolidated entity have an equity interest in that consolidated entity. Consequently, NCI relates to consolidated subsidiaries, and not to those investments in subsidiaries accounted for at fair value through profit or loss in accordance with IFRS 9 by an investment entity (see
# Chapter 7 at section 10.3). [IFRS 10.31, IFRS 10.32, IFRS 10.33]. This principle applies regardless of the
decision-making ability of that interest holder and where in the group that interest is held. Therefore, any equity instruments issued by a subsidiary that are not owned by the parent (apart from those that are required to be classified by IAS 32 as financial liabilities in the consolidated financial statements, as discussed in section 5.6 below) are NCI, including, for example: • Ordinary shares • Equity components of convertible debt and other compound financial instruments • Preference shares that are classified as equity (including both those with, and without, an entitlement to a pro rata share of net assets on liquidation) • Warrants that are classified as equity • Options over own shares that are classified as equity • Awards under equity-settled share-based payment transactions Options and warrants classified as equity are NCI, regardless of whether they are vested and regardless of the exercise price (e.g., whether they are ‘in-the-money’ or not). Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests 5.2 Initial measurement of NCI 5.2.1 Initial measurement of NCI in a business combination IFRS 3 requires any NCI in an acquiree to be recognised, [IFRS 3.10], but there are differing measurement requirements depending on the type of equity instrument. There is a choice of two measurement methods for those components of NCI that are both present ownership interests and entitle their holders to a proportionate share of the entity’s net assets in the event of a liquidation (referred to here as ‘qualifying NCI’). They can be measured at either: • Option 1 Acquisition-date fair value (consistent with the measurement principle for other components of the business combination) Or • Option 2 The present ownership instruments’ proportionate share in the recognised amounts of the acquiree’s identifiable net assets The choice of method is to be made for each business combination on a transaction-by-transaction basis, rather than being a policy choice. This choice of measurement is discussed in Chapter 10 at section 8. However, this choice is not available for all other components of NCI, which are required to be measured at their acquisition-date fair values, unless another measurement basis is required by IFRS accounting standards. [IFRS 3.19]. Of the items listed in section 5.1 above, entities are only given a choice of either proportionate share in the recognised amounts of the acquiree’s identifiable net assets or acquisition-date fair value, for ordinary shares or preference shares classified as equity that are entitled to a pro rata share of net assets on liquidation. Another measurement basis (referred to as a ‘market-based measure’) is required by IFRS 3 for share-based payment transactions classified as equity in accordance with IFRS 2 - these are measured in accordance with the method in IFRS 2. [IFRS 3.30, IFRS 3.B62A, IFRS 3.B62B]. The accounting for replaced and not replaced share- based payment transactions in a business combination is discussed in Chapter 10 at sections 7.2, 8.4 and 11.2.2 and in Chapter 30 at sections 11.2 and 11.3. The other items listed in section 5.1 above, e.g., the equity component of convertible debt or other compound financial instruments, preference shares classified as equity without an entitlement to a pro rata share of net assets upon liquidation, warrants and options over own shares, must be measured at acquisition-date fair value. The initial recognition of NCI in a business combination is discussed in more detail in Chapter 10 at section 8. That chapter also addresses many of the topics discussed in this chapter as well as an indirect acquisition of NCI where the acquiree in a business combination is the owner of an NCI in an acquirer’s existing subsidiary (see Chapter 10 at section 11.5). The reciprocal interests can also give rise to a measure of double counting of profits and net assets between the investor and its associate. How we see it Where an associate holds an interest in a subsidiary, we believe that there are two possible approaches to determine the amount of NCI in the subsidiary: • The NCI are determined after considering the associate’s ownership of the subsidiary (‘look through approach’) Or • The NCI are determined based on the holdings of the group in the subsidiary (‘black box approach’) An entity must apply the chosen approach consistently. The ‘black box approach’ will often lead to reporting higher consolidated net assets and results than when using the ‘look through approach’ as this adjustment is not made, although the amounts attributed to the owners of the parent are expected to be the same under both approaches. [IAS 32.AG29]. See Chapter 43 at section 4.8.1. [IAS 32.AG29A]. For example, put or call options over NCI accounted for as equity should be accounted for in consolidated financial statements as contracts over own equity instruments under IAS 32 (see Chapter 43 at section 11). For example, the acquirer might have a call option, i.e., a right to acquire the outstanding shares at a future date for a particular price. Alternatively, the acquirer might have granted a put option to the other shareholders whereby they have the right to sell their shares to the acquirer at a future date for a particular price. In some cases, there may be a combination of such call and put options, the terms of which may be equivalent or may be different. How we see it IFRS 3 gives no guidance as to how to account for a combination of call and put options over NCI in a business combination. Therefore, when determining the appropriate accounting in the consolidated financial statements in such situations, IFRS 10, IAS 32, and IFRS 9 need to be considered. Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests Factors to consider in determining whether the NCI put might provide a present ownership interest to the acquirer include: • Pricing - to the extent that the price is fixed or determinable, rather than being at fair value (or an amount that approximates fair value) of the underlying share - so that substantially all the variations in fair value of the underlying share accrete to the acquirer. However, in situations where there is only an NCI put, the level of the exercise price needs to be sufficiently high compared to the fair value of the underlying shares to exceed likely fair value variations in order to provide a present ownership interest. If the exercise price is low compared to fair value of the underlying shares, it is unlikely that the NCI will exercise the put. Even if the exercise price exceeds fair value of the underlying shares at the outset, reasonably possible upward fair value movements might mean that the NCI put does not provide a present ownership interest to the acquirer. • Voting rights and decision-making - to the extent that the voting rights or decision-making connected to the shares concerned are restricted. • Dividend rights - to the extent that the dividend rights attached to the shares concerned are restricted. It is likely that the NCI retains a present ownership interest where it has been agreed between the parties that, prior to the exercise of the option, all retained profits are to be freely distributed to the existing shareholders according to their current shareholdings (and there is no adjustment to the option exercise price for dividends paid). However, in many cases, dividend rights do not affect the assessment as the parent (through its control) can generally prevent distributions to the NCI prior to exercise or the exercise price of the NCI put is adjusted downwards for payment of any dividends to the NCI. • Issue of call options - a combination of NCI puts and calls, with the same period of exercise and same or similar pricing indicates that the arrangement is in the nature of a forward contract. This feature means that the instrument is highly likely to be exercised by one of the counterparties in situations where the exercise price is not at fair value (or an amount that approximates fair value) of the underlying shares. See section 6.3 below. The parent will generally have obtained a present ownership interest if there is an NCI put and call with matching terms and a fixed exercise price (or a fixed price forward contract to purchase the entity’s shares held by the NCI). Where the pricing of the NCI put and call is at fair value (or an amount that approximates fair value), the present ownership will generally remain with the NCI. Other situations, including where the exercise price is based on a formula, are likely to require further analysis, based on the factors outlined above. If it is concluded that the acquirer has a present ownership interest in the shares concerned, it is accounted for as an acquisition of those underlying shares, and no NCI is recognised. The accounting is described in section 6.2.3.A below. 6.2.3 The NCI put does not provide a present ownership interest How we see it When the terms of the transaction do not provide the parent with a present ownership interest of the shares subject to an NCI put, there are four approaches that can be taken. Key policy decisions that management must make in order to conclude an accounting approach, are: • Due to the potential contradictions between IAS 32 and IFRS 10, which standard takes precedence? • If an NCI is recognised on initial acquisition, whether or not it continues to be recognised? If the entity chooses to base its accounting policy on IAS 32, i.e., IAS 32 takes precedence, then it will only recognise a financial liability for the NCI put and not recognise an NCI. The approach is described in section 6.2.3.A below. If the accounting policy choice is that IFRS 10 takes precedence, the entity will initially recognise both the NCI and the financial liability under the NCI put. It initially measures any ‘qualifying NCI’ (i.e., those components of NCI that are both present ownership interests and entitle their holders to a proportionate share of the entity’s net assets in the event of a liquidation), either at fair value or at the proportionate share of net assets, with this choice available for each transaction (as discussed in section 5.2.1 above). [IFRS 3.19]. There is then a further accounting policy choice as to whether the NCI that was initially recognised continues to be recognised (as described in sections 6.2.3.B to 6.2.3.D below).

Approach 1 could be used when the entity does not have a present ownership interest but concludes that IAS 32 takes precedence over IFRS 10. By recognising a liability for the put option over the shares held by the NCI, no NCI is recognised. The business combination is accounted for on the basis that the underlying shares subject to the NCI put have been acquired. Thus, if the acquirer has granted a put option over all of the remaining shares, the business combination is accounted for as if the acquirer has obtained a 100% interest in the acquiree. No NCI is recognised when the acquirer completes the purchase price allocation and determines the amount of goodwill to recognise. The consideration transferred for the business combination includes the present value of the amount payable upon exercise of the NCI put to the non-controlling shareholders. Approach 1 is based on the requirements and guidance within IAS 32. IAS 32 requires the NCI put to be recognised as a liability, as discussed in section 6.2.1 above. [IAS 32.23]. IAS 32 explains that when a subsidiary issues a financial instrument and a parent or other entity in the group agrees additional terms directly with the holders of the instrument (e.g., a guarantee), it could be the case that the group does not have discretion over distributions or redemption. Even though the subsidiary appropriately classifies the instrument without regard to these additional terms in its financial statements, their effect is taken into account in the consolidated financial statements. To the extent that there is such an obligation or settlement provision, the instrument (or the component of it that is subject to the obligation) is classified as a financial liability in the consolidated financial statements. [IAS 32.AG29]. The implication is that the underlying financial instruments (i.e., the shares in the subsidiary) are represented by the financial liability. Accordingly, since the shares held by those non-controlling shareholders are not treated as equity interests in the consolidated financial statements, there is no NCI to be accounted for under IFRS 10. This means that the profit or loss (and changes in OCI) with respect to the subsidiary are allocated to the owners of the parent and not to the NCI, as there is none. Under this approach, any dividends paid to the other shareholders are recognised as an expense in the consolidated financial statements, except where they represent a repayment of the liability (e.g., where the exercise price is adjusted by the dividends paid). The NCI put is accounted for as a financial liability under IFRS 9. [IAS 32.23, IFRS 9.4.2.1, IFRS 9.4.2.2]. Changes in the carrying amount of the financial liability are recognised in profit or loss. If the NCI put is exercised, the financial liability is extinguished by the payment of the exercise price. If the NCI put is not exercised, then the entity has effectively disposed of a partial interest in its subsidiary, without loss of control, in return for the amount recognised as the financial liability at the date of expiry. The entity accounts for the transaction as discussed in section 4 above. The consideration received is the amount of the financial liability extinguished and any difference between this and the carrying amount of the NCI (as of the date that the NCI put expires) is recognised within equity attributable to the owners of the parent (i.e., parent equity). 6.2.3.B Full recognition of NCI (Approach 2) Approach 2 is one of the alternatives that could be used when the entity does not have a present ownership interest in the shares concerned and concludes that IFRS 10 takes precedence. The acquirer initially recognises the NCI, either at fair value or at the proportionate share of the acquiree’s net assets. [IFRS 3.19]. Approach 2 takes the view that the NCI continues to be recognised within equity until the NCI put is exercised. The carrying amount of the NCI changes due to allocations of profit or loss, allocations of changes in OCI and dividends made for the reporting period (see section 5.7 above). The financial liability for the NCI put is recognised at the present value of the amount payable upon exercise of the NCI put and is subsequently accounted for under IFRS 9 like any other written put option on equity instruments. On initial recognition, the corresponding debit is made to another component of parent equity, not to the NCI. All subsequent changes in the carrying amount of the financial liability that result from the remeasurement of the present value of the amount payable upon exercise of the NCI put are recognised in the profit or loss attributable to the owners of the parent, and not the NCI’s share of the profit or loss of the subsidiary. If the NCI put is exercised, the entity accounts for an increase in its ownership interest as an equity transaction (see section 4 above). Consequently, the financial liability, as remeasured immediately before the transaction, is extinguished by payment of the exercise price and the NCI purchased is derecognised against parent equity. If the NCI put is exercised, the carrying amount of the financial liability at that date is extinguished by the payment of the exercise price. If the NCI put expires unexercised, the liability is derecognised with the NCI being reinstated as if nothing happened. However, neither IFRS 10 nor IFRS 3 specifically addresses the accounting for a sequence of transactions that begins with an acquirer gaining control over another entity, followed by acquiring additional ownership interests shortly thereafter. This frequently happens where public offers are made to a group of shareholders and there is a regulatory requirement for an acquirer to make an offer to the non-controlling shareholders of the acquiree. The IFRS IC considered this issue and tentatively agreed that the initial acquisition of the controlling stake and the subsequent mandatory tender offer should be treated as a single transaction. However, there was no consensus among the IFRS IC members on whether a liability should be recognised for the mandatory tender offer at the date that the acquirer obtains control of the acquiree. A small majority expressed the view that a liability should be recognised in a manner that is consistent with IAS 32. Other IFRS IC members expressed the view that a mandatory tender offer to purchase NCI is not within the scope of IAS 32 or IAS 37 Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets and that a liability should therefore not be recognised.37 The issue was escalated to the IASB38 and the accounting for mandatory tender offers has subsequently been considered as part of the FICE project. See sections 7.3 and 7.4 below. How we see it In our view, when determining the appropriate accounting, a simple distinction between a statutory obligation and a contractual obligation is not always clear and the effects of a mandatory tender offer may, in any event, be economically similar to a contractual NCI put. Statutory requirements (such as a mandatory tender offer or a statutory process that has the same effect) may have the effect of modifying the contractual terms of the equity instruments, thereby giving rise to a contractual obligation to purchase the equity instruments. The rules and regulations regarding tender offers differ by jurisdiction and careful analysis of the facts and circumstances is needed. In any event, even if it is established there is no contractual obligation, the effects of a mandatory tender offer are economically similar to a contractual NCI put and, in the absence of specific requirements in IFRS accounting standards, it might be appropriate to account in the same way. We believe that this is an area where the IASB needs to provide further clarification. How we see it In the absence of any specific requirements in IFRS accounting standards for transactions such as mandatory tender offers arising in connection with a business combination, we believe that entities generally have an accounting policy choice to:
(a) Account for the transaction as a single linked transaction in which control is gained (a single
business combination), where the indicators of a linked transaction are met Or
(b) Account for the transaction as a discrete transaction (a business combination followed by an
acquisition of NCI) However, policy (a) can only be applied where the acquisition of NCI is assessed as linked to the same transaction as that by which control is gained (as explained in section 6.2.4.A below). Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests 6.2.4.A Identifying a linked transaction The acquisition of the NCI is a linked transaction when it arises from the same transaction as that by which control was gained. This will generally be the case where it arises as part of the same offer, including where legal or regulatory requirements lead to the offer being extended through the creation of a shareholder put, or acquirer compulsory acquisition rights. How we see it In many cases where NCI is acquired in connection with a transaction in which control is gained, it will be clear where there is a single offer. Where it is not clear, the existence of all of the following factors indicate a linked transaction: • The option over the remaining interest and subsequent acquisition is not negotiated separately by the non-controlling shareholders • The offer period is short • The price per share offered for subsequent increases in shareholding is fixed and consistent with the price paid for the controlling interest These factors are generally all present in the case of public offers to the entire group of shareholders. This is not necessarily the case for private offers where, for example, some of the options may be for extended terms. If a put option is granted over the NCI and the terms of the put option are such that the present ownership interest attached to the underlying shares is gained at the same time as gaining control, this will satisfy the factor that ‘the offer period is short’. Even if the put nominally extends over a long period, the effect is that ownership has already passed to the acquirer. See section 6.2.2 above for the factors to be considered in assessing whether or not the acquirer gains present ownership interest over the underlying shares. Where the other factors listed in the ‘How we see it’ box above are also met, this is a linked transaction. 6.2.4.B Accounting for the linked transaction (where a policy to account as a single business combination is followed) A linked transaction is accounted for as if all ownership interests were acquired at the acquisition date as part of the transaction to gain control. The consideration transferred is the sum of the amount paid for the controlling interest and NCI (that is acquired as part of the linked transaction) and the percentage acquired is the sum of the respective shareholdings. If, at the date of gaining control the NCI has not actually been acquired, a financial liability is recognised at the present value of the amount payable upon exercise of the option to acquire the NCI. If, at the date the NCI is actually acquired, the percentage acquired differs to that originally accounted for as being acquired, the purchase accounting is adjusted to reflect the actual percentage acquired. A ‘true up’ exercise is performed to adjust the total consideration paid and therefore the amount of goodwill recognised. It is not accounted for as a partial disposal of NCI (changes in ownership interest without loss of control are addressed in section 4 above). The NCI is measured as of the date of acquisition, not as of the date that the offer expires. When the transaction is linked because the arrangement provides a present ownership interest in the NCI, the entity will not recognise the NCI. Accounting for the transaction is as described in Approach 1 in section 6.2.3.A above.

Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests 6.3 Combination of call and put options In some business combinations, there might be a combination of call and put options, the terms of which may be equivalent or may be different. How we see it The appropriate accounting for a combination of call and put options over NCI is determined based on the discussions in sections 6.1 and 6.2 above. However, where there is a call and put option with matching terms, particularly at a fixed price, the combination of the options is more likely to mean that they give the acquirer a present ownership interest. Where the exercise price is at fair value (or an amount that approximates fair value), however, the returns of ownership arising from fair value movements will accrete to the NCI and consequently, this situation is unlikely to give the acquirer a present ownership interest. In cases where the combination of call and put options gives the acquirer a present ownership interest, where the options are over all of the shares not held by the parent, the acquirer has effectively acquired a 100% interest in the subsidiary at the date of the business combination. The entity is likely to be economically in a similar position as if it had acquired a 100% interest in the subsidiary with either deferred consideration (where the exercise price is fixed) or contingent consideration (where the settlement amount is not fixed, but is dependent upon a future event). See Chapter 10 at sections 7 and 7.1. As noted in section 6 above, similar considerations would apply where the acquirer entered into a forward purchase contract for the shares held by the other shareholders. 6.4 Call and put options entered into in relation to existing NCI - additional accounting considerations The discussion in sections 6.1 to 6.3 above focused on call and put options entered into at the same time as control is gained of the subsidiary. However, an entity may enter into the options with non-controlling shareholders after gaining control. How we see it The appropriate accounting policy for call and put options entered into in relation to existing NCI will be based on the discussions in sections 6.1 to 6.3 above. Where the entity already has a controlling interest and as a result of the options now has a present ownership interest in the remaining shares concerned, or concludes that IAS 32 takes precedence, the NCI is no longer recognised within equity. The transaction is accounted for as an acquisition of the NCI, i.e., it is accounted for as an equity transaction, (see section 4 above), because such acquisitions are not business combinations under IFRS 3. 6.5 Call and put options in separate financial statements Purchased call options (and written put options) over shares in an acquired subsidiary are derivatives in the separate financial statements. [IFRS 9.2.1(a)]. Note that this treatment differs from that in the consolidated financial statements which is addressed generally in section 6 above. A purchased call option over shares in an acquired subsidiary is initially recognised as a financial asset at its fair value, with any subsequent changes in the fair value of the option recognised in profit or loss. Similarly, a written put option over shares in an acquired subsidiary is initially recognised as a financial liability at its fair value, with any subsequent changes in the fair value of the option recognised in profit or loss. The initial credit entry for the call option (and the initial debit entry for the put option) will depend on the transactions giving rise to the options. For example, the entity may have paid (or received) consideration for the call (or put option) in a separate transaction or as part of a larger transaction. Where a purchased call option (or written put) option lapses, the financial asset (or financial liability) is derecognised, with a debit (or credit) to profit or loss. Where a purchased call option (or written put option) is exercised, the financial asset (or financial liability) is derecognised with an adjustment to the cost of investment of purchasing the shares subject to the option. The fair value of the purchased call option (or written put option) is not expected to be significant if the option is exercisable at the fair value (or at an amount that approximates fair value) of the underlying shares at the date of exercise. Where there is both a purchased call and a written put option, particularly where the options have the same fixed redemption price, it is likely that the fair values of the purchased call option and written put option will differ.

Consolidation procedures and non-controlling interests Appendix B - Defined terms Extract from IFRS 10 Appendix A Defined terms consolidated financial statements The financial statements of a group in which the assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses and cash flows of the parent and its subsidiaries are presented as those of a single economic entity. group A parent and its subsidiaries. non-controlling interest Equity in a subsidiary not attributable, directly or indirectly, to a parent. parent An entity that controls one or more entities. subsidiary An entity that is controlled by another entity. In addition, the following terms are used throughout this chapter: • ‘NCI’ to refer to non-controlling interest(s) • ‘NCI put’ to refer to a written put option over non-controlling interest(s) • ‘NCI puts and calls’ to refer to written put options and purchased call options over non-controlling interest(s) • ‘Present ownership interest’ to include existing legal ownership interests together with potential voting rights and other derivatives that, in substance, already provide existing ownership interests in a subsidiary

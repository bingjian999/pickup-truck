Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement Overview This chapter deals with the recognition and initial measurement of financial instruments as per IFRS 9 Financial Instruments. Chapter 41 provides a general background to financial instruments, and discusses which financial instruments are within the scope of which standards. Chapters 43 and 44 detail the classification requirements. This chapter then deals with the question of when financial instruments should be recognised in the financial statements, and the amount that they are measured at initially. IFRS 9 requires that an entity must recognise a financial asset or a financial liability in its statement of financial position when, and only when, the entity becomes a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument. There are however certain exceptions to this general principle, and these are dealt with in this chapter. Initial measurement is normally based on the fair value of an instrument and most, but not all, of the detailed requirements of IFRS accounting standards governing fair values are dealt with in IFRS 13 Fair Value Measurement, which is covered in Chapter 15. However, IFRS 9 also contains certain requirements addressing fair value measurements of financial instruments, and these are covered in this chapter. The treatment of financial instruments after initial recognition is dealt with in subsequent chapters. In particular, Chapter 46 deals with the subsequent measurement of financial instruments, Chapter 47 deals with the impairment of financial assets, and Chapter 48 deals with the derecognition of financial instruments. What you need to know • The general principle for recognition is that an entity recognises a financial instrument when, and only when, it becomes a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument. • As an exception, assets subject to regular way transactions are permitted to be recognised, or derecognised, either at the trade date or at the settlement date. • The general principles for initial measurement are: • Financial instruments classified at fair value through profit or loss are normally measured at fair value. • Other financial instruments not classified at fair value through profit or loss are normally measured at fair value, but are adjusted for incremental and directly attributable transaction costs. • There is an exception to the general initial measurement principles for certain trade receivables within the scope of IFRS 15 Revenue from Contracts with Customers. • There are situations where the initial measurement of financial instruments require additional consideration, such as when there is a difference between the fair value at initial recognition, and the transaction price of the financial instrument. Some examples of where this difference may arise include where an entity has an interest free or low interest rate loan, or acquires financial assets and liabilities as part of a group that does not constitute a business, or simply that an entity has priced an instrument in a way that appears to result in it making an immediate (Day 1) profit. When such a difference arises, IFRS 9 has additional requirements and limitations about the recognition of such a difference.

Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement Initial recognition 2.1 General requirements IFRS 9 provides that an entity must recognise a financial asset or a financial liability in its statement of financial position when, and only when, the entity becomes a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument. [IFRS 9.3.1.1, IFRS 9(2026).B3.1.2A]. Before that, the entity does not have contractual rights or contractual obligations. Hence, there is no financial asset or financial liability, as defined in IAS 32 Financial Instruments: Presentation, to recognise. IFRS 9 provides a practical exception to the application of this general principle for ‘regular way’ purchases and sales (see section 2.2 below). Listed below are examples of the application of this principle. 2.1.1 Receivables and payables Unconditional receivables and payables are recognised as assets or liabilities when the entity becomes a party to the contract and, as a consequence, has a legal right to receive, or a legal obligation to pay cash. [IFRS 9.B3.1.2(a)]. 2.1.2 Firm commitments to purchase or sell goods or services Under IFRS, assets to be acquired and liabilities to be incurred as a result of a firm commitment to purchase or sell goods or services are generally not recognised until at least one of the parties has performed under the agreement. For example, an entity that receives a firm order for goods or services does not generally recognise an asset for the consideration receivable (and the entity that places the order does not generally recognise a liability for the consideration to be paid) at the time of the commitment, but instead delays recognition until the ordered goods or services have been shipped, delivered or rendered. [IFRS 9.B3.1.2(b)]. This accounting applies on the assumption that the firm commitment to buy or sell non-financial items is not: • Net settled and treated as if it were a derivative (see Chapter 41 at section 3.3.1) • Irrevocably designated at fair value through profit or loss on initial recognition. This is only allowed if an accounting mismatch is eliminated or significantly reduced (see Chapter 41 at section 3.3.6) Or • Designated as a hedged item in a fair value hedge (see Chapter 49 at section 5.1) If any of the above situations apply, the firm commitment would be recognised as an asset or liability before delivery of the underlying goods or services. [IFRS 9.B3.1.2(b), IFRS 9.2.4]. 2.1.3 Forward contracts A forward contract obliges one party to the contract to buy, and the other party to sell, an underlying asset that is the subject of the contract, for a fixed price at a future date (for further discussion on the treatment of a forward contract, see Chapter 41 at section 3). In most cases, a forward contract to purchase or sell a financial asset is within the scope of IFRS 9. However, in certain cases it will not be in the scope, for example, a forward contract between an acquirer and a selling shareholder to buy or sell an acquiree that will result in a business combination at a future acquisition date (see Chapter 41 at section 2.7.2). Certain forward contracts to buy or sell non-financial items that can be settled net, including where the non-financial item is readily convertible into cash, will be within the scope of IFRS 9 (see Chapter 41 at section 1.2.5). If a forward contract is within the scope of IFRS 9, it is recognised as an asset or a liability at commitment date, rather than on the date on which settlement takes place. When an entity becomes a party to a forward contract, the fair values of the right and obligation are often equal, so that the net fair value of the forward at inception is zero. If the net fair value of the right and obligation is not zero, the contract is recognised as an asset or liability. [IFRS 9.B3.1.2(c)]. 2.1.4 Option contracts An option contract is a contract which gives one party to the contract the right, but not the obligation, to buy from, or sell to, the other party to the contract the underlying asset, for a fixed price, at a future date (or during a period of time). An option giving the right to buy an asset is referred to as a ‘call’ option, and one giving the right to sell an asset as a ‘put’ option. An option is referred to as a ‘bought’ or ‘purchased’
Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement option from the perspective of the party with the right to buy or sell (the ‘holder’) and as a ‘written’ option from the perspective of the party with the potential obligation to buy or sell. Option contracts that are within the scope of IFRS 9 are recognised as assets or liabilities when the holder or writer becomes a party to the contract (see Chapter 41 at section 1.2.8). [IFRS 9.B3.1.2(d)]. 2.1.5 Planned future transactions (forecast transactions) Planned future transactions, no matter how likely, are not assets and liabilities because the entity has not become a party to a contract. They are therefore, not recognised under IFRS 9. [IFRS 9.B3.1.2(e)]. However, transactions that have been entered into as a cash flow hedge of certain ‘highly probable’ future transactions are recognised under IFRS 9 (see Chapter 49 at section 5.2). 2.1.6 Transfers of financial assets not qualifying for derecognition by transferor - impact on recognition IFRS 9 states that, where a financial asset is transferred from one party to another in circumstances that preclude the transferor from derecognising the asset, the transferee must not recognise the transferred asset. Instead, the transferee derecognises the cash or other consideration paid, and recognises a receivable from the transferor. [IFRS 9.B3.1.1]. If the transferor has both a right, and an obligation, to reacquire control of the entire transferred asset for a fixed amount (such as under a repurchase agreement
- see Chapter 48 at section 4.1), the transferee will account for its receivable at amortised cost, if the
criteria for classification as measured at amortised cost are met [IFRS 9.B3.2.15]. Underlying this principle appears to be a concern that more than one party cannot satisfy the criteria in IFRS 9 for recognition of the same financial asset at the same time. However, this principle may not hold in all circumstances, since it is sometimes possible for the same asset to be simultaneously recognised by more than one entity, for example if a seller adopts settlement date accounting, and a purchaser trade date accounting (see section 2.2 below). In addition, IFRS 9 clarifies that to the extent that a transfer does not qualify for derecognition, the transferor does not account for its contractual rights or obligations related to the transfer separately as derivatives, if recognising both the derivative and either the transferred asset, or the liability arising from the transfer, would result in recognising the same rights or obligations twice. For example, a call option retained by the transferor may prevent a transfer of financial assets from being accounted for as a sale. In that case, the call option is not separately recognised as a derivative asset. [IFRS 9.B3.2.14]. Sometimes a transfer of financial assets (the ‘underlying assets’) can involve the transferee issuing to the transferor a non-derivative financial instrument, such as subordinated debt, the effect of which is that substantially all the risks and rewards of the assets are not transferred and therefore, the transferor does not derecognise the underlying assets. This is particularly common when the transferee is a securitisation vehicle. The subordinated debt could be issued as part of the consideration for the transferred assets, alongside the cash consideration. Alternatively, part of the cash consideration received in exchange for the underlying assets could be reinvested in the subordinated debt. How we see it In the circumstances described above, we would normally expect the transferor to apply by analogy the guidance for derivatives outlined above in IFRS 9 B3.2.14. As a result, the transferor would not recognise the non-derivative financial instrument as a separate asset. Instead, the transferor would recognise a financial liability equal to the net cash received. This will prevent the transferor from recognising as a financial asset the subordinated debt, while also still recognising the underlying asset that failed derecognition. However, IFRS 9 is less clear about the transferee’s position and an entity will need to determine whether it should recognise the subordinated loan as a separate financial liability, or to regard the subsequent cash flows on this loan as part of the cash flows arising on the receivable. The guidance in Chapter 42 at section 2.2 addresses whether two financial instruments should be accounted for as one (which in those cases would lead to the recognition of a derivative), and these principles can help the transferee determine whether it recognises both a financial liability and a receivable, or a derivative. However, this can be very judgemental, and we see a degree of diversity in the way securitisation vehicles account for these arrangements.
Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement 2.1.7 Collateral The implementation guidance accompanying IFRS 9 addresses the recognition of cash collateral. When an entity receives cash collateral that is not legally segregated (e.g., not treated as ‘client money’), it must recognise the cash, and a related payable to the entity providing such collateral. The reason for this is that the ultimate realisation of a financial asset is its conversion into cash, and hence, no further transformation is required before the economic benefits of the cash received can be realised. [IFRS 9.IG D.1.1]. On the other hand, the entity providing the cash collateral derecognises the cash and recognises a receivable from the receiving entity. [IFRS 9.IG D.1.1]. The accounting treatment of ‘client money’ is discussed in detail in Chapter 48 at section 8. The requirements related to non-cash collateral are addressed in Chapter 48 at section 5.5.2. 2.1.8 Principal versus agent When an entity acts as an intermediary in transactions involving financial instruments, the question of whether it is acting as an agent, or a principal may arise. This is a particularly relevant matter for brokers and similar institutions. Diversity in practice in this regard had been observed, so the question was put to the IFRS IC in June 2017 about how brokers should account for back-to-back derivatives with their client and the central clearing counterparty. Frequently asked questions Question 2-1: In relation to clearing brokers and similar institutions, when dealing with centrally cleared client derivatives, is the clearing broker acting as an agent or a principal? In June 2017, the IFRS IC concluded that the clearing broker first applies the requirements for financial instruments, and observed that: • If the transaction(s) results in contracts that are within the scope of IFRS 9, then the clearing member applies to those contracts the recognition requirements of IFRS 9. In the statement of financial position, the clearing member presents the assets and liabilities arising from its contracts with the clearing house separately from those arising from its contracts with its clients, unless net presentation is required pursuant to the offsetting requirements in IAS 32. This implies that the clearing member is considered to act as principal in both the derivative contracts with the clearing house, and its clients. • If the transaction is not within the scope of IFRS 9, and another standard does not specifically apply, only then would the clearing member apply the hierarchy in paragraphs 10-12 of IAS 8 Accounting Policies, Changes in Accounting Estimates and Errors to determine an appropriate accounting policy for the transaction(s) (see Chapter 3 at section 4.3), e.g., using the principal versus agent guidance in IFRS 15 (see Chapter 28 at section 4.4).1 2.2 ‘Regular way’ transactions 2.2.1 Financial assets: general requirements As discussed in section 2.1 above, the general requirement under IFRS 9 is to recognise a financial asset or a financial liability in the statement of financial position when, and only when, an entity becomes a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument (i.e., the trade date). [IFRS 9.3.1.1]. However, the application of this general requirement to certain transactions, known as ‘regular way’ purchases and sales, presents some challenges. Below, we discuss those challenges and the accounting policy choice that IFRS 9 has provided as a solution. A regular way purchase or sale is defined as: [IFRS 9 Appendix A]

A contract that does not require delivery of the asset, and can be net settled (i.e., payment or receipt of cash or other financial assets equivalent to the change in value of the contract) is not a regular way transaction. Rather, it is accounted for between the trade date and settlement date as a derivative, in accordance with the requirements of IFRS 9 (see Chapter 46 at section 2.4). [IFRS 9.B3.1.4]. Many financial markets provide a mechanism whereby all transactions in certain financial instruments (particularly quoted equities and bonds) entered into on a particular date are settled by delivery within a fixed number of days after that date. The date on which the entity commits itself to purchase or sell an asset is called the ‘trade date’, and the date on which it is settled by delivery of the assets that are the subject of the agreement, is called the ‘settlement date’. [IFRS 9.B3.1.5, IFRS 9.B3.1.6]. In practice, because entitlement to interest often accrues from the due settlement date, the settlement date is often determined with reference to the date that delivery is actually due. Consequently, if settlement is temporarily delayed, an entity using settlement date accounting will account for the purchase or sale of the security on the settlement due date, and recognise a payable or receivable until settlement actually occurs. This results in a disconnect between what occurs in practice, and the stated requirements of IFRS 9 whereby settlement date is the date on which settlement occurs. Another practical implication of settlement date accounting is that, while legal title to the assets that are the subject of the transaction passes only on or after settlement date, the buyer is effectively exposed to the risks and rewards of ownership of the assets from the trade date. Absent of any special provisions, the accounting treatment for regular way transactions under IFRS 9 would be that, between trade date and settlement date, an entity has a forward contract to purchase an asset (see section 2.1.3 above). Consequently, as for all derivatives, this forward contract would be recorded at fair value, with all changes in fair value recognised in profit or loss (see Chapter 46 at section 2.4), unless the special rules for hedge accounting apply (see Chapter 49). This would not only be somewhat onerous, but would also have the effect that changes in a financial asset’s fair value between trade date and settlement date would be recognised in profit or loss, even though the asset itself may not be measured at fair value through profit or loss after initial recognition. To avoid this, IFRS 9 permits assets subject to regular way transactions to be recognised, or derecognised, either as at the trade date (‘trade date accounting’) or as at the settlement date (‘settlement date accounting’). [IFRS 9.3.1.2, IFRS 9.B3.1.3]. This accounting policy choice can be made separately for each of the main classification categories of financial assets identified by IFRS 9, i.e., debt instruments measured at amortised cost, debt instruments at fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI), financial assets mandatorily measured at fair value through profit or loss, debt instruments designated as measured at fair value through profit or loss and equity investments designated as measured at FVOCI (see
# Chapter 44 for further discussion on the classification of financial assets). Once chosen, the accounting
policy needs to be applied consistently and symmetrically (i.e., to acquisitions and disposals) to each category. [IFRS 9.B3.1.3]. Discussed below is additional guidance for determining whether a transaction meets the definition of ‘regular way’, as well as requirements relating to trade date and settlement date accounting. 2.2.1.A No established market The definition of ‘regular way’ transactions refers to terms that require delivery of the asset within the time frame established generally by regulation or convention in the marketplace concerned. Marketplace in this context is not limited to a formal stock exchange or organised over-the-counter market. Rather, it means the environment in which the financial asset is customarily exchanged. For example, a market for private issue financial instruments can be a marketplace. An acceptable time frame would be the period reasonably and customarily required for the parties to complete the transaction and prepare and execute closing documents. [IFRS 9.IG B.28].
Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement 2.2.1.B Contracts not settled according to marketplace convention: derivatives The contract must be accounted for as a derivative when it is not settled in the way established by regulation or convention in the marketplace concerned. Illustration 2-1: Regular way transactions - forward contract Entity A enters into a forward contract to purchase 1 million of B’s ordinary shares in two months for CU10 per share. The contract requires A to take physical delivery of the shares and pay the counterparty CU10 million in cash. B’s shares trade in an active public market at an average of 100,000 shares a day. Regular way delivery is three days. As the transaction is not settled in the way established by regulation or convention in the marketplace concerned, it cannot be regarded as a regular way contract. Therefore, the transaction must be accounted for as a derivative. [IFRS 9.IG B.29]. 2.2.1.C Multiple active markets: settlement provisions If an entity’s financial instruments trade in more than one active market, and the settlement provisions differ in the various active markets, the provisions that apply are those in the market in which the purchase actually takes place. For instance, an entity purchasing shares of a public company listed on a US stock exchange through a broker, where the settlement date of the contract is six business days later, could not apply the regular way trade exemption since trades for equity shares on US exchanges customarily settle in three business days. However, if the entity did the same transaction on an exchange outside the US that has a customary settlement period of six business days, the contract would meet the exemption for a regular way trade.
**[IFRS 9.IG B.30].**
2.2.1.D Exercise of an option An option is typically accounted for as a derivative. However, following its exercise, the settlement of the option by delivery may be governed by regulation or convention in the options market, and could therefore, represent a regular way transaction. This is illustrated as outlined below. Illustration 2-2: Regular way transactions - share purchase by call option Entity A purchases a call option in a public market permitting it to purchase 100 shares of Entity X at any time over the next three months at a price of CU100 per share. If Entity A exercises its option, it has 14 days to settle the transaction according to regulation or convention in the options market. X shares are traded in an active public market that requires three-day settlement. In this case, while the option will be accounted for as a derivative prior to being exercised, the purchase of shares by exercising the option is a regular way purchase of shares because settlement by delivery of the shares within 14 days is a regular way transaction. [IFRS 9.IG B.31]. This is the case even though if the shares had been acquired directly in the market, the market convention for settlement would have been three days.
#### Financial liabilities
The requirements above relating to regular way transactions apply only to transactions in financial assets. IFRS 9 does not contain any specific requirements about trade date and settlement date accounting for transactions in financial liabilities. Therefore, the general recognition and derecognition requirements for financial liabilities in IFRS 9 apply (see above in section 2.1). [IFRS 9.IG B.32]. Consequently, financial liabilities are recognised on the date the entity becomes a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument (see section 2.1 above). Such contracts are not recognised unless one of the parties has performed, or the contract is a derivative contract not exempted from the scope of IFRS 9 (see Chapter 41 at section 3.3). In January 2007, the IFRS IC addressed the accounting for short sales of securities, when the transaction terms require delivery of the securities within the time frame established generally by regulation or convention in the marketplace concerned. Constituents explained that in practice, many entities apply trade date accounting to such transactions. Specifically, industry practice recognised the short sales as financial liabilities at fair value with changes in fair value recognised in profit or loss. Profit or loss would be the same as if the short sales were accounted for as derivatives, but the securities would be presented differently in the statement of financial position. The IFRS IC acknowledged that requiring entities to account for short positions as derivatives may create considerable practical problems for their accounting systems and controls with little, if any, improvement to the quality of financial information presented. Trade date accounting requires: [IFRS 9.B3.1.5]
(a) In respect of an asset to be bought: recognition on the trade date of the asset, and the liability to pay for it.
This means that during the period between trade date and settlement date, the entity accounts for the asset as if it already owned it.
(b) In respect of an asset to be sold: derecognition on the trade date of the asset, together with recognition of
any gain or loss on disposal, and the recognition of a receivable from the buyer for payment. (a) In respect of an asset to be bought: Recognition of the asset on the settlement date (i.e., the date it is
received by the entity). Any change in the fair value of the asset to be received during the period between the trade date and the settlement date is accounted for in the same way as the acquired asset. In other words: • For assets carried at amortised cost, the change in fair value is not recognised (other than impairment losses which are considered from the trade date). This means that such financial assets are not measured at their fair value on the date of initial recognition (i.e., settlement date), but at the amount that represented the asset’s fair value on the trade date. And • For financial assets measured at FVOCI, the change in fair value is recognised in other comprehensive income. Where such changes in fair value are recognised, the entity also recognises a corresponding receivable or payable. At settlement date, this receivable or payable is eliminated by adjusting the amount initially recognised for the financial asset. This results in the asset being recognised at its fair value at the settlement date (see Illustration 2-3 B and Illustration 2-3 C below for an example of the journal entries that would occur). Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement Initial measurement 3.1 General requirements On initial recognition, financial assets and financial liabilities at fair value through profit or loss are normally measured at their fair value on the date they are initially recognised. The initial measurement of other financial instruments is based on their fair value, but adjusted in respect of any transaction costs that are incremental and directly attributable to the acquisition or issue of the financial instrument. [IFRS 9.5.1.1]. Furthermore, as outlined in Chapter 46 at section 3.1, there is also an adjustment to the fair value at initial recognition for fees received as these are included in the calculation of the effective interest rate. [IFRS 9 Appendix A]. There are also certain exceptions and additional considerations to these general requirements that are addressed in the sections 3.2 to 3.7 below. If at initial recognition, there is uncertainty about the collection of future cash flows relating to financial assets, this uncertainty is reflected in the fair value measurement at initial recognition. As a result, a separate allowance for expected credit losses relating to uncertain future cash flows is not recognised at initial recognition. However, at the first reporting date after initial recognition, for a financial asset subject to the impairment requirements of IFRS 9, an impairment assessment will be required. [IFRS 9.5.5.3]. 3.2 Short-term receivables and payables and trade receivables With the exception of trade receivables arising from transactions within the scope of IFRS 15 dealt with below, short-term receivables and payables with no stated interest rate could be measured at invoice amounts, without discounting, when the effect of not discounting is immaterial. [IFRS 9.B5.1.1, IFRS 9.B5.1.2A, IFRS 13.58, IFRS 13.B4]. Although IFRS 13 specifies how to measure fair value, IFRS 9 contains restrictions on recognising differences between the transaction price and the initial fair value as measured under IFRS 13 (referred to here as day 1 profits), which apply in addition to the requirements of IFRS 13 (see Chapter 15 at section 12.2). [IFRS 9.5.1.1A, IFRS 13.60, IFRS 13.BC138]. It may be the case that the transaction price is determined to represent the fair value of a financial instrument at initial recognition, and a valuation technique that uses unobservable inputs will be used to measure the fair value of the item in subsequent periods. In this case, the valuation technique must be calibrated at initial recognition to ensure that the result of the valuation technique equals the transaction price, reflecting current conditions (see Chapter 15 at section 13.1.3.A). [IFRS 13.64]. If an entity determines that the fair value on initial recognition differs from the transaction price, then the facts and circumstances leading to that difference need to be assessed to determine if part of the consideration is for something other than the financial instrument. For example, the difference may qualify for recognition as another type of asset, liability or other transaction such as an equity contribution from an owner (see section 3.3.1 below). [IFRS 9.B5.1.1]. Another Illustration is given in Chapter 24 at section 4.5.9 with regards to security deposits received in lease agreements where the deposit earns interest below the market rate. If it is determined that the difference does not qualify for recognition as another type of asset, liability or as an equity transaction, then the day 1 profit is recognised as a gain or loss on initial recognition only if the fair value is based on a quoted price in an active market for an identical asset or liability (i.e., a Level 1 input), or based on a valuation technique that uses only data from observable markets. Otherwise, the day 1 profit is deferred. [IFRS 9.B5.1.2A]. The deferred profit is recognised as an adjustment to the initial carrying amount of the financial asset or financial liability. This is on the basis that the financial asset or financial liability represents a single unit of account, and IFRS 9 requires that it be initially measured at the fair value, adjusted to defer the difference between the fair value and the transaction price. [IFRS 9.B5.1.2A(b)]. However, there may be practical difficulties in adjusting the carrying amount of the financial instrument directly. For example, a portfolio approach may be used on initial recognition to measure the fair value of a number of financial instruments for which there is a resulting deferred profit, and the entity may have difficulty in allocating the deferred profit to the individual financial instruments. In such cases, the entity performs an analysis based on the facts and circumstances, and the general principles in IFRS accounting standards (including an assessment of materiality), to determine the appropriate treatment. Accordingly, in practice we have observed cases where the deferred profit is recognised separately from the individual financial assets and liabilities. The deferred day 1 profit is subsequently recognised as a gain or loss only to the extent that it arises from a change in a factor (including time) that market participants would take into account when pricing the asset or liability. [IFRS 9.B5.1.2A(b)]. As a result of the day 1 profit provisions discussed above, entities that trade in financial instruments are prevented from immediately recognising a profit on the initial recognition of many financial instruments that are not quoted in active markets, or whose fair value is not measured based on valuation techniques that use only observable inputs. Consequently, locked-in profits will emerge over the life of the financial instrument, although precisely how they should emerge is not at all clear.
Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement The IASB has previously been asked to clarify whether straight-line amortisation was an appropriate method of recognising the day 1 profits, but it decided not to do so. IFRS 9 does not discuss this, although IAS 39 Financial Instruments: Recognition and Measurement used to state (without further explanation) that straight-line amortisation may be an appropriate method in some cases, but will not be appropriate in others. [IAS 39(2017).BC222(v)(ii)]. As a result, one interpretation is that the deferred day 1 profit is recognised on a systematic basis over time (for example using straight line amortisation). However, if the underlying instrument has non-linear features (such as option based contracts), then non-linear amortisation may be more appropriate than straight-line amortisation. An alternative interpretation is that the deferred day 1 profit should remain unrecognised until all market inputs have become observable. The appropriate approach for the recognition of the deferred day 1 profit will depend on the particular facts and circumstances, and must be applied consistently to underlying financial instruments of a similar nature and type. 3.3.1 Interest-free and low-interest long-term loans As noted in section 3.3 above, the fair value of a financial instrument on initial recognition is normally the transaction price. IFRS 9 further explains that if part of the consideration given or received was for something other than the financial instrument, the entity must measure the fair value of the financial instrument in accordance with IFRS 13. For example, the fair value of a long-term loan or receivable that carries no interest can be estimated as the present value of all future cash receipts, discounted using the prevailing market rate(s) of interest for instruments that are similar as to currency, term, type of interest rate, credit risk and other factors. Any additional amount advanced is an expense or a reduction of income unless it qualifies for recognition as some other type of asset. [IFRS 9.B5.1.1]. IFRS 13 requires the application of a similar approach in such circumstances. [IFRS 13.60]. For example, an entity may provide an interest free loan to a supplier in order to receive a discount on goods or services purchased in the future, and the difference between the fair value and the amount advanced might well be recognised as an asset, for example under IAS 38 Intangible Assets, if the entity obtains a contractual right to the discounted supplies. Similar issues often arise from transactions between entities under common control. In fact, IFRS 13 suggests a related party transaction might indicate that the transaction price is not the same as the fair value of an asset or liability [IFRS 13.B4(a)] (see Chapter 15 at section 12.1.1). How we see it Parent entities may lend money to subsidiaries on an interest-free or low-interest basis, where the loan is not repayable on demand. Where, in its separate financial statements, the parent (or subsidiary) is required to record a receivable (or payable) on initial recognition at a fair value that is lower than cost, the additional consideration will normally represent an additional investment in the subsidiary (or equity contribution from the parent). This is covered further in Chapter 9 at section 4.4.5.A. Another example is a loan received from a government at a rate that appears to be below-market. If there is a benefit of a below-market rate loan (i.e., the excess of the consideration received over the initial carrying amount of the loan), then this may have to be accounted for as a government grant. [IAS 20.10A]. The treatment of government grants is discussed further in Chapter 25. If a financial instrument is recognised where the terms are ‘off-market’ (e.g., the stated interest rate is lower than the market rate for similar loans), but instead a fee is paid or received in compensation, the instrument is recognised at its fair value i.e., net of the fee that is paid or received. [IFRS 9.B5.1.2]. This is outlined in the Illustration below. Illustration 3-1: Off-market loan with origination fee Bank J lends CU1,000 to Company K. The loan carries interest at 5% and is repayable in full in five years’ time, even though the market rate for similar loans is 8%. To compensate J for the below market rate of interest, K pays J an origination fee of CU120. There are no other directly related payments by either party. The loan is recorded at its fair value of CU880 (in this Illustration, assumed to be the net present value of CU50 interest payable annually for five years and CU1,000 principal repaid after five years, all discounted at 8%). This equals the net amount of cash exchanged (CU1,000 loan less CU120 origination fee), and hence no gain or loss is recognised on initial recognition of the loan. The upfront fee received of CU120 compensates Bank J for the interest shortfall of CU30 for each of the next five years, discounted at the market rate of 8%.

[IFRS 9.5.1.1]. **[IFRS 9.B5.7.1, IFRS 9.IG E.1.1].**
And • Transaction costs relating to the acquisition or incurrence of financial instruments at fair value through profit or loss are recognised in profit or loss as they are incurred. [IAS 32.38]. The initial recognition of a compound instrument is discussed in more detail in Chapter 43 at section 6.2. This is discussed further in Chapter 47 at section 5.8.1.A. Treating internal costs as transaction costs could open a number of possibilities for abuse by allowing entities to defer expenses inappropriately. Consequently, internal costs are treated as transaction costs only if they are incremental and directly attributable to the acquisition, issue or disposal of a financial asset or financial liability. [IAS 39(2017).BC222(d)].5 Therefore, it will be rare for internal costs (other than, for instance, commissions paid to sales staff in respect of a product sold that results in the origination or issuance of a financial instrument) to be treated as transaction costs. The determination of which costs are incremental and directly attributable can be highly judgemental, particularly when costs are incurred in advance of the instrument being recognised. For example, a lender may incur professional fees to value a property that would serve as collateral for a loan in advance of providing a quote to the customer. In this case, it is useful to consider the guidance in IAS 32 which specifies that if an equity transaction is abandoned, then the costs are recognised as an expense (see Chapter 43 at section 8.1). [IAS 32.37]. This suggests that the possibility of a transaction ultimately not occurring is not by itself a reason to not consider those costs as transaction costs. Where transaction costs are incurred in advance of the underlying financial instrument, they are normally capitalised as a prepayment. 3.5 Embedded derivatives and financial liability hosts Under IFRS 9, an embedded derivative is separated from a financial liability host contract when certain conditions are met (see Chapter 42 at section 3). Embedded derivatives are not separated from financial assets within the scope of IFRS 9, and the requirements of IFRS 9 are applied to the hybrid contract as a whole.
**[IFRS 9.4.3.2].**
If there is a difference between the transaction price, and the fair value of the financial instrument as a whole, a day 1 profit will arise. However, this day 1 profit may only be recognised as a gain or loss on initial recognition if the hybrid instrument has a quoted price in an active market for an identical instrument (i.e., a level 1 input), or if it is valued using a valuation technique that uses only data from observable markets (see section 3.3 above). [IFRS 9.B5.1.2A(a)]. In Chapter 42 at section 5, the treatment at initial recognition of an embedded derivative and the financial liability host contact is discussed. It is explained that an embedded non-option derivative is required to have a fair value of zero on initial recognition. By contrast, at initial recognition an embedded option-based derivative would not normally have a fair value of zero. Furthermore, the financial liability host instrument shall initially be measured based on the residual amount, after separating out the embedded derivative. [IFRS 9.B4.3.3].
This discussion was included in the Basis for Conclusions to IAS 39. However, it holds true under IFRS 9 as the related requirements were brought into IFRS 9 unchanged.
Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement IFRS 9 does not address how to allocate transaction costs that relate to a hybrid financial liability where an embedded derivative is separated from the financial liability host. Therefore, when allocating transaction costs to a hybrid financial instrument, entities should choose an accounting policy and apply it consistently. The approaches more commonly observed are: • Approach 1: To allocate the transaction costs exclusively to the non-derivative financial liability host, in which case the transaction costs are included in full in the host liability’s initial measurement. • Approach 2: To allocate the transaction costs to the non-derivative financial liability host and the embedded derivative in proportion to their fair values at initial recognition, i.e., in a similar fashion to compound financial instruments. Under this approach: • The proportion attributable to the non-derivative financial liability host will be included in the host liability’s initial measurement • The proportion attributable to the embedded derivative will be expensed as incurred as the embedded derivative is measured at fair value through profit or loss If the embedded derivative is required to have an initial fair value of zero, then in essence, under Approach 2, no transaction costs will be allocated to the embedded derivative. Hence the choice of approach will usually only lead to a different outcome on initial recognition when the embedded derivative has a non-zero fair value, for example the embedded derivative has an option feature. Approach 2 would need to be adapted if the embedded derivative is an asset, for example by allocating the transaction costs in proportion to the absolute fair values of the host and embedded derivative. Illustration 3-4: Initial recognition of embedded derivatives and financial liability hosts (with transaction costs) Background Information • Entity E with a functional currency of euros, issues a zero-coupon bond with a par value of €100, which is determined to be the fair value of the hybrid instrument on that date. The proceeds received were €100. • The bond has a maturity of 3 years. At that point the counterparty has the option to demand settlement at US$100 or €100. • Transaction costs of €5 were incurred by Entity E. • Entity E will account for the bond at amortised cost. • As the settlement feature at maturity could be in a currency that is different to the nominal value, there is a resulting cross currency option. • This embedded derivative is not considered to be closely related to the host liability measured at amortised cost, and therefore separation is required (see Chapter 42 section 4.2.1). • The embedded derivative is assessed to be a liability, with a fair value on initial recognition of €20. At initial recognition • €20 is allocated to the derivative liability as the fair value at initial recognition. • The financial liability host instrument is measured based on the residual amount, after separating out the embedded derivative. Therefore, a value of €80 (€100-€20=€80) is allocated initially to the liability. Allocation of the transaction costs • If Approach 1 is followed, the transaction costs are allocated exclusively to the non-derivative financial liability host. Consequently, the full €5 is allocated to the host liability. As the bond is carried at amortised cost, the transaction costs are included in full in the bond’s initial measurement. As a result, the bond is initially recognised at €75; and the embedded derivative is initially measured at €20. • If Approach 2 is followed, the transaction costs are allocated to the non-derivative financial liability host and the embedded derivative in proportion to their fair values. Consequently: • Transaction costs of €1 (€5x20/100) are allocated to the derivative. As the derivative is carried at fair value, the transaction costs of €1 are recognised immediately in profit and loss, and the embedded derivative is recognised initially at €20. • Transaction costs of €4 (€5x80/100) are allocated to the liability, and deducted from its initial carrying amount. As a result, the bond liability is initially recognised at €76 (€80-€4).

In such circumstances, rather than being initially measured by reference to their fair value on the date they are first recognised, i.e., settlement date, these financial instruments are initially measured by reference to their fair value on the trade date (see Illustration 2-3A at section 2.2.5 above).
**[IFRS 9.5.1.2].**
In practice, the difference will rarely be significant because of the short time scale involved between trade date and settlement date. 3.7 Assets and liabilities arising from loan commitments Loan commitments are a form of derivative financial instrument, although for pragmatic reasons the IASB decided that certain loan commitments could be excluded from most of the recognition and measurement requirements of IFRS 9 (see Chapter 41 at section 2.5). Nevertheless IFRS 9 does require issuers to apply its impairment rules to such loan commitments (see Chapter 47 at section 11). [IFRS 9.2.1(g)]. This creates a degree of uncertainty over how assets and liabilities arising from such arrangements should be measured on initial recognition, as discussed in the Illustration below. For simplicity, any other amounts payable by the borrower to the lender (such as commitment and non- utilisation fees), have not been illustrated. The treatment of commitment fees received or paid is addressed in the frequently asked questions within Chapter 46 at section 2.8. Illustration 3-5: Drawdown under a committed borrowing facility • Company H obtains from Bank Q a committed facility allowing it to borrow up to CU10,000 at any time over the following five years, provided certain covenants specified in the facility agreement are not breached. • Interest on any drawdowns is payable at the risk-free rate, plus a fixed margin, representing Q’s initial assessment of H’s credit risk, resulting in a variable market rate. • Any such borrowings can be repaid at any time at the option of H, but must be repaid by the end of five years, unless the facility is renegotiated and extended. They also become repayable immediately in the event that H breaches the covenants. • After one year, no drawdowns have been made and H’s credit risk has increased (although it has not breached any of the covenants and there is no expectation of default). As a result of this change in credit risk, the fair value of the facility is CU200 (positive value to H, negative value to Q). • Shortly afterwards, H draws down the maximum CU10,000 available under the facility. • Because of the change in credit risk, the loan resulting from the drawdown has a fair value at that date of CU9,800. The CU200 difference between the fair value of CU9,800 of the financial instrument created, and the CU10,000 cash transferred effectively represents the change in fair value of the commitment arising from the change in H’s credit risk. Questions: • Should Q (H) initially measure the resulting asset (liability) at its CU9,800 fair value or at CU10,000, being the amount of cash actually exchanged? • If it is recognised at CU9,800, how is the ‘spare’ CU200 accounted for, and does Q (H) recognise this as a loss (profit)? In order to be able to answer this question, we need to consider the accounting for the loan commitment up until the date of drawdown and how its carrying amount impacts the initial measurement of the resulting loan.
Financial instruments: Recognition and initial measurement 3.7.1 Loan commitments outside the scope of IFRS 9 In Illustration 3-5 above, the commitment cannot be settled net and it is not at a below-market interest rate. Therefore, unless H or Q designate the loan commitment at fair value through profit or loss, it will be outside the scope of IFRS 9. The accounting for the loan commitment outside the scope of IFRS 9 would be as follows: • At the next reporting date after Q and H enter into the loan commitment, Q would assess and record a provision for expected credit losses under the impairment requirements of IFRS 9 (see Chapter 47 at section 11), say CU10. Nothing else is recognised in the accounts of H in respect of the facility because the loan commitment is not recognised. • When the credit risk increases in the following year, Q assesses and recognises any impact the increase in credit risk may have had on the provision for expected credit loss. Assume the ECL provision at this point has increased from CU10 to CU35. Therefore, until the time of drawdown, the only accounting entries for Q are in relation to the impairment requirements applicable to loan commitments. At the time the loan is drawn down, Q classifies the loan within financial assets at amortised cost and H classifies it within financial liabilities at amortised cost. The general requirement under IFRS 9 as noted in section 3.1 above would require the asset (liability) to be initially measured at its fair value, i.e., CU9,800. This would result in: • For Q, the recognition of a further loss of CU165 (CU200 - CU35). • For H, the recognition of a profit of CU200. This is because the spare CU200 arising as the difference between the fair value (CU9,800) and the amount delivered (CU10,000) does not represent any other asset or liability arising from the transaction. However, the Basis for Conclusions on IFRS 9 explains that the effect of the loan commitment exception is to achieve consistency with the measurement basis of the resulting loan when the holder exercises its right, i.e., amortised cost. Changes in the fair value of these commitments arising from changes in market interest rates or credit spreads will therefore not be recognised or measured, in the same manner that changes in such rates and spreads would not affect the amortised cost of the financial asset (or financial liability) recognised once the right is exercised. [IFRS 9.BCZ2.3]. This is exactly what the ‘spare’ CU200 represents, so in accordance with the underlying rationale and objective of allowing loan commitments to be excluded from the scope of IFRS 9, it is appropriate to initially measure the asset or liability arising in this case at CU10,000. It is worth mentioning that the expected credit loss provision previously recognised for the loan commitment is incorporated into the allowance for the drawn down loan upon initial recognition, and then subsequently reassessed at each reporting date. The treatment under the loan commitment exception is consistent with that of similar assets arising from regular way transactions recognised using settlement date accounting (see section 3.6 above). This is relevant because the IASB introduced the loan commitment exception as a result of issues identified by the IAS 39 Implementation Guidance Committee (IGC) and the only solution the IGC could identify at the time involved treating loan commitments as regular way transactions and using settlement date accounting.6
IAS 39 Implementation Guidance Committee (IGC), Q&A 30-1, July 2002.

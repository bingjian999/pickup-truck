Statement of cash flows Overview IAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows requires the provision of information about the historical changes in cash and cash equivalents of an entity by means of a statement of cash flows. IAS 7 requires that this statement of cash flows, which is a primary financial statement, classifies cash flows as operating, investing or financing. This chapter addresses the preparation of the statement of cash flows and the related disclosures required by IAS 7. The chapter addresses the practical issues that frequently arise when attempting to classify cash flows as operating, investing and financing, and includes a number of areas where judgement may be required. The chapter also discusses the definitions of cash and cash equivalents, and the difficulties commonly encountered when applying these definitions. What you need to know • IAS 7 requires all entities to prepare a statement of cash flows that reports changes in cash and cash equivalents in the period. • Cash flows must be classified as operating, investing or financing, having regard to the definitions in IAS 7, in a manner that is most appropriate to the business of the entity. • Only investments that are held to meet short term cash commitments can be classified as cash equivalents. • Non-cash transactions must be excluded from the statement of cash flows. • IAS 7 sets out additional disclosure requirements, including: • Information on changes in liabilities arising from financing activities • Details of components of cash and cash equivalents and the policy the entity adopts for determining the composition of cash and cash equivalents • The amount of significant cash and cash equivalent balances not available for use by the entity
Statement of cash flows Introduction 1.1 Background IAS 7 is one of the older standards in IFRS accounting standards, having been issued in 1992. The standard specifies how entities report information about the historical changes in cash and cash equivalents and applies to all entities regardless of their business activities, including financial institutions. Modifications to IAS 7 since it was originally published have been limited resulting in a standard that is much shorter and more succinct than many new and revised IFRS accounting standards. It was last updated in 2023 to require entities to provide disclosures about supply chain financing arrangements. IAS 7 requires that inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents are classified and reported under three different headings being: • Operating activities • Investing activities • Financing activities [IAS 7.10] The resulting information in the statement of cash flows should provide insight into an entity’s ability to generate cash to fund operations, reinvest to maintain or expand operating capacity, repay debt and distribute dividends, and how it manages cash. The definitions of operating, investing and financing activities in the standard, which are covered in section 1.5 below, provide guidance on how to distinguish the cash flows by activity. The standard is accompanied by a number of illustrative examples. These illustrative examples are referred to, where relevant, throughout this chapter. 1.2 The statement of cash flows as a primary financial statement The statement of cash flows is a primary financial statement and must be presented with equal prominence to the statement of profit or loss and comprehensive income, the statement of financial position and the statement of changes in equity. [IAS 1.11]. Arguably, it provides some of the most important information in the financial statements, as all entities need cash to run their day-to-day operations, pay their obligations and provide returns to investors, regardless of their particular line of business or geographical location. This information, when combined with information in the rest of the financial statements, is useful in assessing factors that may affect the entity’s liquidity, financial flexibility, profitability, and risk. The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting (the Conceptual Framework) highlights the importance of information about cash flows in the reporting period as a means to help users understand a reporting entity’s operations, evaluate its financing and investing activities, assess its liquidity or solvency and interpret other information about financial performance. [CF 1.20]. This was rightly noted by the IFRS IC in its July 2014 agenda decision regarding the presentation and content of the condensed statement of cash flows in the interim financial statements according to IAS 34 Interim Financial Reporting. The agenda decision 1 noted that even a condensed statement of cash flows should include all information that is relevant in understanding the entity’s ability to generate cash flows and the entity’s needs to utilise these cash flows. Cash flow information also helps users to assess management’s stewardship of the entity’s economic resources. The Conceptual Framework guidance is further discussed in Chapter 2 at section 4.2. 1.3 Transparency and consistency of cash flow presentation In recent years, the statement of cash flows has become an area of scrutiny for investors as well as regulators from a number of jurisdictions, with both classification errors and insufficient disclosures highlighted as key areas of concern. This is partly because the use of cash flows as a key performance metric in entities’ communications to the financial markets has become increasingly common along with, and sometimes even in replacement of, other Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) more focused on the generation of income.

Whilst it is tempting to think that a statement of cash flows will be a simple matter of fact with less need for judgement around recognition and measurement of cash flows compared to other areas of the financial statements, the lack of clarity in the standard as to how some cash flows from common transactions are required to be categorised has resulted in diversity amongst preparers. Some common areas where judgement may be required are discussed in sections 3 and 4 below in reference to the definition of cash and cash equivalents and classification. In addition, the interaction of IAS 7 with new standards such as IFRS 9 Financial Instruments, IFRS 15 Revenue from Contracts with Customers and IFRS 16 Leases has led to further difficulties in applying the standard. The IFRS IC has received numerous requests for clarification on the requirements of the standard. However, many of these clarifications have never been finalised, leaving preparers and users faced with many practical difficulties, particularly in the application of the classification requirements of the standard. Some of the most common difficulties are discussed in the remainder of this chapter. The preparation of a cash flow statement is not merely a mechanical exercise. As a result, investors and regulators have focused on the adequacy of disclosures explaining how the requirements of IAS 7 have been applied. Where the appropriate classification of cash flows in the cash flow statement is not clear from IAS 7 and an entity has therefore applied judgement to determine the appropriate presentation of material items based on the principles of IAS 7, these judgements must be clearly disclosed. This is further discussed in section 4.4 below. 1.4 Future developments 1.4.1 IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial statements IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements, which supersedes IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements for reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027 (with earlier application permitted) was issued by the IASB in April 2024. The standard makes limited consequential amendments to the existing requirements of IAS 7. The amendments eliminate the current policy choice in IAS 7 for classification of cash flows from interest and dividends in the statement of cash flows. Dividends paid will be included in financing cash flows. Entities that do not have a specified main business activity of investing in assets or providing finance to customers will be required to classify interest paid as financing activities, and interest and dividends received as cash flows from investing activities. For entities that do have a specified main business activity of investing in assets or providing finance to customers, the classification of dividends received, interest paid and interest received will be impacted by the classification of the related income and expenses in the statement of profit or loss. When applying the indirect approach, IAS 7 is amended to require that the starting point for determining cash flows from operating activities is the operating profit or loss subtotal, which is a required subtotal in the income statement under IFRS 18. IFRS 18 and the impact on the statement of cash flows is discussed further in Chapter 4 at section 5.1. 1.4.2 IFRS 19 Subsidiaries without Public Accountability: Disclosures IFRS 19 Subsidiaries without Public Accountability: Disclosures was issued by the IASB in May 2024 to simplify the preparation of financial statements by eligible subsidiaries. Eligible subsidiaries that elect to apply IFRS 19 are allowed to provide reduced disclosures while still applying the recognition, measurement and presentation requirements in other IFRS accounting standards. IFRS 19 is effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with earlier application permitted (subject to jurisdictional endorsement). Statement of cash flows Objective and scope of IAS 7 2.1 Objective The objective of IAS 7 is to require entities to provide information about changes in cash and cash equivalents in the reporting period in a statement which classifies cash flows from operating, investing and financing activities. The standard aims to give users of financial statements a basis to evaluate the entity’s ability to generate cash and cash equivalents and its needs to utilise those cash flows. [IAS 7 Objective]. The standard explains that a statement of cash flows, in combination with the rest of the financial statements, provides information to users to enable them to evaluate the changes in net assets of an entity, its financial structure (including its liquidity and solvency) and its ability to affect the amounts and timing of cash flows to adapt to changing circumstances and opportunities. [IAS 7.4]. The historical cash flow information is often used as an indicator of the amount, timing and certainty of future cash flows. It is also useful in examining the relationship between profitability and net cash flow and the impact of changing prices. [IAS 7.5]. 2.2 Scope A statement of cash flows is required to make up a complete set of any financial statements in accordance with IFRS accounting standards. [IAS 1.10]. IAS 7 therefore applies to all entities, regardless of size, operations, ownership structure or industry, and includes wholly owned subsidiaries and banks, insurance entities and other financial institutions. There are no exemptions from the requirements of the standard. The standard argues that users of an entity’s financial statements are interested in how the entity generates and uses cash and cash equivalents regardless of the nature of its activities and irrespective of whether cash can be viewed as the product of the entity, as may be the case with a financial institution. All entities need cash to conduct their operations, to pay their obligations, and to provide returns to their investors. Accordingly, all entities are required to present a statement of cash flows. [IAS 7.3].
Statement of cash flows Defining cash and cash equivalents Since the objective of a statement of cash flows is to provide an analysis of changes in cash and cash equivalents, an understanding of the definition of cash and of cash equivalents is essential to its preparation. A statement of cash flows under IAS 7 excludes movements between items that are cash and cash equivalents because these are components of an entity’s cash management, rather than part of its operating, investing and financing activities. [IAS 7.9]. Amounts that fail to meet the definitions of cash and cash equivalents, for example due to restrictions (as in Illustration 3-2 in section 3.2.5 below), cannot be included as part of cash and cash equivalents for the purposes of preparing the statement of cash flows. In the statement of financial position, such amounts must be presented separately from cash and cash equivalents. Cash flows relating to restricted amounts that fail to meet the definition of cash and cash equivalents would generally meet the definition of investing activities, as they relate to the acquisition or disposal of investments (see section 4.2 below). Cash held in a foreign currency or investments acquired in a currency other than the functional currency of the entity (‘foreign currency’) are not excluded from qualifying as cash and cash equivalents merely because they are denominated in a foreign currency, provided they meet the other criteria discussed above. 3.1 Cash Extract from IAS 7 The following terms are used in this Standard with the meanings specified: Cash comprises cash on hand and demand deposits. …

IAS 7 defines cash as ”cash on hand and demand deposits” and includes no other requirements with respect to determining whether an item qualifies as cash. This is in contrast to the definition of cash equivalents which, as explained in section 3.2 below, includes the requirement that cash equivalents are held for the purpose of meeting short-term cash commitments rather than for investment or other purposes. In defining ‘cash’, IAS 7 does not explain what is meant by ‘demand deposits’, perhaps because the term is commonly understood as amounts that can be withdrawn on demand, without prior notice being required or a penalty being charged (for example, by an additional fee or forfeiture of interest). In contrast to ‘demand deposits’, term deposits are discussed in section 3.2.3 below. In practice, cash may be held by an entity for a specific purpose or may be subject to restrictions on its use, imposed either by the bank, by the entity or by a third party. The nature of any restriction on use of cash (and cash equivalents - see section 3.2.5 below) must be carefully assessed to determine if the balance still meets the definition of cash in the standard. 3.1.1 Restrictions on the use of cash In March 2022, the IFRS IC issued an agenda decision 3 following a request about whether an entity includes a demand deposit as a component of cash and cash equivalents in its statements of cash flows and financial position when the demand deposit is subject to contractual restrictions on use agreed with a third party. In the fact pattern described in the request, the entity: • Holds a demand deposit whose terms and conditions do not prevent the entity from accessing the amounts held in it (that is, were the entity to request any amount from the deposit, it would receive that amount on demand). • Has a contractual obligation with a third party to keep a specified amount of cash in that separate demand deposit and to use the cash only for specified purposes. If the entity were to use the amounts held in the demand deposit for purposes other than those agreed with the third party, the entity would be in breach of its contractual obligation.
IFRIC Update, March 2022, available on the IFRS Foundation’s website.
Statement of cash flows The IFRS IC concluded that • Restrictions on the use of a demand deposit arising from a contract with a third party do not result in the deposit no longer being cash unless those restrictions change the nature of the deposit in a way that it would no longer meet the definition of cash in IAS 7. • In the fact pattern described in the request, the contractual restrictions on use of the amounts held in the demand deposit do not change the nature of the deposit - the entity can access those amounts on demand. The IFRS IC therefore concluded that the entity includes the demand deposit as a component of ‘cash and cash equivalents’ in its statement of cash flows. The IFRS IC also noted that: • IAS 1 requires the entity to disaggregate the cash and cash equivalents line item in the statement of financial position and present the demand deposit separately when this is relevant to an understanding of its financial position. [IAS 1.54, IAS 1.55]. • IAS 7 requires an entity to disclose the components of cash and cash equivalents: applying this requirement, in this fact pattern, the entity discloses the demand deposit as a component of cash and cash equivalents. [IAS 7.45]. • The entity must also consider whether to disclose additional information in the context of the requirements in IFRS 7 Financial Instruments: Disclosures about liquidity risk and how the entity manages that risk. • If the information provided in accordance with IAS 7 and IFRS 7 is insufficient to enable users of financial statements to understand the impact of the restrictions on the entity’s financial position, the entity considers whether additional disclosures are required. [IAS 1.31]. Where the restrictions on use do not arise from a separate contractual arrangement with a third party but are part of the terms and conditions directly relating to use of and access to the bank account so that cash cannot be withdrawn on demand the cash held in the bank account will fail to meet the definition of cash. However, it is possible that it may meet the definition of cash equivalents. Section 3.2 below discusses considerations relevant for the classification of a deposit other than cash as cash equivalent. The IFRS IC agenda decision discussed in this section does not address the classification of cash held by a subsidiary of a group that meets the definition of cash in respect of the subsidiary but for which use is restricted within the wider group, for example, cash and cash equivalents held by a subsidiary operating under exchange controls or other legal restrictions that prevent their general use by the parent or other subsidiaries. Section 11.1.4 below discusses the disclosure requirements that apply in these situations. 3.1.2 Cryptocurrencies vs. Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDC) In a June 2019 agenda decision, the IFRS IC defined a cryptocurrency as a crypto-asset with all of the following characteristics: • a digital or virtual currency recorded on a distributed ledger that uses cryptography for security, • not issued by a jurisdictional authority or other party, and • does not give rise to a contract between the holder and another party. In the agenda decision the IFRS IC observed that cryptocurrencies did not have at that time the characteristics of cash. In particular, the IFRS IC noted that, although some cryptocurrencies could be used in exchange for particular goods or services, it was not aware of any cryptocurrency that was used as a medium of exchange or as the monetary unit in pricing goods or services to such an extent that it would have been the basis on which all transactions were measured and recognised in financial statements.4 Until now, the volume of transactions in which cryptocurrencies are used as a medium of exchange has remained quite low and some crypto-assets are not used as a medium of exchange at all at this stage. There are some jurisdictions where payment in bitcoin, for example, is accepted, but currently, the acceptance of cryptocurrencies by a merchant is not mandated in most jurisdictions.

Statement of cash flows How we see it We are not aware of any cryptocurrencies that would currently be considered cash. In future periods, a holder would need to consider whether those cryptocurrencies represent a medium of exchange and unit of account to such extent that it could be the basis on which the holder recognises and measures all transactions in its financial statements (i.e., it could act as the functional currency of an entity). Cryptocurrencies also currently do not meet the definition of cash equivalents because they are generally not convertible to known amounts of cash, nor are they subject to an insignificant risk of change in value or have maturities. Cryptocurrencies are discussed in more detail in Chapter 18 at section 11.6 and in our Applying IFRS: Accounting by holders of crypto assets.5 In contrast, many governments and central banks worldwide are exploring the possibility of using government-backed digital currencies. Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDC) are issued and regulated by a nation’s monetary authority or central bank. They are the digital form of a country’s fiat currency. For example, in October 2021, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) issued guidelines for the operation of a permissioned blockchain based CBDC named ’eNaira’. As per the Guidelines, eNaira is the digital form of the Nigerian fiat currency (Naira), issued by the CBN at par with the physical Naira (that is 1:1), as a direct liability of the CBN and as legal tender and part of the currency circulation. CBN exclusively can mint, distribute, redeem and destroy eNaira. How we see it Unlike cryptocurrencies discussed above, we believe that a Central Bank Digital Currency would normally be considered as cash because it is the digital form of a country’s fiat currency. This is because it is issued by a central bank, as a direct liability of that central bank and as legal tender in the jurisdiction of that Central bank recognised as a medium of exchange in the country of issuance, where prices are expressed in the local currency, of which the CBDC is the digital version. 3.1.3 Client money Some financial institutions and other entities hold money on behalf of clients. The terms on which such money is held can vary widely. This is discussed in more detail in Chapter 48 at section 8. There are numerous types of arrangements that result in an entity holding client money, and conversely result in an entity depositing their money in a client account held by another entity. It is impossible to generalise as to the appropriate treatment in either of the parties’ financial statement as this will be dependent on a careful assessment of the terms and conditions of each specific arrangement. However, as explained in Chapter 48 at section 8, there are a number of factors that must be considered. From the perspective of the depositary entity (i.e., the entity that holds the money on behalf of its client) it is important to identify precisely how the funds are held. Are they required to be segregated in bank accounts separate from that of the depositary entity itself or are they allowed to be commingled with those of the depositary entity? Other key considerations include: • Which party is at risk from the failure of assets, such as bank accounts, in which the client money is held? • The status of the funds in the event of the insolvency of either the reporting entity or its client • Whether the reporting entity can use the cash for its own purposes as opposed to administering the cash on behalf of the client in its capacity as an agent And • Which party has the benefit of income from the assets If the depositary entity assesses that the funds will be included as an asset on its statement of financial position, it would then need to assess what kind of financial asset this represents; cash or cash equivalents, by applying the definitions in IAS 7. A corresponding liability would also be recognised. Any restrictions on the use of the cash or cash equivalents must be considered and disclosed as explained in section 11.1 below. From the perspective of the entity depositing its money in a client account held by another entity, it would be necessary to consider whether the funds still meet the definition of cash (see section 3.1 above). This would only be the case if the funds are available to withdraw on demand, and without penalty, directly by the entity. [IAS 7.6]. If the funds held are subject to an advance notice period before they can be withdrawn or used, or there are other restrictions on their withdrawal, it is unlikely that they will meet the definition of cash.

For an investment to qualify as a cash equivalent it must be readily convertible to a known amount of cash and be subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. Therefore, an investment normally qualifies as a cash equivalent only when it has a short maturity of, say, three months or less from the date of acquisition. Equity investments are excluded from cash equivalents unless they are, in substance, cash equivalents, for example in the case of preferred shares acquired within a short period of their maturity and with a specified redemption date.
**[IAS 7.6, IAS 7.7].**
It is important to understand the reporting entity’s cash management policies, especially when considering whether balances that are not obviously cash on hand and demand deposits would be classified as cash equivalents, as the standard states that cash equivalents are held to meet short-term cash commitments rather than for investment or other purposes. [IAS 7.7]. Cash management includes the investment of cash in excess of immediate needs into cash equivalents, [IAS 7.9], such as short-term investments. For investments to qualify as a cash equivalent, they must be: • Short term • Highly liquid • Readily convertible into known amounts of cash And • Subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value [IAS 7.6] However, the purpose for which the investments are held must also be determined. Where investments meet the four criteria above but are not held by the entity to meet short term cash commitments, they cannot be classified as cash equivalents. As discussed below, careful consideration of facts and circumstances and judgement may be needed to determine whether an investment meets the definition of cash equivalents. 3.2.1 Short-term investments Cash equivalents are held for the purpose of meeting short-term cash commitments rather than for investment or other purposes. For an investment to qualify as a cash equivalent it must be readily convertible to a known amount of cash and be subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. The standard argues that, normally, only an investment with a short maturity of, say, three months or less from the date of acquisition qualifies as a cash equivalent - see section 3.2.3 below. Equity investments are excluded unless they are cash equivalents in substance. IAS 7 provides an example of such an instrument, being redeemable preference shares acquired within a short period of their maturity and with a specified redemption date. [IAS 7.7]. The IFRS IC considered, in July 2009,6 what the standard means when it refers to a ‘known amount of cash’ and confirmed that the amount of cash that will be received must be known at the time of the initial investment. Accordingly, traded commodities, such as gold bullion, would not be eligible for inclusion in cash equivalents because the proceeds to be realised from such an investment is determined at the date of disposal rather than being known or determinable when the investment is made.

Statement of cash flows 3.2.2 Money market funds Entities commonly invest in money market-type funds such as an open-ended mutual fund that invests in money market instruments like certificates of deposit, commercial paper, treasury bills, bankers’ acceptances and repurchase agreements. Although there are different types of money market funds, they generally aim to provide investors with low-risk, low-return investment while preserving the value of the assets and maintaining a high level of liquidity. The question then arises as to whether investments in such funds can be classified as cash equivalents. As discussed below, entities may consider the underlying investments of the fund when assessing the significance of the risk of changes in value, however when determining whether classification as a cash equivalent is appropriate, the unit of account is the investment in the money market fund itself. To meet the definition of a cash equivalent, an investment must be short term; highly liquid; readily convertible to a known amount of cash; and subject to insignificant risk of changes in value. IAS 7 considers a maturity date of three months or less from the date of acquisition to be short-term in the context of maturity, however money market funds generally do not have a legal maturity date. Instead, if the investments are puttable and can be sold back to the fund at any time, they meet the short-term criterion. Typically, investments in money market funds are redeemed directly with the fund, therefore in assessing liquidity of the investment, focus must be on the liquidity of the fund itself. In making this assessment, entities must consider the nature of any redemption restrictions in place as these may prevent the redemption of the investment in the short term. Alternatively, if a money market fund investment is quoted in an active market, it might be regarded as highly liquid on that basis. The short-term and highly liquid investment must also be readily convertible into known amounts of cash and be subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. [IAS 7.6]. As discussed in section 3.2.1 above, this means that the amount of cash that will be received must be known at the time of the initial investment. Accordingly, investments in shares or units of money market funds cannot be considered as cash equivalents simply because they are convertible at any time at the then market price in an active market. The IFRS IC also confirmed that an entity would have to satisfy itself that any investment was subject to an insignificant risk of change in value for it to be classified as a cash equivalent.7 How we see it In our view, in order for an entity to satisfy itself that an investment in a money market fund is subject to an insignificant risk of change in value, the entity must conclude that the range of possible returns is very small. This evaluation is first made at the time of acquiring the investment and reassessment is required if the facts and circumstances change. The evaluation will involve consideration of factors such as: • the maturity of the underlying investments of the fund • the credit rating of the fund; the nature of the investments held by the fund (i.e., not subject to volatility) • the extent of diversification in the portfolio (which is expected to be very high) • the investment policy of the fund And • any mechanisms by the fund to guarantee returns (for example by reference to short-term money market interest rates) Investments are often held for purposes other than to act as a ready store of value that can be quickly converted into cash when needed to meet short-term cash commitments. It is therefore important, even where the criteria above are met, to understand the purpose of an investment in a particular money market fund. Where an investment otherwise satisfies the criteria in IAS 7.6 but is not held by the entity to satisfy short-term cash commitments, it must not be classified as a cash equivalent. [IAS 7.7]. Substantial judgement may be required in assessing whether an investment in money market funds can be classified as a cash equivalent. Therefore, appropriate disclosure is essential. As discussed in section 11 below, entities are required to disclose the composition of cash equivalents and, where relevant, this will include significant judgements made in classifying investments in money market funds.

Statement of cash flows 3.2.3 Investments with maturities greater than three months The longer the term of an investment, the greater the risk that a change in market conditions (such as interest rates) can significantly affect its value. For this reason, IAS 7 excludes most equity investments from cash equivalents and restricts the inclusion of other investments to those with a short maturity (which the standard illustrates as three months or less from the date of their acquisition by the entity). [IAS 7.7]. An investment with a term on acquisition of, say, nine months is not reclassified as a cash equivalent from the date on which there is less than three months remaining to its maturity. This is because the classification as cash equivalent is based on the maturity and the nature of the instrument at acquisition. The fact that the instrument is now approaching its maturity does not change its nature nor the purpose for which it was acquired. In the final analysis, cash equivalents are held to meet short-term cash commitments and amounts can only be included in cash equivalents if they can be regarded as being nearly as accessible as cash and essentially as free from exposure to changes in value as cash. For example, an entity might justify including in cash equivalents a fixed deposit with an original term longer than three months if it effectively functions like a demand deposit. Typically, a fixed deposit will carry a penalty charge for withdrawal prior to maturity. A penalty will usually indicate that the investment is held for investment purposes rather than the purpose of meeting short-term cash needs. However, some fixed deposits still offer interest at a prevailing demand deposit rate in the event of early withdrawal, with any penalty limited to the entity being required to forego the incremental higher interest that it would have received if the deposit were held to maturity. In this case, it may be arguable that there is effectively no significant penalty for early withdrawal, as the entity receives at least the same return that it otherwise would have in a demand deposit arrangement. How we see it In our view, investments in deposit accounts that upon withdrawal are subject to penalties of such significance, that the funds obtained are appreciably different than the funds that would have been obtained had a shorter-term investment been initially purchased, are not consistent with the assertion that the investment was held to meet short-term cash needs. Significance is viewed from the perspective of the entity, taking into consideration the environment in which it operates. If the investor would be indifferent to the penalty because the funds obtained are relatively consistent with the funds that would have been obtained had a shorter-term investment been initially purchased, the terms of the instrument are consistent with the assertion that the investment was held for meeting short-term cash needs. This assessment may be subject to some degree of judgement based on facts and circumstances. If such a large disincentive exists, this would be inconsistent with the assertion that the investment is held for meeting short-term cash needs and the fixed deposit would not meet the definition of a cash equivalent. Therefore, the fixed deposit qualifies as a cash equivalent. Even if the DD rate is not significantly higher than zero, as long as it is not negative, the interest received on early redemption is still consistent with management’s investment purpose to meet short-term cash commitments and the fixed deposit qualifies as a cash equivalent. Evidence supporting the latter assertion would be that the bank balance often fluctuates from being positive to overdrawn. [IAS 7.8]. However, it is implicit in the definitions in IAS 7 that the amounts must still meet those definitions at the end of each reporting period to be presented as such. Changes that may affect the criteria for classification could arise from contractual or non-contractual changes. For contractual changes, it will usually be clear when these changes are made and what the nature of the changes are. Such changes could affect the nature of the asset and require the new asset to be tested against the definition of cash and cash equivalents. Other changes in facts and circumstances could also require careful consideration and the application of judgement. For cash equivalents, reassessment may be particularly relevant when looking at the requirements that cash equivalents are highly liquid and subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. For example, since initial recognition of an investment as a cash equivalent, there may have been adverse changes to the credit status of the counterparty to the investment or a significant decline in the economic stability of the jurisdiction in which the funds are invested. [IAS 7.6]. The standard states that the value of information on operating cash flows is twofold. It provides a key indicator of the extent to which the entity has generated sufficient cash flows from its operations to repay debt, pay dividends and make investments to maintain and increase its operating capability, without recourse to external sources of financing. Also, information about the components of historical operating cash flows may assist in the process of forecasting future operating cash flows, when used in conjunction with other financial statement information. [IAS 7.13]. Cash flows from operating activities generally result from transactions and other events that enter into the determination of profit or loss. Examples include: [IAS 7.14] • Cash receipts from the sale of goods and the rendering of services • Cash receipts from royalties, fees, commissions and other revenue • Cash payments to suppliers for goods and services • Cash payments to and on behalf of employees • Cash payments or refunds of income taxes unless they can be specifically identified with financing and investing activities • Cash receipts and payments from contracts held for dealing or trading purposes (see section 4.4.12 below regarding the allocation of cash flows paid or received under derivative contracts) Operating activities is also a ‘default category’ for any cash flows that do not meet the criteria of investing or financing cash flows. For example, as discussed in section 9.3.1 below, acquisition-related costs in a business combination that must be recognised as an expense, [IFRS 3.53], would also be classified as operating cash flows because there is no related asset that would justify classification as an investing cash flow. [IAS 7.16]. The nature of the business conducted by the entity will be a relevant factor in determining the appropriate classification of cash flows as operating, investing or financing. For example, when an entity holds securities and loans for dealing or trading purposes, they are similar to inventory acquired specifically for resale. Therefore, any related cash flows are classified as operating activities. Similarly, cash advances and loans made by financial institutions are usually classified as operating activities, since they relate to the main revenue-generating activity of that entity (see section 10.1 below). [IAS 7.15]. The proceeds from the sale of property, plant and equipment, which are usually included in cash flows from investing activities, are an example of an item that enters into the determination of profit or loss that is not usually an operating cash flow. [IAS 7.14]. However, the proceeds from sales of assets previously held for rental purposes are classified as cash flows from operating activities if the entity routinely sells such assets in its ordinary course of business. Similarly, cash payments to manufacture or acquire property, plant and equipment held for rental to others, and that are routinely sold in the ordinary course of business after rental, are also classified as cash flows from operating activities (see section 4.4.7 below). [IAS 7.14]. Cash flows from operating activities may be reported on a gross or net basis, also known as the direct and indirect methods. [IAS 7.18]. The use of the direct or indirect method is an accounting policy choice and must be applied consistently from period to period. Any change in method must be reported under IAS 8 Accounting Policies, Changes in Accounting Estimates and Errors. This would require comparatives to be restated and additional disclosures given, including the reasons for the change in policy. [IAS 8.29].

[IAS 7.31]. For a financial institution, interest paid, and interest and dividends received are usually classified as operating cash flows. However, IAS 7 notes that there is no consensus on the classification of these cash flows for other entities and goes on to state that: • Interest paid may be classified under either operating or financing activities. • Interest received and dividends received may be included in either operating or investing cash flows.
**[IAS 7.33].**
The standard allows dividends paid to be classified as a financing cash flow (because they are a cost of obtaining financial resources) or as a component of cash flows from operating activities. [IAS 7.34]. In Practical example 4-1 in section 4 above, AstraZeneca PLC has included interest paid under operating activities, interest received under investing activities and dividends paid under financing activities, as permitted by the standard. A different treatment is adopted by Telefonica, S.A. in Practical example 4-2 in section 4.1.1 above, where interest paid, interest received and dividends received are all included as operating cash flows and dividends paid are included as financing cash flows. All these treatments comply with the standard; however, the most appropriate treatment will depend on the nature of the entity and its activities. For example, if an entity does not include interest received or dividends received within revenue it may be more appropriate to include the interest or dividend cash inflows as investing cash flows rather than operating cash flows. This is because cash flows from operating activities are primarily derived from the principal revenue-generating activities of the entity, [IAS 7.14], and the amount of cash flows arising from operating activities is intended to be a key indicator of the extent to which the operations of the entity have generated sufficient cash flows to repay loans, pay dividends and make new investments without recourse to external sources of financing. [IAS 7.13]. Similarly, an entity could treat interest and dividends paid as financing cash flows because they are a cost of obtaining financial resources. [IAS 7.34]. In addition, the standard requires the total amount of interest paid during the period to be disclosed in the statement of cash flows, whether it has been recognised as an expense or capitalised as part of the cost of an asset in accordance with IAS 23 Borrowing Costs. [IAS 7.32]. A literal reading of this requirement might suggest that interest paid must be presented as a single figure under operating or financing activities. However, it could also be appropriate to classify payments of interests in accordance with the classification of the underlying asset to which those payments were capitalised provided that, when this is done, the total amount of interest paid is also disclosed either on the face of the statement of cash flows or in the notes. For example, payments of interest that is capitalised as part of the cost of property, plant and equipment would be classified as part of an entity’s investing activities, and payments of interest that is capitalised as part of the cost of inventories would be classified as part of an entity’s operating activities. An attempt to clarify the issue through an annual improvement12 was never finalised, mainly because the IFRS IC observed that amendments to IAS 7 relating to classification of cash flows should not be made on a piecemeal basis unless the classification was evident from the current guidance in IAS 7 and an amendment to IAS 7 would make that classification clearer.13 Where an entity has issued shares that are classified as financial liabilities in accordance with IAS 32 Financial Instruments: Presentation (see Chapter 43), the dividends paid on those shares will be presented in the statement of cash flows as either operating or financing in line with the entity’s presentation of other interest paid.

Statement of cash flows 4.4.2 Taxes on income Cash flows arising from taxes on income shall be separately disclosed within operating cash flows unless they can be specifically identified with investing or financing activities. [IAS 7.35]. Whilst it is possible to match elements of tax expense to transactions for which the cash flows are classified under investing or financing activities; taxes paid are usually classified as cash flows from operating activities because it is often impracticable to match tax cash flows with specific elements of tax expense. Also, those tax cash flows may arise in a different period from the underlying transaction. [IAS 7.36]. However, when it is practicable to make this determination, the tax cash flow is identified as an investing or financing activity in accordance with the individual transaction that gives rise to such cash flows. In cases where tax cash flows are allocated over more than one class of activity, disclosure is required of the total amount for taxes paid. [IAS 7.36]. 4.4.3 Sales taxes and other non-income tax cash flows Although it sets out requirements for the treatment of taxes on income, IAS 7 does not specifically address the treatment of cash flows relating to other taxes, such as value added tax (VAT) or other sales taxes and duty. The IFRS IC has considered whether it should add the question about VAT to its agenda and decided that it was not appropriate to develop an interpretation. [IAS 7.14(d)]. Such cash outflows would typically involve wages and salaries paid to employees, taxes paid on behalf of employees and any other employee benefit contributions made on behalf of employees. In the case of debt factoring, an entity aims to receive cash flows from trade receivables more quickly than would arise from normal collection from customers, generally by transferring rights over those receivables to a financial institution. This raises questions as to whether the transfer results in derecognition of the trade receivables, or to the continued recognition of the trade receivables and the recognition of a financial liability for the funding received from the factoring entity. Statement of cash flows How we see it In our view, the classification of the cash receipt from the financial institution depends on the accounting treatment of the trade receivables. Only to the extent that the factoring arrangement results in the derecognition of the original trade receivable would it be appropriate to regard the cash receipt in the same way as any other receipt from the sale of goods and rendering of services and classify it in operating activities. In cases where the trade receivable is not derecognised and a liability is recorded, the nature of the arrangement is a borrowing secured against trade receivables and accordingly we believe that the cash receipt from factoring must be treated in the same way as any short-term borrowing and included in financing activities. [IAS 7.17(c)]. The later cash inflow from the customer for settlement of the trade receivable would be included in operating cash flows and the reduction in the liability to the financial institution would be a financing outflow. Following the principle in IFRS 9 for the disclosure of income and expense relating to a transferred asset that continues to be recognised, [IFRS 9.3.2.22], these two amounts would not be offset in the statement of cash flows. However, it would be acceptable for the entity to disclose the net borrowing receipts from, and repayments to, the financial institution, if it was determined that these relate to advances made for and the repayment of short-term borrowings such as those which have a maturity period of three months or less. [IAS 7.23A(c)]. In some cases, the factoring arrangement requires customers to remit cash directly to the financial institution, but the arrangement does not result in the derecognition of the original trade receivables. However, there is currently diversity as to whether, and how, the cash received by the financial institution from customers are presented in the entity’s statement of cash flows. When the transfer does not give rise to derecognition of the trade receivable by the reporting entity, the reporting entity may determine that the factoring entity is acting as its agent and is therefore collecting the cash on its behalf before drawing down amounts to settle the liability it is owed by the reporting entity. In this case, the entity would report an operating cash inflow from the customer and a financing cash outflow to the financial institution when the financial institution collects the cash from the customers. Statement of cash flows 4.4.7 Property, plant and equipment held for rental Payments to acquire and receipts from the sale of, property, plant and equipment are usually included in investing cash flows; however, this is not always the case. A number of entities routinely sell assets that were previously held for rental, for example, car rental companies that acquire vehicles with the intention of holding them as rental cars for a limited period and then selling them. IAS 16 Property, Plant and Equipment requires an entity, that, in its ordinary course of business, routinely sells items of property, plant and equipment that it has held for rental to others, to classify the proceeds on the sale of such property, plant and equipment as revenue. [IAS 16.68A]. Accordingly, the proceeds from the sale of such assets are classified as cash flows from operating activities, as are cash payments to manufacture or acquire property, plant and equipment held for rental to others and routinely sold in the ordinary course of business. [IAS 7.14]. In our view, the requirement to classify payments for such property, plant and equipment held for rental under operating cash flows is intended to avoid initial expenditure on purchases of assets being classified as investing activities, while inflows from sales are recorded within operating activities. However, this means that management will need to determine, at the time of acquisition or manufacture, which of the assets that it intends to rent out will be ultimately held for sale in the ordinary course of business. 4.4.8 Cash received as compensation for an insured loss As noted in section 4.2 above, cash flows arising from investing activities include receipts from the sale of property, plant and equipment and other long-term assets. [IAS 7.16(b)]. This classification would also be appropriate if the receipt of cash came from insurance proceeds received to cover for the loss or damage of such assets. It would not be appropriate to present the cash inflow as an operating cash flow as the insurance proceeds are not derived from the principal revenue-generating activities of the business. [IAS 7.14]. However, the nature of the claim giving rise to the cash inflow must be carefully considered. If the claim relates only partially to the loss of damage of a long-term asset, it may be necessary for the cash inflows to be allocated between cash flows from operating activities (e.g., for the compensation for business interruption) and cash flows from investing activities. 4.4.9 Cash flows for service concession arrangements Because a cash flow is only classified in investing activities if it results in a recognised asset in the statement of financial position, a question arises regarding the classification of the cash inflows and outflows of the operator of a service concession arrangement that is within the scope of IFRIC 12 Service Concession Arrangements. IFRIC 12 features two possible accounting models - the intangible asset model or the financial asset model. Under both models, the service element relating to the construction of the infrastructure asset is accounted for in accordance with IFRS 15 and the revenue recognised gives rise to an intangible or a financial asset, in the form of a receivable, respectively. It is unclear whether cash flows incurred in the construction or upgrade phase must always be regarded as operating cash flows because they relate to the provision of construction services; or whether they are more accurately classified as investing activities, as they reflect cash outflows that result in the recognition of an asset. In the case of an arrangement under the intangible asset model, the cash flows incurred during construction or upgrading could be classified in investing activities as they relate to the acquisition of an intangible that will generate future income and cash flows. Once the operating phase is reached, the cash inflows received would be most appropriately classified in operating activities as most operating and maintenance costs are likely to be executory and will be accounted for as incurred. On the other hand, when the financial asset model applies, cash outflows during the construction phase may be considered to represent the provision of financing to the grantor and as such an investing outflow. In a corollary of the discussion on deferred payments in section 7.1 below, where the time value of money is significant to the transaction, the substance of the transaction may be the provision of a loan. Where this is the case the repayment of such an instrument by the grantor during the operating phase would be considered an investing cash flow (or potentially split between investing and operating cash flows for the capital and interest components respectively, depending on the policy of the entity). Since there is no specific guidance relating to the classification of cash flows for service concession arrangements, current practice is mixed. IFRIC 12 is discussed in more detail in Chapter 26.
Statement of cash flows 4.4.10 Treasury shares Treasury shares are an entity’s own equity instruments that are acquired and held by the entity, a subsidiary, or other members of the consolidated group. The consideration paid or received for treasury shares is recognised directly in equity and not as a movement in investments. [IAS 32.33]. Similarly, IAS 7 requires that payments and receipts to acquire or dispose of treasury shares are classified within financing activities. [IAS 7.17]. Even where such treasury shares are acquired by the entity as part of an equity-settled share-based payment transaction, the cash outflow is classified under financing activities. Whilst cash payments to and on behalf of employees are classified under operating activities, [IAS 7.14], the acquisition of treasury shares does not settle a transaction between the entity and its employees. An equity-settled share-based payment transaction is completed when the entity transfers its equity instruments to employees in consideration for the services received. When a cash payment is made by a subsidiary to its parent or a trust that holds treasury shares as part of an equity-settled share-based payment arrangement, the payment is accounted for as a deduction from equity in the separate financial statements of the subsidiary, on the grounds that the payment does not settle the transaction with the employees, but is effectively a distribution to the parent or the trust (see
# Chapter 30 at section 12.2.7). Having regarded this as a distribution, it follows that the cash flow must be
classified as either operating or financing, according to the entity’s policy on dividends as discussed in section 4.4.1 above. 4.4.11 Cash flows related to the costs of a share issue Costs directly related to the issue of shares are required to be deducted from equity. [IAS 32.35]. As the costs reduce the amount of the proceeds received from the share issue, they are classified as a financing cash flow. [IAS 7.17(a)]. However, where a proposed share issue is cancelled, there would be no proceeds from the issue to record and the related expenses would be included in profit and loss rather than equity. As such, the definition of a financing cash flow would not be met, and the transaction costs would be classified in operating cash flows. 4.4.12 Cash flows paid or received under derivative contracts Payments and receipts relating to derivative contracts can be classified within operating, investing or financing in different circumstances. Where the contract is held for dealing or trading purposes, the cash flows are classified under operating activities. [IAS 7.14(g)]. IAS 7 requires that payments for, and receipts from, futures contracts, forward contracts, option contracts and swap contracts are classified as cash flows from investing activities, except when the contracts are held for dealing or trading purposes, or the cash flows are classified as financing activities. [IAS 7.16(g)]. The standard adds that when a contract is accounted for as a hedge of an identifiable position, the cash flows of the contract are classified under the same heading as the cash flows of the position being hedged. [IAS 7.16]. An example is an interest rate swap. An entity wishing to convert an existing fixed rate borrowing into a floating rate equivalent could enter into an interest rate swap under which it receives interest at fixed rates and pays at floating rates. All the cash flows under the swap are reported under the same cash flow heading as interest paid (i.e., as financing activities or operating activities, in accordance with the entity’s determined policy, as discussed in section 4.4.1 above), because they are equivalent to interest or are hedges of interest payments. More generally, IFRS 9 adds to the requirements of IAS 7 by clarifying that cash flows arising from hedging instruments are classified as operating, investing or financing activities, on the basis of the classification of the cash flows arising from the hedged item and that this should be consistent with the classification of these instruments as hedging instruments under IFRS 9. [IFRS 9.IG G.2]. This seems to leave an open question as to how an entity classifies the cash flows from a derivative contract that is considered by management as part of a hedging relationship, but for which the entity elects not to apply hedge accounting (taking all movements to profit or loss)? Since IAS 7 has not been updated to reflect IFRS 9, this question is not dealt with explicitly in IAS 7. How we see it In our view, if a hedging relationship would qualify for hedge accounting had it been so designated, but has not been accounted for applying the hedge accounting requirements of IFRS 9, it would be permitted for the cash flows from the hedging instrument to be presented in the same category as the cash flows of the position being hedged.
Statement of cash flows This can be explained in the following Illustration: Illustration 4-1: Cash flows from derivatives not designated as hedges Company A has the euro as its functional currency. On 1 January 20X1, it sells goods to a US customer for which it charges US$1,000,000. The spot exchange rate on this date is 1:1 and it recognises revenue of €1,000,000. Payment is due to be received on 30 June 20X1. A enters into a forward contract to exchange US$1,000,000 for €1,095,000 on 30 June 20X1. The hedging relationship would qualify to be accounted for applying the hedge accounting requirements of IFRS 9. However, Company A does not designate it as a hedge because the effects of movements on the contract and those of retranslating the receivable will already offset in profit or loss. On 30 June 20X1 the exchange rate is such that A receives the equivalent of €1,200,000 from its customer and pays €105,000 on the forward contract. Taken literally, IAS 7 would suggest that, because the forward contract is not held for dealing or trading purposes and is not accounted for as a hedge of an identifiable position, the €105,000 cash outflow on the forward contract cannot be classified under operating activities. As such, the €105,000 would have to appear in investing or possibly financing cash flows. However, had the entity elected to apply hedge accounting, the standard would require the €105,000 to be included in operating cash flows. Therefore, as explained above, in our view entity A could include the payment on the forward contract in cash flows from operating activities. 4.4.13 Debt instrument issued at a discount or redeemed at a premium Cash outflows that represent the repayment of the principal amount of a loan or other debt instrument are classified as financing activities. [IAS 7.17]. As noted in section 4.4.1 above, interest paid may be classified under either operating or financing activities. Therefore, where an entity adopts a policy of classifying interest under operating activities, it may be necessary to consider carefully the nature of any payment to the lender to ensure the appropriate classification between operating and financing. This will be particularly relevant where the pattern of payments differs from the recognition of the interest expense in profit or loss. For example, a company that issues a zero-coupon bond at nominal value, with a premium payable on redemption, will not make any interest payments over the life of the bond. However, in accounting for the financial liability at amortised cost under IFRS 9, the entity will recognise interest at the EIR, calculated to reflect the premium payable on redemption, as explained in Chapter 46 at section 3.1. This raises the question of how to present the cash paid on redemption. Is it appropriate to present the entire cash outflow within financing activities as the redemption of a debt instrument? How we see it In our view, when an entity adopts a policy of classifying interest under operating activities and the cash paid on redemption is presented in the statement of cash flows, a split must be made to show the amount that relates to the interest cost (within operating activities) and the amount that relates to the repayment of the principal (within financing activities). The same principles would be applied where a debt is issued at a discount or in any other situation where the pattern of cash flows relating to interest on a financial liability are not reflected in the pattern of recognition of the interest cost. 4.4.14 Early settlement of a debt instrument A similar issue to that discussed in sections 4.4.13 above is how to present the cash payments on early settlement of a loan or other borrowing. As already noted in section 4.3 above, cash flows arising from financing activities include outflows to repay borrowings. However, in some situations an element of the cash paid on early settlement could be viewed as compensation to the lender for lost interest. Lenders will often include a prepayment penalty provision in loan agreements that can be based on a number of factors, including an approximation of the interest that will not be paid as a result of early settlement. This would support a split presentation of the cash outflow between repayment of borrowings and cash outflow for interest. Alternatively, the nature of the entire cash outflow may also be viewed as relating to a financing activity, being the extinguishment of a liability, which is then be presented as one amount in the financing activities section of the statement. 4.4.15 Receipt of a government grant The accounting requirements for government grants are set out in IAS 20 Accounting for Government Grants and Disclosure of Government Assistance and are covered in Chapter 25.
Statement of cash flows Neither IAS 20 nor IAS 7 provide guidance on where in the statement of cash flows to present the cash inflows from the receipt of government grants. The IFRS IC discussed in July 2012 the presentation of cash flows for several fact patterns including government grants.18 The issue was further discussed in March 2013. The IFRS IC observed that the primary principle behind the classification of cash flows in IAS 7 is that cash flows should be classified on the basis of the nature of the activity in a manner that is most appropriate to the business of the entity in accordance with paragraph 11 of IAS 7 and with the definitions of operating, investing and financing activities in paragraph 6 of IAS 7. In the course of the discussions held, staff initially suggested that grants were providing a source of financing to the entity, and consequently cash received would be part of the entity’s financing activities. In a later meeting, however, it reached a different conclusion that cash received from a government grant should be presented as an operating cash flow instead, on the basis that although cash inflows from a grant provide the entity with financing for the designated asset/activity, a grant does not represent a borrowing activity because there is no expectation that the resource will be returned to the provider of finance; or that the provider of finance will be appropriately compensated. Eventually, the IFRS IC concluded that clarifying the application of the primary classification principle in IAS 7 was a matter too broad for it to address and discontinued its work.19 Consequently, until and unless the IASB addresses this issue as part of its upcoming project on the cash flow statement, there is likely to be diversity in practice for the presentation of all government grants in the statement of cash flows, whether related to assets or to income. Cash flows received from government grants could be considered to be financing by nature because they provide a source of financing to the entity. Alternatively, cash flows received from government grants (or only those from grants related to income) could be presented as operating cash flows by default since they do not seem to meet the definition of financing activities and cash flows received from government grants related to assets could be presented as investing cash flows as they relate to an investing activity. How we see it Selecting the most appropriate presentation will be a matter of judgement. Once adopted, the presentation principles must be applied consistently and clear disclosure of the principles and the impact thereof on the cash flow statement must be provided in accordance with IAS 1. As discussed in section 5.2.1 below, IAS 7 requires the cash inflow from the receipt of a government grant to be presented gross in the statement of cash flows as there is no exception provided to the presentation of gross cash receipts and payments in the standard for such transactions. IAS 20 further emphasises this requirement specifically for government grants related to assets regardless of whether or not the government grant is deducted from the related asset for presentation purposes in the statement of financial position. Government grants are discussed in more detail in Chapter 25. 5.3 Presentation of cash paid or received on an entity’s behalf As discussed in section 7 below, IAS 7 requires that transactions that do not require the use of cash or cash equivalents are excluded from an entity’s statement of cash flows. [IAS 7.43]. Examples of such non-cash transactions provided in the standard are the conversion of debt to equity; acquiring assets by assuming directly related liabilities or by entering into a lease; and issuing equity as consideration for the acquisition of another entity. [IAS 7.44]. However, there is no broader guidance in IAS 7, or elsewhere in IFRS accounting standards, to help determine whether cash flows should be included in the statement of cash flows because the entity has directed another party to pay and/or receive cash on its behalf and so, in substance, the cash flows are the entity’s cash flows. This is a matter of judgement based on facts and circumstances. There may be transactions that, in substance, involve cash flows of the entity, without the cash resources of the entity actually changing. For example, where an entity purchases an item of property and finances the purchase by borrowing the funds from a financial institution, it may arrange for the money to be paid directly by the financial institution to the seller rather than receive the cash inflow from the loan into its bank account and make a subsequent cash payment to the seller. The relationship between the entity and the financial institution may be determined to be fiduciary in nature with the financial institution acting as an agent of the entity and receiving and incurring cash flows on its behalf. In all situations, the nature of the relationship with the third party will need careful examination, including assessment of the terms and conditions of each specific arrangement. Factors to consider as part of the consideration could include: • Who is directing the payment or receipt? If the reporting entity is directing the movement of the cash, it is more likely that the third party will be acting as its agent and therefore the cash flows will be cash flows of the entity. • Who controls the timing and amount of the payment or receipt? If the reporting entity has control over the timing and the amount this may suggest the third party is acting as its agent and that the payment or receipt is, in substance, the entity’s cash flow. • Why has the arrangement with the third party been established and who benefits from the arrangement? If the arrangement has been established for the convenience of the reporting entity (for example because they do not have a bank account in a certain jurisdiction) this may provide evidence that the cash flow is, in substance, the entity’s cash flow. How we see it Where it is determined that cash flows made or received by a third party are, in substance, cash flows of the entity, these cash flows must be reported in the statement of cash flows of the entity. Appropriate disclosures will be important, in particular, disclosure of the facts considered, and the judgements management has made in applying IAS 7 in accordance with paragraphs 117 and 122 of IAS 1 (see Chapter 3 at section 5.1.1.A). This issue is also discussed in the context of specific scenarios in section 4.4.5 above (factoring of trade receivables), section 4.4.6 above (supply-chain financing arrangements and reverse factoring arrangements), section 8.1 below (the in-substance purchase of an asset) and section 9.5.3 below (group treasury arrangements).
Statement of cash flows Foreign currency cash flows Foreign currency cash flows can arise either when an entity enters into transactions where the cash payments or receipts are denominated in a foreign currency or when the consolidated financial statements of a group include the cash flows of a subsidiary whose functional currency is different from the presentation currency of the group (e.g., a foreign subsidiary). IAS 21 The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates excludes from its scope the translation of cash flows of a foreign operation and the presentation of foreign currency cash flows in a statement of cash flows. [IAS 21.7]. Nevertheless, IAS 7 requires foreign currency cash flows to be reported in a manner consistent with IAS 21. [IAS 7.27]. The requirements for entities falling within the scope of IAS 29 Financial Reporting in Hyperinflationary Economies are discussed in Chapter 17. 6.1 Foreign currency denominated transactions When an entity enters into a transaction denominated in a currency other than its functional currency, there are no direct consequences for the statement of cash flows until payments are received or made. Once payment is made or received, the cash outflows or inflows are recorded in the entity’s accounting records at the exchange rate prevailing at the date of payment and these amounts are reflected in the statement of cash flows. [IAS 7.25]. If the entity’s presentation currency is different from its functional currency, the functional currency cash flows are translated into the presentation currency at the rates at the date of the cash flows. [IAS 7.26]. For practical reasons, an entity can apply a rate that approximates the actual rate on the date of the cash flow (such as a weighted average for a period) but, like the requirements of IAS 21 for income and expenses, translation using the exchange rate as at the end of the reporting period is not permitted. [IAS 7.27]. For operating cash flows presented using the direct method (see section 4.1.1 above), investing and financing cash flows, these requirements are relatively easy to understand, i.e., the amounts reported should be the cash paid or received, translated at the exchange rate prevailing at the date of the payment or receipt or a rate approximating that. For operating cash flows presented using the indirect method, there are additional complications which are considered below. 6.1.1 Operating cash flows presented using the indirect method For operating cash flows presented using the indirect method, the entity presents the net cash flows from operating activities by working back from amounts reported in the income statement (see section 4.1.2 above). The most common approach adjusts reported profit or loss for the effects of:
(a) Changes in inventories and operating receivables and payables during the period
(b) Non-cash items such as depreciation, provisions, deferred taxes, unrealised foreign currency gains and
losses, and undistributed profits of associates And
(c) All other items for which the cash effects are investing or financing cash flows [IAS 7.20]
The exchange differences reported in profit or loss may include realised and unrealised gains and losses arising from operating activities and investing and financing activities. Entities must therefore determine what adjustments to make to profit or loss to ensure that foreign currency items are only included in the operating cash flow reconciliation to the extent that cash flows have occurred and that they relate to operating activities. This is discussed in the sections below. 6.1.1.A Realised and unrealised gains and losses relating to operating activities For realised gains or losses that relate to operating items such as sales or purchases of inventory by an entity, no adjustment is needed to get from profit or loss to operating cash flows if the starting point for the reconciliation is profit before tax or profit after tax. This is because it will include both the amount recorded at the date of sale and the amount of the exchange difference on settlement, the combination of which gives the amount of the actual cash flow. For unrealised gains or losses that relate to operating items, no adjustment will be needed as the unrealised exchange gain or loss is generally already reflected in the movement in the related payable or receivable included in the reconciliation to operating cash flows. Alternatively, some entities may choose to reflect these gains and losses as a reconciling item and then also include an equivalent adjustment in the movement in the related receivable or payable, however this method is rarely seen in practice.
Statement of cash flows 6.1.1.B Realised and unrealised gains and losses relating to non-operating activities For realised gains or losses arising on a settled transaction relating to investing or financing activities, an adjustment is needed to reconcile reported profit or loss to the net cash flow from operating activities. For example, the foreign currency purchase of property, plant and equipment would be recorded at the rate prevailing on the date of the purchase and, if not paid for immediately, a creditor would be recorded for the same amount. Any exchange difference arising on final settlement of the creditor would be taken to profit or loss as an exchange gain or loss. If left unadjusted, operating cash flows determined using the indirect approach would include this amount of exchange gain or loss. In addition, the investing cash flow for the asset purchase would be unadjusted and recorded at the purchase date exchange rate, rather than at the exchange rate prevailing at the date of settlement of the creditor. Therefore, this difference needs to be considered in calculating the cash flow to be shown under the relevant classification, in this case investing cash flows, so that the cash flow for the purchase of the asset reflects the exchange rate at the date of settlement of the related creditor. The difference also needs to be adjusted by subtracting or adding back the exchange gain or loss in the reconciliation of operating cash flows. For unrealised gains or losses relating to investing or financing activities, an adjustment must be made to reconcile reported profit or loss to the net cash flow from operating activities. This is because the unrealised exchange differences will be reflected in the income statement as an exchange gain or loss. If left unadjusted, the reconciliation of operating cash flows prepared using the indirect approach would total to a figure for operating cash flows which would incorrectly include this amount of non-operating, unrealised exchange gain or loss. 6.2 Cash and cash equivalent balances denominated in foreign currencies Unrealised gains and losses arising from exchange rate movements on foreign currency cash and cash equivalents are not cash flows. However, it is necessary to include these exchange differences in the statement of cash flows in order to reconcile the movement in cash and cash equivalents at the beginning and end of the reporting period. The effect of exchange rate movements on cash and cash equivalents is presented as a single amount at the foot of the statement of cash flows, separately from operating, investing and financing cash flows and includes the differences, if any, had those cash flows been reported at end of period exchange rates. [IAS 7.28]. An example of this is illustrated in Practical example 4-1 in section 4 above. This reconciling item reflects: • The difference between the opening and closing exchange rate used multiplied by the foreign currency cash balances held at the beginning of the year. And • The difference between the exchange rates used to translate foreign currency cash flows (e.g., an average rate) and the closing exchange rate multiplied by the net cash flow activity for the period measured in the foreign currency. 6.3 Consolidated financial statements including foreign subsidiaries Section 9.1 below discusses the general principles of preparing a consolidated statement of cash flows. When the group contains a foreign subsidiary, the cash flows of that subsidiary must be translated into the presentation currency of the group using the exchange rates prevailing at the dates of the cash flows. [IAS 7.26]. This is similar to the treatment of foreign currency transactions discussed at section 6.1 above. A rate that approximates the actual rate on the date of the cash flow (such as a weighted average for a period) can be used. [IAS 7.27]. However, this does not apply to the cash flows of a foreign subsidiary whose functional currency is the currency of a hyperinflationary economy as all amounts, including cash flows, are translated at the closing rate at the date of the most recent statement of financial position. See Chapter 16 at section 6.1 for more discussion on translating amounts in a functional currency that is the currency of a hyperinflationary economy.
Statement of cash flows 6.3.1 The indirect method and foreign subsidiaries Entities should exercise care when applying the indirect method at the ‘more consolidated level’ as described in section 9.1 below when there are foreign subsidiaries. If the translated financial statements are used, exchange differences will be included in the movements between the opening and closing group balance sheets. For example, if inventories held by a US subsidiary of a UK parent increase from $240 to $270 during the year and the opening exchange rate of £1=$1.60 becomes £1=$1.80 by the year-end, inventories will be reported as an unchanged amount of £150 in both balance sheets. This could result in the reconciliation showing no movement between opening and closing inventories which would be incorrect. In these circumstances it would be advisable for an entity to take the functional currency financial statements of the foreign subsidiary as the starting point, or otherwise ensure the exchange differences have been correctly reflected. 6.3.2 Exchange differences on retranslation of foreign subsidiaries reported in other comprehensive income. All exchange differences arising on translation of foreign operations will have been recognised in other comprehensive income - see Chapter 16 at section 6.1. Such exchange differences are not cash flows, and will therefore not be reported as such in the consolidated statement of cash flows. However, any exchange differences relating to the translation of cash and cash equivalents held in foreign currencies by the foreign subsidiaries will be included in the reconciliation of cash and cash equivalents at the beginning and end of the period, as reported at the foot of the statements of cash flows - see section 6.2 above. [IFRS 15.60]. Statement of cash flows 7.4 Variable and contingent consideration for asset acquisitions outside of a business combination Contingent consideration paid on a business combination is discussed in section 9.3.3 below. The purchase price of intangible assets or tangible assets acquired outside of a business combination also sometimes includes variable or contingent consideration for example to resolve an uncertainty or a difference in views about the initial value of the asset transferred. Accounting for the contingent consideration element of such asset purchases is discussed in Chapter 18 at section 4.5 for intangible assets, and Chapter 19 at section 4.1.9 for property, plant and equipment. As explained in those chapters, such transactions can be very complex and payment can be dependent on a number of factors, with no specific guidance provided in IAS 38 Intangible Assets or IAS 16. Therefore, an entity must develop and consistently apply an accounting policy for the initial and subsequent measurement of such assets that results in information that is relevant and reliable in its particular circumstances. Once such an accounting policy is developed, the presentation in the statement of cash flows should be informed by the accounting treatment adopted in the statement of financial position. As discussed in section 7.1 above, even though the reason for the cash flows in a genuine contingent consideration is to acquire a long-term asset, classifying the resulting cash flows as investing would only be appropriate if the cash flows are in settlement of an expenditure that results in a recognised asset in the statement of financial position. [IAS 7.16]. This is because paragraph 16 of the standard requires the classification of cash flows from investing activities in the statement of cash flows to be aligned with the presentation of recognised assets in the statement of financial position rather than to reflect an entity’s judgement as to the most appropriate classification based the entity’s business and the definitions of investing activities. [IAS 7.BC5, IAS 7.BC6, IAS 7.BC7]. How we see it In our view, cash payments up to the amount capitalised in relation to the acquired assets would be classified in investing activities. Since variable and contingent consideration generally arises for reasons other than the provision of finance to the acquirer, any resulting payments would generally not seem to be eligible for classification as financing cash flows. As a result, any variable and contingent consideration in excess of the amount that has been capitalised as part of the cost of the asset (whether on initial recognition or to reflect changes in variable and contingent consideration that have been capitalised in line with the entity’s accounting policy for such payments), would need to be presented as operating cash flows by default. However, if the accounting variability of the contingent consideration is driven from factors such as currency, time value of money or the existence of an embedded derivative that are within the scope of other standards (e.g., IAS 21, IFRS 9), the cash flow presentation must be consistent with such standards.

The requirements of IFRS 16 are covered in Chapter 24. The presentation and disclosure requirements of IFRS 16 for lessees are discussed in Chapter 24 at sections 5.7 and 5.8. In relation to the cash flow presentation, a lessee classifies:
(a) Cash payments for the principal portion of the lease liability within financing activities.
(b) Cash payments for the interest portion of the lease liability in a consistent manner to the presentation
other interest paid (i.e., as operating or financing - see section 4.4.1 above). And
(c) Short-term lease payments, payments for leases of low-value assets and variable lease payments not
included in the measurement of the lease liability within operating activities. [IFRS 16.50]. IFRS 16 also requires disclosure of the total cash outflow for leases. [IFRS 16.53(g)]. This will include lease payments for short-term leases and leases of low-value assets and variable lease payments. See Chapter 24 at section 5.8. A lessee must also include lease liabilities (and the related cash and non-cash movements) in the disclosures required by IAS 7.44A of changes in liabilities arising from financing activities - see section 11.2 below. However, care may be needed sometimes to distinguish a lease from a purchase of an asset based on the substance of that transaction and not its legal form. IFRS 16 applies to contracts that convey the right to use an underlying asset for a period of time and does not apply to transactions that transfer control of the underlying asset to an entity - such transactions are sales or purchases within the scope of other standards. [IFRS 16.BC139, IFRS 16.BC140]. Consequently, if a contract grants rights that represent the in-substance purchase of an item of property, plant and equipment, such transaction may need to be presented as the purchase of the underlying asset (regardless of whether legal title transfers) either on deferred terms (see section 7.1 above) if entered into directly with the manufacturer or dealer of the asset or together with the provision of financing if entered into with a financial institution which purchases the underlying asset on the entity’s behalf from a designated supplier. In the latter case, this raises the question as to whether the payment of the supplier directly by the financial institution will be treated as a non-cash transaction or as a transaction in which the financial institution is acting as a paying agent for the entity. In this case, the entity would report an investing cash outflow to the supplier and a financing cash inflow from the financial institution upon taking control of the underlying asset and subsequent financing cash outflows when the liability is settled in future periods. In all cases, determining whether another party is acting as agent for the entity is key to whether the cash flows are presented in the statement of cash flows or whether the transaction is disclosed as a non-cash transaction. In all these situations, the nature of the relationship with the third party will need careful examination to ensure that the entity complies with the requirement in IAS 7 that “investing and financing transactions that do not require the use of cash or cash equivalents are excluded from an entity’s statement of cash flows”. [IAS 7.43]. Factors to consider in making this determination are discussed in section 5.3 above. 8.2 Payments made by the lessee before commencement date Where a lessee makes a lease payment to the lessor at or before the commencement date, this payment is included in the initial measurement of the right-of-use asset (see Chapter 24 at section 5.2.1). The nature of the activity giving rise to this cash outflow must be considered to determine how it is presented in the statement of cash flows. For example, if the payment represents all or substantially all of the total lease payments to be made over the lease term, the lessee classifies the cash outflow as an investing activity, being the acquisition of the right of use asset. [IAS 7.16]. In contrast, if the payment is only the first of the periodic lease payments that the lessee will make and is financing in nature, being a payment for the reduction of the lease liability, the cash outflow would then be classified as a financing activity. [IAS 7.17(e)].
Statement of cash flows 8.3 Lease incentives Lease incentives are defined in IFRS 16 as “payments made by a lessor to a lessee associated with a lease, or the reimbursement or assumption by a lessor of costs of a lessee” and are discussed in Chapter 24 at section 4.5.2. [IFRS 16 Appendix A]. Neither IFRS 16 nor IAS 7 provide specific guidance on the presentation of the cash inflows from lease incentives. It is however clear that IAS 7 considers a lease transaction to be essentially the provision of finance by the lessor to the lessee. [IAS 7.17(e)]. IFRS 16 defines lease payments as “Payments made by a lessee to a lessor relating to the right to use an underlying asset during the lease term … less any lease incentives”. [IFRS 16 Appendix A]. Therefore, lease incentives are clearly a reduction of the payments made to the lessor as repayment for the financing provided. How we see it Considering this definition of lease payments in the context of IAS 7, in our view, the appropriate presentation of cash flows from lease incentives would be to present the corresponding cash flows as part of financing activities in the statement of cash flows, regardless of whether they are received after the commencement date of the lease and are deducted from the lease liability or before the lease commencement date. Where lease incentives are received in the same period that lease payments are made, these cash inflows and outflows must, where material, be separately presented and not netted. See section 5.2 above. 8.4 Sale and leaseback transactions A sale and leaseback transaction involves the transfer of an asset by an entity (the seller-lessee) to another entity (the buyer-lessor) and the leaseback of the same asset by the seller-lessee. Sale and leaseback transactions are discussed in Chapter 24 at section 8. In terms of presentation in the statement of cash flows, IFRS 16 refers only to leases, requiring the cash payments for the principal portion of the lease liability to be presented in financing activities and cash payments for the interest portion to be presented in in a consistent manner to the presentation other interest paid - see section 8.1 above. [IFRS 16.50]. Nothing is included in IFRS 16 or IAS 7 with respect to cash flow presentation in the case of a sale and leaseback transaction. Presentation of the cash flows relating to the sale and leaseback transaction for a seller-lessee will depend on how the transaction is accounted for under IFRS 16, specifically whether the transfer of the asset is accounted for as a sale of the asset in accordance with the requirements of IFRS 15. [IFRS 16.99]. If the transaction does not satisfy the requirements of IFRS 15 to be accounted for as a sale (i.e., not a sale and leaseback transaction), the cash received is classified in the cash flow statement as a cash inflow from financing activities because the substance of the transaction is that the lessee has raised finance, with the asset provided as security. The resulting financial liability is accounted for under IFRS 9, [IFRS 16.103], and the seller-lessee splits the cash outflows between repayment of the principal and payment of interest. Repayments of the principal are classified as financing cash flows, whereas interest must be presented in a manner consistent with the presentation of other interest paid in statement of cash flows (see section 4.4.1 above). [IFRS 16.50, IFRS 16.BC211]. If the transaction does satisfy the requirements of IFRS 15 to be accounted for as a sale, the analysis is more complex, as the cash received comprises an amount reflecting the fair value of the underlying asset plus the amount of any additional financing provided by the buyer-lessor to the seller-lessee. [IFRS 16.101]. This will be reflected in the statements of cash flows by splitting the proceeds between investing cash flows and financing cash flows. However, there are different approaches as to how this split could be determined. From an economic standpoint, the seller-lessee has sold only its interest in the value of the underlying asset at the end of the leaseback - it has retained its right to use the asset for the duration of the leaseback. Therefore, the amount that would be shown as an investing cash inflow must reflect this.
Statement of cash flows How we see it In our view there are two acceptable approaches for determining the split between investing cash flows and financing cash flows. One approach is to present the investing cash inflow as the amount of the sale proceeds that reflects the proportion of the fair value of the underlying asset that is not retained by the seller-lessee. Any excess would be presented as part of cash flows from financing activities. This presentation in the statement of cash flows would be consistent with the requirement in IFRS 16 to recognise only the amount of any gain or loss on the sale and leaseback relating to the rights that have been transferred to the buyer-lessor and also to measure the right-of-use asset as a proportion of the previous carrying amount of the underlying asset. Alternatively, the seller-lessee may also present any excess of the sale proceeds over the fair value of the asset as financing cash inflows and the remaining proceeds as investing cash inflows. Illustration 8-1: Sale and leaseback transaction: presentation of cash flows An entity (Seller-lessee) sells a building to another entity (Buyer-lessor) for cash of CU2,000,000. At the same time, Seller-lessee enters into a contract with Buyer-lessor for the right to use the building for 18 years, with annual payments of CU120,000 payable at the end of each year. Accordingly, Seller-lessee and Buyer-lessor account for the transaction as a sale and leaseback. This example ignores any initial direct costs. An alternative approach in this example, could be to treat only the excess of any sale proceeds above the fair value of the underlying asset of CU200,000 (i.e., CU2,000,000 - CU1,800,000) as financing cash flows with CU1,800,000 presented within investing activities. 8.5 Accounting as lessor For lessors, IFRS 16 is silent on the presentation of cash flows arising from their leasing activities. However, since lessor accounting under IFRS 16 is substantially unchanged from the accounting under the predecessor standard, IAS 17 Leases, in practice, the presentation of cash flows for lessors should be established and well understood. In summary, the cash inflows from operating leases will form part of the cash flows from operating activities. [IAS 7.14]. The cash inflows from finance leases will follow the treatment set out in section 7.2 above in relation to assets disposed of on deferred terms.
Statement of cash flows Additional IAS 7 considerations for groups IAS 7 does not distinguish between single entities and groups, and there are no specific requirements as to how an entity prepares a consolidated statement of cash flows. In the absence of specific requirements, cash inflows and outflows would be treated in the same way as income and expenses under IFRS 10 Consolidated Financial Statements. Applying these principles, the statement of cash flows presented in consolidated financial statements will reflect only the flows of cash and cash equivalents into and out of the group, i.e., consolidated cash flows are presented as those of a single economic entity. [IFRS 10 Appendix A]. On the same basis, the cash flows of a consolidated subsidiary must be included in the consolidated statement of cash flows for the same period as its results are reported in the consolidated statement of comprehensive income, i.e., from the date the group gains control until the date it loses control. [IFRS 10.B88]. Cash flows that are internal to the group (such as payments and receipts for intra-group sales, management charges, dividends, interest and financing arrangements) must be eliminated. [IFRS 10.B86]. However, transactions with non-controlling interests as well as with associates, joint ventures and unconsolidated subsidiaries would not be eliminated and are discussed in greater detail below. 9.1 Preparing a consolidated statement of cash flows In principle, the group statement of cash flows will be compiled from those prepared by individual subsidiaries with intra-group cash flows being eliminated as part of the aggregation process. This would generally be the case for entities presenting operating cash flows under the direct method, where information on gross cash receipts and payments has been obtained from each group entity’s accounting records. In practice, however, it may be possible to prepare a statement of cash flows at a more consolidated level, by starting with the disclosures in the consolidated statement of comprehensive income and statement of financial position and then applying the adjustments reflected as part of the financial statement consolidation process, together with information provided on external cash flows by individual subsidiaries. Thus, an entity adopting the direct method could use this information to derive the value of the major classes of gross cash receipts and gross cash payments. [IAS 7.19]. An entity presenting operating cash flows under the indirect method would use this information to calculate the values for movements in inventories, operating receivables and payables and other non-cash items that appear in the reconciliation of consolidated profit or loss to the group’s cash flow from operating activities. [IAS 7.20]. As noted in section 6.3 above, groups with foreign subsidiaries will need to take extra care when using this method to ensure exchange differences are treated appropriately. Cash flows from investing and financing activities could similarly be derived from a reconciliation of the relevant headings in the consolidated statement of comprehensive income to statement of financial position movements. However, for this to be possible, subsidiaries would have to provide supplementary information (as part of internal group reporting) to prevent gross cash flows from being netted off and to ensure that the cash flows are shown under the correct classifications. In particular, detailed information about receivables and payables would be essential to ensure that the movements in operating, investing and financing receivables and payables are identified. 9.2 Transactions with non-controlling interests Dividends paid to non-controlling interest holders in subsidiaries are included under cash flows from financing activities or operating activities, in accordance with the entity’s determined policy for dividends paid (see section 4.4.1 above). IFRS 10 requires entities to distinguish between transactions that give rise to a change in control and those that do not, because a transaction when there is no change in control is effectively one with the owners in their capacity as owners. [IFRS 10.BCZ168]. Changes in ownership interests in a subsidiary that do not result in a loss of control are therefore accounted for as equity transactions, and the resulting cash flows are classified in the same way as other transactions with owners. [IAS 7.42B]. Accordingly, IAS 7 requires cash flows arising from changes in ownership interests in a subsidiary that occur after control is obtained, but do not give rise to a loss of control to be classified as cash flows from financing activities. [IAS 7.42A].

Statement of cash flows 9.3.1 Acquisition-related costs IFRS 3 Business Combinations requires acquisition-related costs (other than those costs relating to the issue of equity or debt securities) to be recognised as an expense in the period in which the costs are incurred and the services are received. [IFRS 3.53]. As discussed in section 4.2 above, the definition of investing activities in IAS 7, states that “only expenditures that result in a recognised asset in the statement of financial position” give rise to investing cash flows. [IAS 7.16]. As a result, cash flows relating to acquisition costs recognised as an expense must be classified within operating activities. 9.3.2 Deferred and other non-cash consideration Not all acquisitions or disposals of businesses are satisfied in full by the exchange of cash. Any non-cash consideration, such as shares issued by either party or amounts to be paid or received by the entity at a later date, is not included in the amount presented under investing activities. [IAS 7.43]. Instead, the non-cash element of the acquisition or disposal is disclosed. As explained in more detail in section 7.1 above, the purchase of assets on deferred terms can be a complicated area because it may not be clear whether to classify the associated cash flows within investing activities or within financing activities, as the repayment of borrowings. Hence, judgement is required when determining the appropriate classification of the deferred payment in the statement of cash flows. These same principles apply on the acquisition of a business involving deferred consideration. 9.3.3 Contingent consideration paid on business combinations When a business combination agreement allows for adjustments to the cost of the combination that are contingent on one or more future events, IFRS 3 requires the acquirer to recognise the acquisition-date fair value of the contingent consideration, [IFRS 3.39], and classify an obligation to pay the contingent consideration as a liability or as equity in accordance with the provisions of IAS 32. [IFRS 3.40]. Changes resulting from events after the acquisition date, such as meeting a performance target, are not reflected by adjusting the recorded cost of the business combination. Where a liability has been recognised for the contingent consideration payable, any subsequent payment in excess of or below the carrying amount of the liability is recognised in profit or loss. [IFRS 3.58]. The primary principle for the classification of cash flows is the nature of the activity giving rise to the cash flow, according to the definitions of operating, investing and financing activities in the standard. This might imply that all payments relating to a business combination must be classified as investing cash flows. However, as discussed in section 4.2 above, the Board added a requirement in IAS 7 that only expenditures that result in a recognised asset are eligible for classification as investing activities. [IAS 7.16]. How we see it In our view, cash payments up to the amount recognised for the acquisition-date fair value of the contingent consideration (and thereby included in the carrying value of the acquired assets including goodwill) would be classified in investing activities. Since contingent consideration generally arises for reasons other than the provision of finance to the acquirer, any resulting payments would generally not seem to be eligible for classification as financing cash flows. As a result, any contingent consideration in excess of the amount that was recorded on the acquisition date, would need to be presented as operating cash flows by default. 9.3.4 Contingent consideration conditional on continuing employment As discussed in Chapter 10 at section 11.2, a transaction that remunerates employees or former owners of the acquiree for future services is excluded from the business combination accounting and accounted for separately. [IFRS 3.52]. Therefore, cash flows relating to payment of purchase consideration that is conditional on the continuing employment of the vendors in the business or the existing employees of the business must be classified within operating activities and not within investing activities. This faithfully reflects the fact that these payments are not part of the consideration transferred on a business combination but are remuneration for post-combination services received. [IFRS 3.B55(a)].
Statement of cash flows 9.3.5 Contingent consideration received on disposals When an entity disposes of a subsidiary, it presents the cash flows separately within investing activities. This will be the case even for the receipt of contingent consideration because the cash inflow is still an investing cash flow being the receipt from the sale of equity or debt instruments of another entity (see section 4.2 above). [IAS 7.16]. 9.3.6 Settlement of amounts owed by the acquired entity A question that sometimes arises is how to treat a payment made by the acquirer, in the consolidated statement of cash flows, to settle amounts owed by a newly acquired subsidiary. If the repayment is made before the acquisition date, the acquiree is not yet part of the group controlled by the acquirer and the debt will not be included in the consolidated balance sheet. Therefore, any payment to settle that debt represents a payment made to acquire the debt instrument of another entity and this would be included under investing activities. [IAS 7.16]. If the repayment is made at or after the acquisition date, it may be more challenging to determine whether the cash outflow should be presented as investing or financing. The repayment may be negotiated with the vendor as a condition of the acquisition agreement or may be triggered by a pre-existing change of control clause in the agreement between the acquired entity and the lender. Alternatively, the payment may be made after the acquisition date and not negotiated and agreed as part of the business combination. IAS 7 defines investing activities as “the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents” and financing activities as “activities that result in changes in the size and composition of the contributed equity and borrowings of the entity”. [IAS 7.6]. As discussed in Section 4 above, the primary principle for classification of cash flows should be in accordance with the nature of the activity in a manner that is most appropriate to the business of the entity. [IAS 7.11]. Therefore, depending on the facts and circumstances associated with repayment, the cash outflow may represent either an investing activity or a financing activity in the consolidated cash flow statement of the acquirer. How we see it Judgement will be needed, based on consideration of all the specific facts and circumstances associated with the payment, to determine whether the cash outflow is one that is investing in nature or financing in nature. In our view, in determining the nature of activities associated with the cash outflow, it is important to consider who is requiring or directing the repayment. If the acquirer is directing the movement of the cash because it has made a choice to repay the external borrowings, perhaps because it wants the acquired entity to be debt free or wishes to arrange financing with its own lenders, then it is more likely that the transaction will be financing in nature. However, if the acquirer is forced to repay the borrowings, for example by a pre-existing change of control clause that the external lender has chosen to exercise, then the payment has been triggered by the business combination and is more likely to be investing in nature. It should be noted that the inclusion of a clause in the acquisition agreement requiring the repayment of the loan by the acquirer is not, in itself, justification for presenting the cash outflow as investing. Although this could be viewed as providing the acquirer with no discretion as to whether it makes the repayment, the acquirer would have been able to influence the content of the acquisition agreement and hence, may have had some choice as to whether this clause was included. In this case, other factors would need to be considered before a conclusion is reached that the cash flow is investing in nature. Clear disclosure of the presentation treatment adopted in the statement of cash flows will be important, in particular, disclosure of the facts considered, and the judgements management has made in determining the nature of the cash flow, in accordance with paragraphs 117 and 122 of IAS 1 (see Chapter 3 at section 5.1.1.A). Other factors that may provide supporting evidence as to the nature of the repayment include: • How close is the timing of the repayment to the date of the acquisition and what is the reason for any difference in timing? The longer the period of time elapsed between the acquisition date and the date of repayment, the more likely it is that the payment is financing in nature. However, it will also be necessary to consider the reason for any significant delay. For example, if the timing of the payment is out of the control of the acquirer, such as where this is dictated by the bank through a change of control clause, a difference in timing may not always be indicative of the payment being financing in nature.
Statement of cash flows • Why is the payment being made by the acquirer directly to the lender rather to the vendor or the acquiree who will then repay the borrowings? Is it for the convenience of the vendor or for the convenience of the acquirer? If the arrangement has been established purely for the convenience of the acquirer so that they do not need to transfer the cash to the acquiree to repay the borrowings after acquisition, then this suggests that this is part of the financing activities of the group. However, if the vendor has requested that this amount negotiated as part of the acquisition cost be paid directly by the acquirer to the bank, rather than to the vendor who will then repay the borrowings, it is more likely that the cash outflow is investing in nature. If it is determined that the cash outflow is financing in nature, being the repayment of the borrowings of the acquired entity which is now part of the group, using funds provided by the parent, this is presented as a cash outflow from financing activities in the consolidated statement of cash flows. [IAS 7.17]. If it is determined that the cash outflow is investing in nature because it is part of the payment to acquire control of the acquired entity, then it is presented as an investing cash outflow. Care should be taken when considering whether the payment is part of the consideration for the business combination under IFRS 3 as the payment is not made to the vendor. This is because IFRS 3 paragraph 51 explains that amounts that are not part of what the acquirer and vendor exchanged in the business combination, i.e., amounts that are not part of the exchange for the acquiree, must be accounted for as separate transactions (see Chapter 10 at section 11). 9.3.7 Settlement of intra-group balances on a demerger A similarly fine distinction might apply on the demerger of subsidiaries. These sometimes involve the repayment of intra-group indebtedness out of the proceeds from external finance raised by the demerged subsidiary. If the external funding is raised immediately prior to the subsidiary leaving the group, it is strictly a financing inflow in the consolidated statement of cash flows, being cash proceeds from issuing short or long-term borrowings. [IAS 7.17]. If the subsidiary both raises the external funding and repays the intra-group debt after the demerger, the inflow is shown in the consolidated statement of cash flows under investing activities, being a cash receipt from the repayment of advances and loans made to other parties.

9.3.8 Acquisition of a business that is not a subsidiary A business combination may be enacted through the acquisition of a subsidiary (i.e., a business held in a separate legal entity) or through the acquisition of a group of assets and liabilities that constitute a business (sometimes referred to as ‘a purchase of net assets and trade’). Paragraphs 39 and 40 of IAS 7 refer to “obtaining or losing control of subsidiaries or other businesses” and hence are applicable to both types of business combination. Therefore, the cash flows arising on the acquisition and disposal of a group of assets and liabilities that constitute a business would be classified as investing activities. In addition, the considerations set out in sections 9.3.1 to 9.3.7 above in respect of deferred and contingent consideration, acquisition costs and settlement of amounts must also be applied. The disclosure requirements in IAS 7.40 would also be required. 9.4 Cash flows of associates, joint arrangements and investment entities 9.4.1 Investments in associates and joint ventures Changes in cash and cash equivalents relating to associates or joint ventures accounted for under the equity or cost method will be reflected in the entity’s statement of cash flows only to the extent of the cash flows between the group and the investee. [IAS 7.37]. The same concept would apply to associates or joint ventures carried at fair value as allowed by IAS 28 Investments in Associates and Joint Ventures (discussed in Chapter 12). Examples include cash dividends received and loans advanced or repaid. [IAS 7.37]. Cash flows in respect of an entity’s investment in an equity accounted associate or joint venture would also be presented. [IAS 7.38].
Statement of cash flows Cash dividends received from equity accounted associates and joint ventures would be classified as operating or investing activities in accordance with the entity’s determined policy for other dividends received (see section 4.4.1 above). Loans advanced or repaid would be classified as investing activities. [IAS 7.16]. Where the net cash inflow from operating activities is determined using the indirect method, the group’s share of profits or losses from equity-accounted investments will appear as a non-cash reconciling item in the cash flow statement (see section 4.1.2 above). The cash flows arising on the acquisition and disposal of an associate or a joint venture would be classified as investing activities. In addition, the considerations set out in section 9.3 above in respect of deferred and contingent consideration on acquisition and disposal of a subsidiary must also be applied when determining the appropriate classifications of cash flows relating to the acquisition and disposal of associates and joint ventures. 9.4.2 Cash flows of joint operations IAS 7 does not specifically deal with the treatment of the cash flows of joint operations. However, following the principles in IFRS 11 Joint Arrangements that all transactions shall be reflected in the accounts of the joint operator’s financial results to the extent of its interests in those transactions, [IFRS 11.20], the cash flows of the joint arrangement are already included in the operator’s financial statements and no additional adjustments are required to reflect the activities of the joint operation. The treatment of cash flows for the acquisition and disposal of a joint operation is less clear as there is an argument for presentation either as a single net cash flow in investing activities (as is required for the cost of a business combination, discussed in section 9.3 above); or as separate cash flows, classified according to the nature of the underlying assets and liabilities acquired. IFRS 11 requires an entity to determine whether the activity undertaken by a joint operation constitutes a business as defined in IFRS 3 and to apply business combination or asset acquisition accounting in accordance with that analysis. [IFRS 11.21A]. How we see it In our view, an entity presents the cash flows for the acquisition and disposal of a joint operation consistently with the treatment of the joint operation as either a business or a group of assets and liabilities as required by IFRS 11.21A. Therefore, acquisitions and disposals of operations meeting the definition of a business would give rise to a single investing cash flow, and acquisitions and disposals of operations not regarded as a business would give rise to cash flows presented according to the nature of the assets and liabilities acquired or disposed. 9.4.3 Cash flows in investment entities IFRS 10 includes an exception to the consolidation principle for a parent that meets the definition of an investment entity. An investment entity measures its investments in subsidiaries, other than those solely providing services that relate to its investment activities, at fair value through profit or loss in accordance with IFRS 9 instead of consolidating those subsidiaries. For example, where a parent provides a loan to a subsidiary, an associate or a joint venture, it will be necessary to consider whether the cash outflow (and the subsequent cash inflow when the loan is repaid) is presented as part of operating activities or investing activities in the separate financial statements of the parent. [IAS 7.13, IAS 7.16]. Conversely, where a subsidiary, an associate or a joint venture provides a loan to the parent, this is much more clearly part of financing activities of the parent. [IAS 7.17]. See sections 4.1 to 4.3 above for details of the definitions of operating, investing and financing activities in IAS 7. 9.5.2 Group cash pooling and cash sharing arrangements Many groups use some form of cash pooling or cash sharing arrangement to manage the group’s cash position on a consolidated basis, effectively concentrating the group’s cash in one place. Such arrangements seek to maximise the return for the group as a whole on its cash, minimise the cost of funding and give visibility to the group of its combined cash and currency position. The terms and conditions of such arrangements vary, and it is important to understand the specific nature of the pooling or sharing arrangement in place, in order to determine the appropriate accounting treatment. In sections 9.5.3 and 9.5.4 below we discuss three of the most common types of arrangements. 9.5.3 Group treasury arrangements Some groups adopt treasury arrangements under which cash resources are held centrally, either by the parent company or by a designated subsidiary company. Any excess cash is transferred to the central bank account which is in the name of the designated ‘treasury entity’. In some cases, each individual group company might not even have its own bank account, with all receipts and payments being made directly from the centrally controlled funds. Each individual group company will need to consider how to present the funds transferred to the treasury entity at the period end; as cash, as cash equivalents or as a short-term intercompany receivable, by examining the specific facts and circumstances of each arrangement with reference to the definitions of cash and of cash equivalents as discussed in sections 3.1 and 3.2 above. In order to meet the definition of cash, the individual company must be able to withdraw the funds from the treasury entity on demand without prior notice being required and without penalty. To meet the definition of cash equivalents, the funds must be held by the individual company for the purpose of meeting short- term cash commitments and be a short-term highly liquid investment that is readily convertible into known amounts of cash and subject to insignificant risk of changes in value. [IAS 7.6, IAS 7.7]. In relation to the definitions of both cash and cash equivalents, one important factor to consider is the liquidity of the treasury entity holding the cash on behalf of the group companies and of the group as a whole. Clearly, if the treasury entity is limited in its ability to “release” cash to other group companies due to liquidity constraints, it will be difficult to justify that the other group companies have a balance that is available for withdrawal on demand (as in the definition of cash) or “highly liquid” (as required by the definition of cash equivalents). [IAS 7.6].
Statement of cash flows When looking at the definition of cash equivalents (see section 3.2 above), the funds deposited also need to be subject to an insignificant risk of change in value. [IAS 7.7]. As explained in section 3.2.2 above, this means that the entity must conclude that the range of possible returns is very small. Therefore, the use of the funds by the treasury entity (e.g., the nature and diversification of the investments made by the treasury entity) as well as the credit risk of the treasury entity and the group as a whole, will be important facts to consider. A further question arises as to whether the subsidiary must prepare a statement of cash flows if there is no cash or cash equivalents balance held at each period end and, in some cases, at any time during the year. How we see it In our view, the preparation of the statement of cash flows will be based upon the actual cash flows during the period regardless of cash and cash equivalents balance held directly by the entity. Where no cash flows through an entity, but rather all transactions flow through the group treasury function acting on behalf of the entity, the entity would still record receipts from debtors and payments to suppliers, albeit with an associated deposit to or withdrawal from a balance with another group company. Just as a bank processes payments and receipts as agent for the account holder, so the group treasury function is acting as agent for the entity, and these transactions will be reflected in a statement of cash flows. For guidance to assist in the determination of whether another entity is acting on behalf of the reporting entity, as the entity’s agent, see section 5.3 above. Where the subsidiary makes net deposits of funds to, or net withdrawals of funds from the designated group entity during the reporting period, a further question arises as to how to present these movements in the subsidiary’s statement of cash flows. As explained above, these transactions normally give rise to intercompany balances. Therefore, the deposits or withdrawals must be evaluated against the definitions of the categories of cash flows and presented as either operating activities or alternatively as investing or financing activities, as appropriate. Further consideration may be made as to whether these cash flows meet the criteria for net presentation as discussed in section 5.2 above. 9.5.4 Cash pooling Groups often use what are commonly known as cash pooling arrangements. Typically, these will involve a number of subsidiaries within a group each having a legally separate bank account with the same bank, but these accounts are managed, with the agreement of the bank, on a consolidated basis. For example, interest will normally be determined on a notional basis using the net balance of all accounts; similarly, any overdraft limit will normally apply to the net balance. Typical characteristics of notional cash pooling and physical cash pooling are discussed below however arrangements vary. Therefore, terms and conditions of each arrangement must be reviewed carefully to ensure that the accounting treatment adopted is appropriate for that specific arrangement. 9.5.4.A Notional cash pooling Notional cash pooling arrangements allow the group to net off the balances of different accounts across jurisdictions, without physically moving the cash to a header or master account, but with each individual entity continuing to have access to the cash it has deposited in its individual account, sometimes called a ‘memo’ or ‘mirror’ account. This is, in essence, an interest rate adjustment, and the ownership of the cash does not change. In such cases, therefore, each entity will continue to report its cash as ‘cash and cash equivalents’. 9.5.4.B Physical cash pooling Typically, in these arrangements, which are also known as zero balancing, cash concentration or physical cash sweeping, the group physically sweeps excess cash out of the individual bank accounts to a ’header account’ on a daily basis. In these arrangements, the ownership of the cash changes: the relationship moves from a deposit with a third-party bank to a loan to the group entity that is the holder of the ’header account’. As in the group treasury arrangements discussed in section 9.5.3 above, the individual companies will need to consider whether to present these balances at the period end as cash, as cash equivalents or as a short-term intercompany receivable. ### Operating cash flows
Cash advances and loans made by financial institutions are usually classified as operating activities (and not as investing activities, as they are for other entities) since they relate to a financial institution’s main revenue-producing activity. [IAS 7.15, IAS 7.16(e)]. Similarly, receipts from the repayment of loans and advances would be included in operating cash flows. [IAS 7.16(f)]. ### Supplier finance arrangements
In May 2023, the IASB published Supplier Finance Arrangements - Amendments to IAS 7 and IFRS 7 to require an entity to provide additional disclosures about its supplier finance arrangements. The amendments are effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024. The disclosure requirements in the amendments are intended to enable users of financial statements to assess the effects of supplier finance arrangements on an entity’s liabilities, cash flows and exposure to liquidity risk. [IAS 7.44F]. The disclosures aim to enable users to understand how the entity might be affected if the arrangements were no longer available to it [IAS 7.BC29(b)]. The disclosure requirements in the amendments do not apply to an entity’s arrangements that finance receivables or inventory. Also, an entity is not required to identify other actions its suppliers might have taken to finance their receivables (for example, factoring of receivables) [IAS 7.BC32(c)]. The scope of the disclosure requirements is discussed in section 11.4.1 below. The amendments clarify the characteristics of supplier finance arrangements. Extract from IAS 7 44G Supplier finance arrangements are characterised by one or more finance providers offering to pay amounts an entity owes its suppliers and the entity agreeing to pay according to the terms and conditions of the arrangements at the same date as, or a date later than, suppliers are paid. These arrangements provide the entity with extended payment terms, or the entity’s suppliers with early payment terms, compared to the related invoice payment due date. Supplier finance arrangements are often referred to as supply chain finance, payables finance or reverse factoring arrangements. Arrangements that are solely credit enhancements for the entity (for example, financial guarantees including letters of credit used as guarantees) or instruments used by the entity to settle directly with a supplier the amounts owed (for example, credit cards) are not supplier finance arrangements.
**[IAS 7.44G].**
Statement of cash flows The amendments introduce a number of specific disclosure requirements, covering the terms and conditions of the arrangements, information about the carrying amounts associated with the arrangements and details of the non-cash changes in the carrying amounts. [IAS 7.44H]. Extract from IAS 7 44H To meet the objectives in paragraph 44F, an entity shall disclose in aggregate for its supplier finance arrangements: (a) the terms and conditions of the arrangements (for example, extended payment terms and security or guarantees provided). However, an entity shall disclose separately the terms and conditions of arrangements that have dissimilar terms and conditions. (b) as at the beginning and end of the reporting period: (i) the carrying amounts, and associated line items presented in the entity’s statement of financial position, of the financial liabilities that are part of a supplier finance arrangement. (ii) the carrying amounts, and associated line items, of the financial liabilities disclosed under (i) for which suppliers have already received payment from the finance providers. (iii) the range of payment due dates (for example, 30-40 days after the invoice date) for both the financial liabilities disclosed under (i) and comparable trade payables that are not part of a supplier finance arrangement. Comparable trade payables are, for example, trade payables of the entity within the same line of business or jurisdiction as the financial liabilities disclosed under (i). If ranges of payment due dates are wide, an entity shall disclose explanatory information about those ranges or disclose additional ranges (for example, stratified ranges). (c) the type and effect of non-cash changes in the carrying amounts of the financial liabilities disclosed under (b)(i). Examples of non-cash changes include the effect of business combinations, exchange differences or other transactions that do not require the use of cash or cash equivalents (see paragraph 43) The amendments require an entity to aggregate information about its supplier finance arrangements. This is because, in most cases, aggregated information about an entity’s supplier finance arrangements will satisfy the information needs of users of financial statements. [IAS 7.BC36]. However, an entity must disaggregate information about unusual or unique terms and conditions of individual arrangements when they are dissimilar. [IAS 7.44H]. Explanatory information about payment due dates, when those payment due date ranges are wide, must also be disaggregated. In considering whether, and if so, how to aggregate (or disaggregate) this information, the IASB noted that paragraphs 29 -31 of IAS 1 are relevant when making this assessment. [IAS 7.BC38]. Accordingly, an entity must ensure it provides an appropriate level of disaggregation of information to avoid obscuring material information. The requirements of IAS 1 in respect of materiality and aggregation are discussed in Chapter 3 at section 4.1.5. Each of the required disclosures is discussed in turn from section 11.4.2 below. 11.4.1 Scope of the disclosure requirements Paragraph 44G of IAS 7 explains that supplier finance arrangements are characterised by one or more finance providers offering to pay amounts an entity owes its suppliers and the entity agreeing to pay according to the terms and conditions of the arrangements at the same date as, or a date later than, suppliers are paid. [IAS 7.44G]. While this paragraph clarifies the characteristics of supplier finance arrangements, it does not define which arrangements are within the scope of the disclosure requirements. Statement of cash flows Appendix B - Defined terms Extract from IAS 7 The following terms are used in this Standard with the meanings specified: Cash comprises cash on hand and demand deposits. Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and which are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. Cash flows are inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents. Operating activities are the principal revenue-producing activities of the entity and other activities that are not investing or financing activities. Investing activities are the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents. Financing activities are activities that result in changes in the size and composition of the contributed equity and borrowings of the entity.

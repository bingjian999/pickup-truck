In an environment where significant levels of inflation exist, the usefulness of reporting is hindered. Changes in the ‘real’ growth of revenues and expenses cannot be discerned, and the values of assets and liabilities ordinarily measured on an historical cost basis are not reflective of their true value at the reporting date. In addition, the loss in value of items of a monetary nature is not reflected. IAS 29 addresses these challenges by providing a framework for identifying currencies that are sufficiently inflationary to warrant adjustment for inflation, as well as the methods for making such adjustments. The standard outlines when hyperinflation exists, as identified by a number of common characteristics of the economy. For periods when hyperinflation is present, IAS 29 requires that all items are restated from their amounts as determined by other standards, by a measure of the inflation from the transaction or measurement date to the reporting date. This also applies to comparative figures, which are to be restated in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the current reporting period. To perform the required restatement of financial statements, assets and liabilities are separated into those that are monetary and non-monetary, with non-monetary items further divided into those measured on either a current or historical basis. Monetary items and non-monetary but current items are already measured at the monetary unit present at the reporting date and therefore do not require restatement. The restatement required by the standard therefore applies to non-monetary historical value assets and liabilities, as well as transactions. This process has consequential impacts on a number of items, including the calculation of deferred tax and the testing for impairments. The gain or loss in the value of monetary items for the period is reflected in profit or loss. The application of this standard requires numerous judgements. Some of these require consensus with the broader reporting community, such as when the use of IAS 29 is required, while others would need to be evaluated based on the circumstances of a particular entity. The standard acknowledges this complexity by conceding that the consistent application of these inflation accounting procedures and judgements from period to period is more important than the precise accuracy of the resulting amounts included in the restated financial statements. The application of IAS 29 is particularly complex where a significant amount of historical information is required. In addition, areas that may require additional attention from preparers include the first-time application of the standard; the computation of deferred tax; groups with both hyperinflationary and non-hyperinflationary entities; and interim reporting. Entities facing hyperinflation are often also affected by complexities relating to foreign currency translation, which are discussed further in Chapter 16. What you need to know • The need to apply IAS 29 is assessed at a currency level, rather than a reporting entity level, and therefore this standard shall only be applied where consensus has been reached for a given currency. • The application of this standard requires judgement in both the interpretation, where specific guidance is not provided, as well as application, and therefore consistency is prioritised over precision. • Given the complexity of the reporting process, preparers of IAS 29 financial statements should be mindful of the requirement to disclose significant judgements and estimates. Hyperinflation Introduction 1.1 Background Accounting standards are applied on the assumption that the value of money (the unit of measurement) is constant over time, which normally is an acceptable practical assumption. However, when the effect of inflation on the value of money is no longer negligible, the usefulness of historical cost based financial reporting is often significantly reduced. High rates of inflation give rise to a number of problems for entities that prepare their financial statements on a historical cost basis, for example: • Historical cost figures expressed in terms of monetary units do not show the ‘value to the business’ of assets. • Holding gains on non-monetary assets that are reported as operating profits do not represent real economic gains. • Financial information presented for the current period is not comparable with that presented for the prior periods. And • ‘Real’ capital can be reduced because profits reported do not take account of the higher replacement costs of resources used in the period. Therefore, if calculating a nominal ‘return on capital’ based on profit, and not distinguishing this properly from a real ‘return of capital’, the erosion of capital may go unnoticed in the financial statements. This is the underlying point in the concept of capital maintenance. The IASB’s The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting discusses the concept of capital maintenance, which raises the issue of how an entity defines capital. In general terms, an entity maintains its capital if it has as much capital at the end of the period as it had at the beginning, the issue being how this evaluation is measured. Whilst there are different concepts of capital maintenance, the IFRS accounting standards are ultimately based on the financial capital maintenance concept. Under the financial capital maintenance concept, the capital of the entity will be maintained if the financial amount of net assets at the end of a period is at least equal to the financial amount of net assets at the beginning of that period, excluding contributions from and distributions to owners during the period. [CF 8.3(a)]. To facilitate the evaluation of capital maintenance in a hyperinflationary environment, IAS 29 was adopted in April 2001. The IASB and IFRS IC subsequently made changes to the standards regarding the subject of hyperinflation only to clarify the provisions of the standard. In 2005, IFRIC 7 Applying the Restatement Approach under IAS 29 Financial Reporting in Hyperinflationary Economies was issued to provide guidance on applying IAS 29 in the reporting period in which an entity’s functional currency first becomes hyperinflationary (see section 10.1 below). In 2010 the IASB issued an amendment to IFRS 1 First-time Adoption of International Financial Reporting Standards for countries that exit severe hyperinflation (see section 10.3 below). FASB differences Differences between the IFRS and US GAAP guidance on this topic are not addressed in this chapter as the standards are not converged. Therefore, differences between IFRS and US GAAP may exist that are not discussed in this chapter. 1.2 Hyperinflationary economies In some countries, inflation has reached such levels that the local currency is no longer a useful measure of value in the economy and the general population may prefer not to hold its wealth in the local currency. Instead, they hold their wealth in a stable foreign currency or non-monetary assets. Such a condition is often referred to as hyperinflation. There are several characteristics that need to be considered under IFRS accounting standards to determine whether hyperinflation exists. The IASB does not monitor inflation rates in specific jurisdictions, nor does it conclude on the applicability of the characteristics to these jurisdictions. Where the assessment of the characteristics is unclear, consensus is at times facilitated by local regulators and professional bodies. Hyperinflation 1.3 Restatement approach The problems of historical cost based financial reporting may reach such a magnitude under hyperinflationary circumstances that financial reporting in the hyperinflationary currency is no longer useful. Therefore, a solution is needed to allow meaningful financial reporting by entities that operate in these hyperinflationary economies. IAS 29 requires a restatement approach, whereby financial information recorded in the hyperinflationary currency is adjusted by applying a general price index and expressed in the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period (i.e., the accounting value is adjusted for a factor of current purchasing power). This process aims to improve comparability between periods by restating financial information for changes in the purchasing power of money. Hyperinflation The requirements of IAS 29 2.1 The context of IAS 29 The underlying premise of IAS 29 is that “reporting of operating results and financial position in the local [hyperinflationary] currency without restatement is not useful”. [IAS 29.2]. The standard’s approach is therefore to require that: (a) The financial statements of an entity whose functional currency is the currency of a hyperinflationary economy shall be stated in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period. (b) The corresponding figures for the previous period required by IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements and any information in respect of earlier periods shall also be stated in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period. And (c) The gain or loss on the net monetary position shall be included in profit or loss and separately disclosed. [IAS 29.8, IAS 29.9]. IAS 29 requires amounts recorded in the statement of financial position, not already expressed in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period, to be restated in terms of the current measuring unit at the end of the reporting period, by applying a general price index. [IAS 29.11]. Hyperinflation 2.2 Scope IAS 29 must be applied by all entities whose functional currency is the currency of a hyperinflationary economy. [IAS 29.1]. The standard should be applied in an entity’s separate financial statements (if prepared) and its consolidated financial statements, as well as by parents that include such an entity in their consolidated financial statements. If an entity whose functional currency is that of a hyperinflationary economy wishes to present the financial statements in a different presentation currency, or if their parent has a different presentation currency, the financial statements of the entity first must be restated under IAS 29. Only then, can the financial statements be translated under IAS 21 The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates (see section 11 below). Almost all entities operating in hyperinflationary economies will be subject to the accounting regime of IAS 29, unless they can legitimately argue that the local hyperinflationary currency is not their functional currency as defined by IAS 21 (see Chapter 16 at section 4). [IAS 21.14]. 2.3 Definition of hyperinflation Determining whether an economy is hyperinflationary in accordance with IAS 29, and when it becomes so, requires judgement. [IAS 29.3, IAS 29.4]. Extract from IAS 29 This Standard does not establish an absolute rate at which hyperinflation is deemed to arise. It is a matter of judgement when restatement of financial statements in accordance with this Standard becomes necessary. Hyperinflation is indicated by characteristics of the economic environment of a country which include, but are not limited to, the following: (a) the general population prefers to keep its wealth in non-monetary assets or in a relatively stable foreign currency. Amounts of local currency held are immediately invested to maintain purchasing power; (b) the general population regards monetary amounts not in terms of the local currency but in terms of a relatively stable foreign currency. Prices may be quoted in that currency; (c) sales and purchases on credit take place at prices that compensate for the expected loss of purchasing power during the credit period, even if the period is short; (d) interest rates, wages and prices are linked to a price index; and (e) the cumulative inflation rate over three years is approaching, or exceeds, 100%. It is preferable that all entities that report in the currency of the same hyperinflationary economy apply this Standard from the same date. Nevertheless, this Standard applies to the financial statements of any entity from the beginning of the reporting period in which it identifies the existence of hyperinflation in the country in whose currency it reports. Identifying when a currency becomes hyperinflationary, and, just as importantly, when it ceases to be so, is not easy in practice and is frequently hampered by a lack of reliable statistics. The consideration of trends and the application of common sense is important in this judgement, as are consistency of measurement and of presentation. Transition into and out of hyperinflationary economies are discussed further in section 10 below. How we see it As at June 2024, the currencies of the following countries were widely considered to be hyperinflationary: Argentina, Ethiopia, Ghana, Haiti, Islamic Republic of Iran, Lebanon, Sierra Leone, South Sudan, Sudan, Suriname, Turkey, Venezuela and Zimbabwe. Preparers should be aware that this list may change by the time of their reporting date. Countries whose currencies should be more closely monitored for a change in the hyperinflationary status of their currency at this date were Burundi, Egypt, Lao P.D.R., Malawi, Nigeria, Pakistan, Sri Lanka and Syria. Hyperinflation 2.4 The IAS 29 restatement process Restatement of financial statements in accordance with IAS 29 can be seen as a process comprising the following steps: (a) Selection of a general price index (see section 3 below) (b) Analysis and restatement of the statement of financial position (see section 4 below) (c) Restatement of the statement of changes in equity (see section 5 below) (d) Restatement of the statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income (see section 6 below) (e) Calculation of the gain or loss on the net monetary position (see section 6.3 below) And (f) Restatement of the statement of cash flows (see section 7 below) IAS 29 requires comparative figures to be restated to the monetary unit current at the reporting date (see section 8 below). In practice, the restatement of comparatives is integral to the restatement of current year figures, and thus is the first step in the process after a general price index has been selected. In the year of initial application of the standard, the retrospective nature of IAS 29 requires the restatement of all historical amounts from varying dates at which they were last current - which is often the date of acquisition, many years ago - and is generally a time consuming and complex process. In subsequent years, the restatement process is considerably less onerous, as the previously reported figures are only adjusted for the inflation in the current period, and information about items recognised in the current period is more readily available. The restatement of comparative information is best completed as the first step in applying IAS 29 as certain items rely on this restated information to determine current year amounts. Some examples would include the closing balance of property plant and equipment, which is often calculated as the sum of the restated opening balance and movements (see Illustration 4-1 in section 4.1.4 below), and fair value gains on non-monetary items, which are determined taking into consideration the restated opening balance (as discussed in section 6.2 below). Hyperinflation Selection of a general price index The standard requires entities to use a general price index that reflects changes in general purchasing power. Ideally all entities that report in the same hyperinflationary currency should use the same price index. [IAS 29.37]. 3.1 Selecting a general price index It is generally accepted practice to use a Consumer Price Index (CPI) for this purpose, unless that index is clearly flawed. National statistical offices in most countries issue several price indices that potentially could be used for the purposes of IAS 29. Important characteristics of a good general price index include the following: • A wide range of goods and services has been included in the price index • Reflective of currency-wide, rather than regional, purchasing power • Continuity and consistency of measurement techniques and underlying assumptions • Free from bias • Frequently updated And • Available for a long period The entity shall use the above criteria to choose the most reliable and most readily available general price index and use that index consistently. It is important that the index selected is representative of the real position of the hyperinflationary currency concerned. In practice, a country’s national price index that is monitored by the International Monetary Fund, and is incorporated in its World Economic Outlook publication, is used as the basis for IAS 29 accounting. 3.2 General price index not available for all periods IAS 29 requires an entity to make an estimate of the price index if the general price index is not available for all periods for which the restatement of long-lived assets is required. The entity could base the estimate, for example, on the movements in the exchange rate between the functional currency and a relatively stable foreign currency. [IAS 29.17]. It should be noted that this method is only appropriate if the currency of the hyperinflationary economy is freely exchangeable, i.e., not subject to currency controls and ‘official’ exchange rates. Entities should also be mindful that, especially in the short term, the exchange rate may fluctuate significantly in response to factors other than changes in the domestic price level. How we see it In a scenario where there is a wide spread consensus that all available general price indices do not meet the minimum criteria for reliability (e.g., because the national statistical office in the hyperinflationary economy may be subject to significant political bias) and thus are fatally flawed, or in the absence of a general price index, entities could use an estimate based on currency movements, similar to the approach to that described in IAS 29.17. In these instances, prepares must consider the reliability of the movements in the currency as an inflation proxy. Where this is also not appropriate, the use of other sources of economic data or even the use of experts may be required. 3.3 Delayed availability of a general price index Inflation statistics are published shortly after the end of the reporting period, but in some jurisdictions the statistics are published several weeks or months later. When considering the requirements of IAS 10 Events after the Reporting Period, the release of this data is an event after the reporting date that provides evidence of conditions that existed at the end of the reporting period (see Chapter 34 at section 2.1.2). This is therefore an adjusting event that is to be taken into consideration in preparing the financial statements. If the publication of these statistics is significantly delayed, preparers may either need to wait for the statistics to become available or will need to make an estimate of the inflation index at the reporting date. The basis of such an estimate could be previous months’ inflation statistics, movements in foreign exchange rates for the period in question, or the opinion of economic experts. IAS 29 defines monetary items as “money held and items to be received or paid in money”. [IAS 29.12]. IAS 21 expands on this definition by defining monetary items as: [IAS 21.8] Extract from IAS 21 The following terms are used in this Standard with the meanings specified: … Monetary items are units of currency held and assets and liabilities to be received or paid in a fixed or determinable number of units of currency. … IAS 21 further states that the essential feature of a monetary item is a right to receive (or an obligation to deliver) a fixed or determinable number of units of currency. Examples given by IAS 21 are pensions and other employee benefits to be paid in cash, provisions that are to be settled in cash and cash dividends that are recognised as a liability. [IAS 21.16]. More obvious examples are cash and bank balances, trade receivables and payables, and loan receivables and payables. IAS 21 also states that “a contract to receive (or deliver) a variable number of the entity’s own equity instruments or a variable amount of assets in which the fair value to be received (or delivered) equals a fixed or determinable number of units of currency is a monetary item.” [IAS 21.16]. Although no examples of such contracts are given in IAS 21, it should include those contracts settled in the entity’s own equity shares that would be presented as financial assets or liabilities under IAS 32 Financial Instruments: Presentation. Conversely, the essential feature of a non-monetary item is the absence of a right to receive (or an obligation to deliver) a fixed or determinable number of units of currency. Examples given by IAS 21 are amounts prepaid for goods and services (e.g., prepaid rent); goodwill; intangible assets; inventories; property, plant and equipment; right-of-use assets; and provisions that are to be settled by the delivery of a non-monetary asset. [IAS 21.16]. IFRS 9 Financial Instruments states that all equity instruments are non- monetary. [IFRS 9.B5.7.3]. Therefore, equity investments in subsidiaries, associates or joint ventures would also be considered non-monetary items. IAS 29 provides separate rules on restatement of such investees (see section 4.3 below). Even with this guidance there may be situations where the distinction is not clear. Certain assets and liabilities may require careful analysis before they can be classified. Examples of items that are not easily classified as either monetary or non-monetary include: (a) Provisions: these can be monetary, non-monetary or partly monetary. For example, a warranty provision would be: (i) Entirely monetary when customers only have a right to return the product and obtain a cash refund equal to the amount they originally paid (ii) Non-monetary when customers have the right to have any defective product replaced And (iii) Partly monetary if customers can choose between a refund and a replacement of the defective product Classification as either a monetary or a non-monetary item is not acceptable in (iii) above. To meet the requirements of IAS 29, part of the provision should be treated as a non-monetary item and the remainder as a monetary item. (b) Deferred tax assets and liabilities: characterising these as monetary or non-monetary can be difficult as explained in section 4.4 below. (c) Associates and joint ventures: IAS 29 provides separate rules on restatement of investees that do not rely on the distinction between monetary and non-monetary items (see section 4.3 below). Hyperinflation (d) Deposits or progress payments paid or received: if the payments made are regarded as prepayments or as progress payments then the amounts should be treated as non-monetary items. However, if the payments made are in effect refundable deposits, then the amounts should probably be treated as monetary items. (e) Insurance contract assets and liabilities: While IFRS 17 Insurance Contracts states that groups of insurance contracts, including the contractual service margin should be treated as a monetary item when applying IAS 21, [IFRS 17.30], it is not clear from the wording in the standard if insurance contracts should also be considered monetary for the purposes of the application of IAS 29. How we see it The practical application of the monetary/non-monetary distinction, discussed above, can be complex and will require judgement on the part of preparers of financial statements. Further examples of problem areas in the application of the monetary/non-monetary distinction are discussed in Chapter 16 at section 5.4. 4.1.2 Monetary items Generally, monetary items are not restated to reflect the effect of inflation because they already reflect their purchasing power at the end of the reporting period. However, monetary assets and liabilities linked by agreement to changes in prices, such as index-linked bonds and loans, should be adjusted in accordance with the terms of the underlying agreement to show the repayment obligation at the end of the reporting period. [IAS 29.13]. This adjustment should be offset against the gain or loss on the net monetary position (see section 6.3 below). [IAS 29.28]. This type of restatement is not a hyperinflation accounting adjustment, but rather a gain or loss on a financial instrument. Accounting for inflation-linked bonds and loans under IFRS 9 may well lead to complexity in financial reporting. Depending on the specific wording of the inflation adjustment clause, such contracts may give rise to embedded derivatives and gains or losses will have to be recorded either in profit or loss or other comprehensive income depending on how the instrument is classified under IFRS 9 (see # Chapter 42 at section 4.1.5 and Chapter 44 at section 5.3.5). 4.1.3 Non-monetary items carried at current cost Non-monetary items carried at current cost are not restated because they are already expressed in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period. [IAS 29.29]. Current cost is not defined by the standard, but the IASB’s Conceptual Framework provides the following definition: “The current cost of an asset is the cost of an equivalent asset at the measurement date, comprising the consideration that would be paid at the measurement date plus the transaction costs that would be incurred at that date. The current cost of a liability is the consideration that would be received for an equivalent liability at the measurement date minus the transaction costs that would be incurred at that date”. [CF 6.21]. IAS 29 expands this definition by including net realisable value and fair value into the concept of “amounts current at the end of the reporting period”. [IAS 29.14]. In summary, this would include items carried at a value that reflects purchasing power at the balance sheet date. It is important to note that non-monetary items that were revalued at some earlier date are not necessarily carried at current cost, and need to be restated from the date of their latest revaluation. [IAS 29.18]. This may include items such as assets impaired to a recoverable amount in an earlier reporting period, which should be adjusted for inflation from the date of such impairment. Likewise, in the case of a non-monetary prepayment for an asset, it may be that the date of the prepayment, rather than the date of initial recognition of the asset, is the date at which the value was current, and therefore the date from which the asset should be adjusted for inflation. Categorisation of prepayments as monetary or non-monetary is discussed in section 4.1.1 above. In hyperinflationary economies, national legislation may require entities to adjust historical cost based financial information in a way that is not in accordance with IAS 29 (for example, national legislation may require entities to adjust the carrying amount of tangible fixed assets by applying a multiplier). Though financial information adjusted in accordance with national legislation is sometimes described as ‘current cost’ information, it will seldom meet the definition of current cost in accordance with the Conceptual Framework. [CF 6.21]. Where this is the case, entities must first determine the carrying value on the historical cost basis for these assets and liabilities before applying the requirements of IAS 29. This means that the individual components of finished goods are restated from their respective purchase dates. Similarly, if production takes place in several distinct phases, the costs associated with each of those phases are restated from the date that the cost was incurred. Given the large number of transactions affecting an entity’s inventory position, it may be difficult to determine the date of acquisition of individual items of inventory. Therefore, entities commonly approximate the ageing of inventories based on inventory turnover. Similarly, the level of the general price index at the date of acquisition is often determined at the average level for the month because an up-to-date price index is not available for each day of the month. Determining the appropriate level of the general price index can be difficult when the price index is updated relatively infrequently and the entity’s business is highly seasonal. IAS 29 requires restatement of inventory by applying a general price index, which could result in an overvaluation when the price of inventory items increases at a different rate from the general price index. At the end of each period, it is therefore essential to ensure that items of inventory are not valued in excess of their net realisable value. Any overstated inventories shall be written down to net realisable value under IAS 2. [IAS 29.19]. 4.3 Restatement of associates, joint ventures and subsidiaries IAS 29 provides separate rules for the restatement of associates and joint ventures that are accounted for under the equity method. [IAS 29.20]. The standard does not permit the investment in the investee to be treated as a single indivisible item for the purposes of the IAS 29 restatement. Hyperinflation Restatement of the statement of changes in equity When an entity first applies IAS 29, it restates the components of owners’ equity at the beginning of the earliest period presented as follows: • The components of owners’ equity, except retained earnings and any revaluation surplus, are restated by applying a general price index from the dates the components were contributed or otherwise arose. • Any revaluation surplus that arose in previous periods is eliminated. And • Restated retained earnings are derived from all the other amounts in the restated statement of financial position. [IAS 29.24]. At the end of the first period and in subsequent periods, all components of owners’ equity are restated by applying a general price index from the beginning of the period or the date of contribution, if later. [IAS 29.25]. Subsequent revaluations may give rise to a revaluation surplus within equity. IFRS does not define a revaluation surplus and it is not always clear which reserves might meet this definition. Depending on how broadly the concept of ’revaluation’ is applied, this may include reserves relating to, for example, financial instruments at fair value through other comprehensive income, or actuarial gains and losses, if these are presented separately. The approach taken on initial application as well as in subsequent years affects, in particular, those reserves reclassified to profit or loss, and is discussed in section 6.4 below. Likewise, IFRS does not define retained earnings and many jurisdictions require entities to appropriate part of the balance into specific (often non-distributable) reserves. In such cases, entities will need to apply judgement to determine whether these reserves are essentially part of retained earnings (and so are not restated by applying the general price index as described in IAS 29.24). If they are considered a separate component of equity, then they are restated by applying the general price index as explained above, both at the beginning of the first period when an entity applies IAS 29 and at the end of the first period and subsequent periods. Where entities have made such judgements concerning the types of reserves held and these judgements have a significant effect on the amounts recognised in the financial statements, IAS 1 requires disclosure to users of the financial statements. [IAS 1.122]. Though IAS 29 provides guidance on the restatement of assets, liabilities and individual components of shareholders’ equity, national laws and regulations with which the entity needs to comply might not permit such revaluations. This can mean that IAS 29 may require restatement of distributable reserves, but that from the legal point of view in the jurisdiction concerned, those same reserves remain unchanged. That is, it is possible that ‘restated retained earnings’ under IAS 29 will not all be legally distributable. It may therefore be unclear to users of financial statements restated under IAS 29 to what extent components of equity are distributable. Because of its global constituents, the IASB’s standards cannot deal with specific national legal requirements relating to a legal entity’s equity. How we see it In jurisdictions with laws or regulations governing the categories of equity and particularly, which categories of equity may be distributed, entities reporting under IAS 29 should disclose the extent to which components of equity are distributable where this is not obvious from the financial statements. In our view, it is important for entities to give supplementary information in the circumstances where the IAS 29 adjustments have produced large apparently distributable reserves that are in fact not distributable. Hyperinflation Restatement of the statement of cash flows The standard requires that all items in the statement of cash flows be expressed in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period. [IAS 29.33]. This is a difficult requirement to fulfil in practice. IAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows requires the following information to be presented: • Cash flows from operating activities, which are the principal revenue-producing activities of the entity and other activities that are not investing or financing activities. • Cash flows from investing activities, which are the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents. And • Cash flows from financing activities, which are activities that result in changes in the size and composition of the equity capital and borrowings of the entity. [IAS 7.6, IAS 7.10]. In effect IAS 29 requires restatement of most items in a statement of cash flows, therefore implying that the actual cash flows at the time of the transactions will be different from the numbers presented in the statement of cash flows itself. However, not all items are restated using the same method and many of the restatements are based on estimates. For example, items in the statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income are restated using an estimate of the general price index at the time that the revenues were earned and the costs incurred. Unavoidably, this will give rise to some inconsistencies. Similarly, the restatement of statement of financial position items will give rise to discrepancies because some items are not easily classified as either monetary or non-monetary. This raises the question of how an entity should classify the monetary gain or loss relating to a statement of financial position item in its statement of cash flows. It is not clear from IAS 29 how a monetary gain or loss should be presented in the statement of cash flows. In practice, the most commonly applied approach restates cash flows by attributing the effect of inflation on operating, investing and financing cash flows to the underlying item and presenting the monetary gain or loss on cash and cash equivalents separately. However, given the lack of guidance in the IFRS accounting standards, and given the complexity of determining the monetary gains or losses specific to cash and cash equivalents, other approaches might be acceptable. How we see it Irrespective of the method chosen to present a statement of cash flows under IAS 29, users of such statement prepared in the currency of a hyperinflationary economy should be mindful of the fact that figures presented in the statement may have been restated in accordance with IAS 29 and may differ from the actual underlying cash flows on the date of the transaction. In our view, it is important for entities that have a significant proportion of their activities in hyperinflationary economies to consider whether it provides sufficient additional disclosures to ensure that the financial statements can be understood correctly. Whether this is limited to a general explanation of the mismatch between reported and actual amounts, or specific information on major transactions is provided, would depend on the nature and materiality of transactions affected. The comparative financial information reported for prior periods must also be restated to the current measuring unit. [IAS 34.IE B33]. Hence, an entity that reports quarterly information must restate the comparative statements of financial position, statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income, and other primary financial statements each quarter. In restating its financial information an entity may not ‘annualise’ the recognition of the gain or loss on the net monetary position or use an estimated annual inflation rate in preparing an interim financial report in a hyperinflationary economy. [IAS 34.IE B34]. Interim reporting of a group containing a subsidiary that reports in a hyperinflationary currency results in particular issues in the year that the subsidiary’s functional currency becomes hyperinflationary. These are discussed further in section 11.3 below. Hyperinflation ## Transition ### Economies becoming hyperinflationary When the functional currency of an entity becomes hyperinflationary it must start applying IAS 29. The standard requires that the financial statements and any information in respect of earlier periods are stated in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period. [IAS 29.8]. IFRIC 7 clarifies that items are restated fully retrospectively. In the first year in which the entity identifies the existence of hyperinflation, the requirements of IAS 29 are applied as if the economy had always been hyperinflationary. The opening statement of financial position at the beginning of the earliest period presented in the financial statements are restated as follows: • Non-monetary items measured at historical cost are restated to reflect the effect of inflation from the date the assets were acquired and the liabilities were incurred or assumed. And • Non-monetary items carried at amounts current at dates other than those of acquisition or incurrence are to be restated to reflect the effect of inflation from the dates those carrying amounts were determined. [IFRIC 7.3]. In practice, the process of applying IAS 29 fully retrospectively requires a number of steps to determine the appropriate comparative values, and specifically the opening equity reserves, to ensure the correct balances at the reporting date. Supposing an entity, with a 31 December reporting date, became hyperinflationary in its 20X2 reporting period, the statement of financial position at 1 January 20X1 (i.e., the beginning of the earliest comparative period), restated to the monetary unit present at that date, would need to be calculated, as well as the components of equity restated to derive the restated retained earnings position at that date (as discussed in section 5 above). This process would require an entity to consider whether the restated values of items subject to comparison against a recoverable amount, as discussed in section 4.1.4 above, would require impairment. In completing this assessment, care should be taken to ensure that such a test is performed by comparing carrying and recoverable amounts stated using financial information measured at consistent dates. For example, the restated carrying amount as calculated at 1 January 20X1 (i.e., the beginning of the earliest comparative period) is compared to the recoverable amount determined at that date. The resulting carrying value would then be restated to the monetary unit present at 31 December 20X2. To calculate the gain or loss on net monetary position for the 20X1 comparative statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income: • All items in the opening statement of financial position, including items carried at amounts current at that date, are adjusted for the inflation during the year to the monetary unit present at 31 December 20X1. • The 20X1 financial statements are restated to the monetary unit at 31 December 20X1. And • The gain or loss on the net monetary position is calculated as discussed in section 6.3 above. The financial information determined in this way would then be adjusted for hyperinflation during 20X2 to the monetary unit present at 31 December 20X2, including items carried at amounts current at the previous reporting dates, to correctly establish the comparatives to be reported. Other areas that are more complex in the period of initial application arise in group reporting environments where a non-hyperinflationary parent controls a hyperinflationary subsidiary. This raises questions on the accounting for the initial restatement process (discussed in Section 11 below) as well as the impact on interim reporting considerations in the period the subsidiary becomes hyperinflationary (discussed in Section 11.3 below). 10.1.1 Presentation of statement of financial position at the beginning of the comparative period As discussed above, upon transition to IAS 29, the statement of financial position at the beginning of the earliest comparative period is required to be calculated as the base of all subsequent reporting. IAS 1 requires an entity to present a statement of financial position as at the beginning of the preceding period when it “applies an accounting policy retrospectively” or “makes retrospective restatements of items” and this has a material effect on the information in the statement of financial position at the beginning of the preceding period. [IAS 1.10(f)]. Hyperinflation This raises the question as to whether the initial application of IAS 29 should be seen as the adoption of a new accounting policy. The application of IFRS requires all standards to be adopted, whether applicable or not. Therefore, the initial application of IAS 29 is not a change in accounting policy but rather the first time that the standard is required to be used. IFRIC 7 describes the initial application of IAS 29 as both a change in status as well as “similar to the retrospective application of a change in accounting policy”. [IFRIC 7.BC17]. This could be interpreted to excuse preparers from the requirement to present the statement of financial position at the beginning of the earliest comparative period, on the grounds that is it not a policy change, or alternatively, to imply that the similarity is such that the disclosure is required. How we see it Preparers of financial statements should consider the extent to which the presentation of a statement of financial position as at the beginning of the preceding period provides information that is useful to the users thereof. Where this additional statement would add meaningful context to the financial statements that is not provided in the notes thereto, its inclusion would be beneficial. In practice, we see limited instances of this additional statement being presented. Where such an opening statement would be required for another reason, such as the retrospective application of a change in policy, the initial application of IAS 29 would not absolve preparers of this requirement. ### Economies ceasing to be hyperinflationary Determining when a currency stops being hyperinflationary is not easy in practice. It is important to review trends, not just at the end of the reporting period but also subsequently. In addition, consistency demands that the financial statements do not unnecessarily fall in and out of a hyperinflationary presentation, where a more careful judgement would have avoided it. When an economy ceases to be hyperinflationary, entities discontinue preparation and presentation of financial statements in accordance with IAS 29. [IAS 29.38]. This issue has been clarified by IAS 21 which specifically prohibits restatement of comparative figures when the presentation currency is not hyperinflationary. [IAS 21.42(b)]. This means that when the financial statements of a hyperinflationary subsidiary are translated into the non-hyperinflationary presentation currency for consolidation into the financial statements of the parent, the comparative amounts in annual and interim financial statements are not adjusted. However, the impact on interim financial statements of such a parent may be more difficult to resolve. For example, a non-hyperinflationary parent (with a December year-end) may own a subsidiary whose functional currency is considered hyperinflationary from, say, 1 July 20X2 onwards. The subsidiary’s second quarter results would not have been adjusted for the impact of hyperinflation, while its third quarter results would reflect the effects of hyperinflation for the period from the beginning of the year to the reporting date. This results in the parent needing to reflect a ‘catch up effect’ in its third quarter or full year reporting. As the prior year comparative figures cannot be restated under IAS 21, the full effect would be included in the current period. [IAS 21.42(b)]. However, IAS 29.4 specifically states that the standard “…applies to the financial statements of any entity from the beginning of the reporting period in which it identifies the existence of hyperinflation in the country in whose currency it reports”. If separate information is presented for each quarter, it is not clear how this effect should be taken into consideration in preparing the parent’s third quarter results, specifically whether the year-to-date results are adjusted as though the subsidiary was hyperinflationary from the beginning of the year or only from the beginning of the third quarter. Possible approaches would include: • Reporting the difference between the year-to-date profit for the third quarter and the year-to-date profit for the second quarter in which the subsidiary is restated under IAS 29 (i.e., with purchasing power adjustments to 30 June). Or • Reporting the third quarter profits as the difference between the year-to-date profit in which the subsidiary is restated under IAS 29, less the year-to-date second quarter profits as they were actually reported (i.e., non-hyperinflation based results). The first approach is consistent with the requirement that the year-end accounts will be prepared as if the economy had been hyperinflationary from the beginning of 20X2, will provide a better basis for comparison in 20X2 and will result in individual quarters in 20X1 being more comparable. Hyperinflation ## Elimination of intergroup transactions When transactions take place between hyperinflationary and non-hyperinflationary entities within a group, the restatement process of IAS 29 and the translation requirements of IAS 21 result in complexities in eliminating these transactions on consolidation as required by IFRS 10 Consolidated Financial Statements and discussed at (Chapter 8 at section 2.4). [IFRS 10.B86(c)]. The elimination of intercompany receivables or payables is straightforward, as balances are translated at closing rates and therefore, the amounts reflected for each entity in the consolidation would be equal to that reflected by the counterparty. In contrast, an intercompany income or expense is affected by the requirement to use different exchange rates and the restatement for inflation. For example, if a management fee, denominated in a hyperinflationary currency, is charged by a subsidiary in a hyperinflationary currency to a fellow subsidiary in a non-hyperinflationary currency: • The hyperinflationary subsidiary would restate its income for the effects of hyperinflation (see section 6 above) and translate the income at the closing rate into the presentation currency for consolidation purposes (see section 4.3.1 above). • The non-hyperinflationary subsidiary would not restate its expense for hyperinflation and likely apply the average exchange rate to translate the expense into the presentation currency for consolidation purposes (see Chapter 16 at section 5.1.3). As a result, the amounts reflected for each entity in the consolidation would not be equal to that reflected by the counterparty. The question therefore arises as to how this difference should be treated upon consolidation. The IFRS IC observed in March 2020 that an entity presents in other comprehensive income any exchange difference resulting from the translation of a hyperinflationary foreign operation.9 How we see it In the absence of specific guidance, the Committee’s observation, as discussed in more detail in section 11 above, provides the clearest guidance. To the extent the difference caused by the IAS 29 restatement effect and the IAS 21 translation effect in combination meet the definition of an exchange difference, applying the principles of this observation, the difference would be eliminated against other comprehensive income. To the extent that the entity considers only the IAS 21 impact to meet the definition of an exchange difference, this portion is eliminated against other comprehensive income and the impact of the restatement for inflation is recognised directly in equity. Under this approach, as only the hyperinflationary entity is exposed to a monetary gain or loss, this is not eliminated on consolidation. **中文:** 然而，当通货膨胀对货币价值的影响不再可忽略时，基于历史成本的财务报告的有用性通常会显著降低。高通胀率给按历史成本编制财务报表的主体带来诸多问题，例如： • 以货币单位表示的历史成本数据无法反映资产的“企业价值”。 • 作为营业利润报告的非货币资产持有收益并不代表真实的经济收益。 • 当期列报的财务信息与前期列报的信息不具有可比性。 • “实际”资本可能因报告利润未考虑当期所用资源的更高重置成本而减少。因此，若基于利润计算名义“资本回报率”，而未将其与真实“资本返还”适当区分，则财务报表中可能无法察觉资本的侵蚀。这正是资本保全概念的核心要点。国际会计准则理事会（IASB）的《财务报告概念框架》讨论了资本保全概念，该概念引出了主体如何界定资本的问题。一般而言，若主体在期末拥有与期初同等规模的资本，则其资本得以保全，问题在于如何计量这一评估结果。尽管存在不同的资本保全概念，但IFRS会计准则最终基于财务资本保全概念。根据财务资本保全概念，若主体在期末的净资产财务金额至少等于期初的净资产财务金额（扣除当期所有者投入和分配），则其资本得以保全。[CF 8.3(a)]

**中文:** 如果这些项目被视为权益的单独组成部分，则需按照上述方法，在主体首次应用《国际会计准则第29号》的第一个期间的期初、该期间期末及后续期间，通过应用一般物价指数进行重述。若主体对持有的储备类型作出此类判断，且该判断对财务报表中确认的金额产生重大影响，《国际会计准则第1号》要求向财务报表使用者披露相关信息。[《国际会计准则第1号》第122段]。尽管《国际会计准则第29号》就资产、负债及股东权益各组成部分的重述提供了指引，但主体需遵守的国家法律法规可能不允许此类重估。这意味着，《国际会计准则第29号》可能要求重述可分配储备，但从相关司法管辖区的法律角度来看，这些储备保持不变。即，根据《国际会计准则第29号》重述的“留存收益”可能并非全部在法律上可分配。因此，根据《国际会计准则第29号》重述的财务报表使用者可能难以明确权益各组成部分的可分配程度。由于国际会计准则理事会（IASB）的准则面向全球主体，无法处理与法律实体权益相关的特定国家法律要求。我们的观点：在存在法律或法规规范权益类别，特别是规定哪些权益类别可分配的司法管辖区，根据《国际会计准则第29号》报告的主体应披露权益各组成部分的可分配程度（若财务报表未明确说明）。我们认为，当《国际会计准则第29号》调整产生大量表面上可分配但实际不可分配的储备时，主体提供补充信息至关重要。

**中文:** 6.1 利息与汇兑差额的重述 一个常见问题是，主体是否应根据《国际会计准则第29号》重述汇兑差额，因为该准则认为“与借入或借出资金相关的外汇差额也与净货币头寸有关”[IAS 29.28]。然而，该准则要求，损益及其他综合收益表中的所有项目均需按报告期末的计量单位列报。“因此，所有金额均需通过自收入与费用项目初始确认于财务报表之日起的一般物价指数变动进行重述”[IAS 29.26]。因此，利息与汇兑差额需根据通货膨胀影响进行重述，这与损益及其他综合收益表中大多数其他项目的处理方式一致，并需单独披露。不过，若将其与净货币头寸的利得或损失一并列示于损益及其他综合收益表中，可能更为有用[IAS 29.28]。

**中文:** 恶性通货膨胀 现金流量表的重述 该准则要求现金流量表中的所有项目均按报告期末的计量单位列报。[IAS 29.33] 这一要求在实务中难以实现。《IAS 7——现金流量表》要求列报以下信息： • 经营活动现金流量，即主体主要创收活动及其他非投资或融资活动的现金流量。 • 投资活动现金流量，即长期资产的购置和处置，以及其他未纳入现金等价物的投资。 以及 • 筹资活动现金流量，即导致主体权益资本及借款规模和构成发生变动的活动。[IAS 7.6, IAS 7.10]

**中文:** 实际上，国际会计准则第29号要求对现金流量表中的大多数项目进行重述，这意味着交易发生时的实际现金流量将与现金流量表本身列示的金额不同。然而，并非所有项目都采用相同的方法重述，且许多重述基于估计。例如，利润或亏损及其他综合收益表中的项目，需使用收入赚取和成本发生时的通用价格指数估计值进行重述。这不可避免地会导致一些不一致性。同样，财务状况表项目的重述也会产生差异，因为某些项目不易归类为货币性或非货币性项目。这引发了一个问题：主体应如何在其现金流量表中对与财务状况表项目相关的货币性利得或损失进行分类？国际会计准则第29号并未明确说明货币性利得或损失应如何在现金流量表中列报。实务中，最常用的方法是将通货膨胀对经营、投资和筹资现金流量的影响归因于相关项目，并单独列示现金及现金等价物的货币性利得或损失。然而，鉴于国际财务报告准则缺乏相关指引，且确定现金及现金等价物特定货币性利得或损失的复杂性，其他方法也可能被接受。我们的观点：无论选择何种方法根据国际会计准则第29号列报现金流量表，以恶性通货膨胀经济体货币编制的该报表使用者应意识到，报表中列示的金额可能已根据国际会计准则第29号进行重述，且可能与交易发生日的实际现金流量存在差异。

**中文:** 我们认为，在恶性通货膨胀经济中开展重大比例业务的主体，应考虑是否提供充分的额外披露，以确保财务报表能被正确理解。这种披露是仅限于对报告金额与实际金额之间差异的一般性解释，还是提供重大交易的具体信息，将取决于受影响交易的性质和重要性。

**中文:** 恶性通货膨胀下比较数据的重述 该准则要求所有财务信息均以当前报告期末的计量单位列报，因此： • 上一报告期的对应数据（无论基于历史成本法还是现行成本法）均需通过一般物价指数进行重述。 • 针对更早期间披露的信息，也应以报告期末的计量单位表述。[IAS 29.34]

**中文:** 若上一报告期已应用IAS 29，则只需将报告期末的计量单位直接应用于上一年度比较数据，即可完成简单的数学计算。上述示例4-1中不动产、厂场和设备期初余额的重述即体现了这一点。首次应用IAS 29时，流程更为复杂，详见下文第10.1节。

**中文:** 恶性通货膨胀下的中期报告 IAS 34《中期财务报告》的示例指出，恶性通货膨胀经济中的中期财务报告应遵循与财务年度末相同的原则编制。[IAS 34.IE B32]

**中文:** 这意味着财务报表必须以中期期末当前计量单位列报，且净货币性头寸的利得或损失计入净利润（损益）。比较财务信息中报告的前期数据也必须按当前计量单位重述。[IAS 34.IE B33] 因此，按季度报告信息的主体必须在每个季度重述比较财务状况表、损益及其他综合收益表以及其他主要财务报表。在重述财务信息时，主体不得将净货币性头寸的利得或损失"按年化处理"，也不得在恶性通货膨胀经济体中编制中期财务报告时使用估计的年通货膨胀率。[IAS 34.IE B34] 若集团中包含以恶性通货膨胀货币报告的子公司在中期报告时，会在该子公司功能货币变为恶性通货膨胀的年度产生特殊问题。这些问题将在下文第11.3节进一步讨论。

## ## 恶性通货膨胀 ### 过渡 #### 经济变为恶性通货膨胀 当主体的功能货币变为恶性通货膨胀时，必须开始应用《国际会计准则第29号》。该准则要求财务报表及任何涉及更早期间的信息均以报告期末当前计量单位列报。[IAS 29.8] 《国际财务报告解释委员会第7号解释》明确，项目需完全追溯重述。在主体首次识别存在恶性通货膨胀的年度，应如同该经济体始终处于恶性通货膨胀状态一样应用《国际会计准则第29号》的要求。

**中文:** 财务报表中最早列报期初的财务状况表按以下方式重述：• 以历史成本计量的非货币性项目需重述，以反映自资产购置日及负债发生或承担日起的通货膨胀影响；• 以购置或发生日以外时点的现行金额列报的非货币性项目，需重述以反映自该等账面金额确定日起的通货膨胀影响。[IFRIC 7.3] 实务中，全面追溯应用《国际会计准则第29号》需通过若干步骤确定适当的比较数值，特别是期初权益储备，以确保报告日余额正确。假设某报告日为12月31日的实体在20X2报告期进入恶性通货膨胀状态，则需计算20X1年1月1日（即最早比较期期初）按当日货币单位重述的财务状况表，以及重述权益各组成部分以得出该日重述留存收益状况（如上述第5节所述）。此过程要求实体考虑：需与可收回金额进行比较的项目（如上述第4.1.4节所述）的重述价值是否需计提减值。完成该评估时，应确保通过比较以一致日期财务信息计量的账面金额与可收回金额进行测试。例如，将20X1年1月1日（即最早比较期期初）计算的重述账面金额与该日确定的可收回金额进行比较，随后将所得账面价值重述为20X2年12月31日的货币单位。

**中文:** 计算20X1年度比较利润表及其他综合收益表中净货币头寸的损益时：• 期初财务状况表中的所有项目（包括按当日现行金额列报的项目）均需根据年内通货膨胀率调整至20X1年12月31日的货币单位。• 20X1年度财务报表需重述为20X1年12月31日的货币单位。• 净货币头寸的损益按上文第6.3节所述方法计算。按此方式确定的财务信息随后需根据20X2年度的恶性通货膨胀调整至20X2年12月31日的货币单位（包括按前期报告日现行金额列报的项目），以正确建立需列报的比较数据。在首次应用期间，其他较为复杂的领域涉及集团报告环境，即非恶性通货膨胀的母公司控制恶性通货膨胀的子公司。这引发了关于首次重述过程的会计处理（见下文第11节）以及子公司进入恶性通货膨胀期间对中期报告考虑事项的影响（见下文第11.3节）等问题。 10.1.1 比较期初财务状况表的列报 如上所述，在首次应用《国际会计准则第29号》时，需计算最早比较期初的财务状况表，作为后续所有报告的基础。《国际会计准则第1号》要求，当主体“追溯应用会计政策”或“对项目进行追溯重述”且对最早比较期初财务状况表信息产生重大影响时，应列报该期初的财务状况表。[《国际会计准则第1号》第10(f)段]

**中文:** 恶性通货膨胀 这引发了一个问题：首次应用《国际会计准则第29号》是否应被视为采用一项新会计政策。国际财务报告准则要求采用所有准则，无论其是否适用。因此，首次应用《国际会计准则第29号》并非会计政策变更，而是该准则首次被要求使用。《国际财务报告准则解释委员会第7号》将首次应用《国际会计准则第29号》描述为一种状态变更，且“类似于会计政策变更的追溯应用”[《国际财务报告准则解释委员会第7号》结论基础第17段]。这可以被解释为：由于并非政策变更，从而免除编制者列报最早比较期期初财务状况表的要求；或者相反，暗示这种相似性要求进行相关披露。我们的观点 财务报表编制者应考虑列报上一期期初财务状况表在何种程度上能为使用者提供有用信息。若该额外报表能为财务报表提供附注中未包含的有意义背景信息，则将其纳入将是有益的。实践中，我们很少见到列报该额外报表的情况。若因其他原因（如会计政策变更的追溯应用）需要列报该期初报表，则首次应用《国际会计准则第29号》并不能免除编制者的此项要求。### 经济体不再处于恶性通货膨胀状态 在实践中，确定货币何时不再处于恶性通货膨胀状态并非易事。重要的是不仅要审视报告期末的趋势，还需关注后续趋势。此外，一致性要求财务报表不应不必要地反复进入和退出恶性通货膨胀列报模式——若更审慎的判断本可避免这种情况。

**中文:** 当经济体不再处于恶性通货膨胀状态时，主体应停止按照《国际会计准则第29号》编制和列报财务报表。以上一报告期末当前计量单位表示的金额，将作为后续财务状况表项目账面金额的基础[IAS 29.38]。作为未来账面金额基础的上一个报告期，可能是上一个年度报告期，也可能是中期报告期，具体取决于主体是否编制中期报告。因此，对于编制中期报告的主体而言，需注意：将适用《国际会计准则第29号》的中期期间与不适用该准则的期间合并处理，可能导致财务报表难以解读。如下方示例10-1所示。

## ## 非恶性通货膨胀主体并入恶性通货膨胀集团

**中文:** 2022年6月，国际财务报告准则解释委员会讨论了一份文件，涉及功能货币为恶性通货膨胀经济货币的母公司在合并功能货币为非恶性通货膨胀经济货币的子公司时应用的会计处理。委员会审议了母公司在编制合并财务报表时，是否应适用《国际会计准则第29号》对其非恶性通货膨胀子公司的当期金额和比较金额进行重述。

**中文:** 若母公司对本期及比较期间金额进行重述，则需以报告期末（报告日）的当前计量单位列报这些金额。委员会讨论的两种方法如下5： • 母公司不将其非恶性通货膨胀子公司的经营成果和财务状况按报告日当前计量单位进行重述：本期财务信息仅按第16章第6.1节所述方法，适用当期适当汇率进行调整。当本期变为比较期间时，恶性通货膨胀母公司的财务信息需重述，而非恶性通货膨胀子公司的财务信息则无需重述，因此需重新编制该期间的合并报表及期初财务业绩表。 • 母公司将其非恶性通货膨胀子公司的经营成果和财务状况按报告日当前计量单位进行重述：本期财务信息按第16章第6.1节所述方法，适用当期适当汇率进行调整。财务状况表中的信息视为当前信息，无需重述，而综合收益表则需根据通货膨胀进行调整，并以报告日当前计量单位列报。当本期变为比较期间时，合并比较信息需按报告期末当前计量单位进行重述。

**中文:** 委员会指出，根据《国际会计准则第21号》和《国际会计准则第29号》对提交的事实模式的要求，母公司可以选择重述或不重述子公司以报告期末当前计量单位计量的业绩和财务状况。6 国际财务报告准则解释委员会建议国际会计准则理事会制定一项范围有限的修订，以解决上述事项以及相关事项：即功能货币为非恶性通货膨胀经济体货币，但以恶性通货膨胀经济体货币列报财务报表的实体。7 2024年7月，一份征求意见稿， *参考：国际会计准则理事会征求意见稿ED/2024/4《折算为恶性通货膨胀列报货币》，针对该问题已* 发布。该征求意见稿建议，当实体将金额从非恶性通货膨胀经济体的功能货币折算为恶性通货膨胀经济体的列报货币时，该实体应使用最近一期财务状况表日的期末汇率折算这些金额（包括比较金额）。国际会计准则理事会预计，这种折算方法将使实体以当前计量单位列报需折算的金额。该文件还针对此类情况下的集团提出了某些披露要求。8 在该征求意见稿最终定稿前，编制者应确保在会计政策中明确概述所采用的方法。*参考：国际会计准则理事会工作人员文件第2号，项目：恶性通货膨胀母公司合并非恶性通货膨胀子公司（《国际会计准则第21号》和* 《国际会计准则第29号》），文件主题：初步考虑，国际财务报告准则解释委员会会议，2022年6月。可在国际财务报告准则基金会网站上查阅。国际财务报告准则解释委员会更新，2022年6月，可在国际财务报告准则基金会网站上查阅。国际财务报告准则解释委员会更新，2023年6月，可在国际财务报告准则基金会网站上查阅。征求意见稿ED/2024/4《折算为恶性通货膨胀列报货币》，国际会计准则理事会，2024年7月。

**中文:** 可在国际财务报告准则基金会网站上查阅。恶性通货膨胀 ## 集团内部交易的抵销 当集团内部恶性通货膨胀主体与非恶性通货膨胀主体之间发生交易时，国际会计准则第29号的重述要求与国际会计准则第21号的折算要求，会导致在合并过程中按照国际财务报告准则第10号《合并财务报表》的规定抵销这些交易时产生复杂性（参见第8章第2.4节）[IFRS 10.B86(c)]。公司间应收或应付款项的抵销较为直接，因为余额按期末汇率折算，因此合并中各主体反映的金额将与交易对手方反映的金额相等。相反，公司间收入或费用则受到使用不同汇率及通货膨胀重述要求的影响。例如，若以恶性通货膨胀货币计价的管理费由恶性通货膨胀主体向非恶性通货膨胀主体收取：• 恶性通货膨胀主体将重述其收入以反映恶性通货膨胀的影响（参见上文第6节），并按期末汇率将该收入折算为合并目的的报告货币（参见上文第4.3.1节）。• 非恶性通货膨胀主体不会重述其费用以反映恶性通货膨胀，并可能采用平均汇率将该费用折算为合并目的的报告货币（参见第16章第5.1.3节）。因此，合并中各主体反映的金额将与交易对手方反映的金额不相等。由此产生的问题是在合并时应如何处理这一差额。

**中文:** IFRS IC于2020年3月指出，主体在折算恶性通货膨胀境外经营时产生的任何汇兑差额均计入其他综合收益。9 我们的观点 在缺乏具体指引的情况下，委员会的意见（详见上文第11节）提供了最清晰的指导。若因《国际会计准则第29号》重述效应与《国际会计准则第21号》折算效应共同产生的差额符合汇兑差额的定义，则根据该意见的原则，该差额应抵销其他综合收益。若主体仅认为《国际会计准则第21号》的影响符合汇兑差额定义，则该部分抵销其他综合收益，而通货膨胀重述的影响直接确认为权益。在此方法下，由于仅恶性通货膨胀主体面临货币性利得或损失，该部分在合并时不予抵销。IFRIC更新，2020年3月，可查阅IFRS基金会网站。恶性通货膨胀 ## 披露 摘自《国际会计准则第29号》 应作出以下披露：(a)财务报表及以前期间对应数据已根据功能货币一般购买力变动进行重述，因此以报告期末的计量单位列报的事实；(b)财务报表是基于历史成本法还是现行成本法编制；以及(c)报告期末价格指数的性质与水平，以及当期与上一报告期间该指数的变动情况。**[《国际会计准则第29号》第39段]。** 需注意，不允许将根据《国际会计准则第29号》重述的财务信息作为未重述财务信息的补充进行披露。

**中文:** 这是为了防止实体将基于历史成本的财务信息置于比根据《国际会计准则第29号》重述的信息更显著的位置。该准则也不鼓励单独列报未经重述的财务信息，但并未明确禁止。[《国际会计准则第29号》第7段]。然而，此类未经重述的财务报表将不符合国际财务报告准则，并应明确标识其性质。若实体因当地税务机关或证券交易所监管机构等要求而需列报未经重述的财务报表，则必须确保国际财务报告准则财务报表被视为主要财务报表，而非仅为补充信息。以下摘录说明了净货币头寸损益的披露、编制基础以及相关价格指数要求的披露内容。实务示例14-1：D-MARKET Elektronik Hizmetler ve Ticaret Anonim Şirketi（2023年）土耳其20-F表格[摘录]第三部分[摘录]第19项：附件[摘录]合并财务报表[摘录]…… 恶性通货膨胀 截至2023年、2022年及2021年12月31日合并财务报表附注[摘录]附注2——合并财务报表列报基础[摘录] ### 编制基础[摘录] …… 恶性通货膨胀 Telefónica, S.A.是一家母公司，其子公司经营所在经济体存在恶性通货膨胀。作为母公司，Telefónica, S.A.不会重述比较金额，因此报告了因恶性通货膨胀调整产生的调节项目。实务示例14-2：Telefónica, S.A.（2023年）西班牙2023年合并年度报告[摘录]2023年合并财务报表[摘录]截至2023年12月31日止年度合并财务报表（合并年度账目）附注[摘录]附注25。

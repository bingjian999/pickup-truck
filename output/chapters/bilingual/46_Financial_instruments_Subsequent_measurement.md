subsequent measurement of financial instruments under IFRS 9 Financial Instruments, including the requirements relating to amortised cost, the effective interest method, modifications of ‘financial assets and liabilities’ and ‘foreign currency’ revaluation. The subsequent measurement of financial assets and liabilities is dependent on their classification, which is covered in # Chapter 44. This chapter also discusses fees that are considered an integral part of the effective interest rate (EIR) and provides examples of how the effective interest method is applied to fixed and floating rate instruments, including those with additional features such as prepayment options or an inflation feature. Guidance is also provided on accounting for modifications of financial assets and liabilities that do not result in derecognition, including the treatment of modification fees. Subsequent measurement of contingent consideration recognised by an acquirer in a business combination to which IFRS 3 Business Combinations applies, that falls within the scope of IFRS 9, is discussed in # Chapter 10 at section 7.1.3. The impairment of financial instruments is addressed in Chapter 47. Most, but not all, of the detailed requirements of IFRS accounting standards governing the measurement of fair values are dealt with in IFRS 13 Fair Value Measurement, which is covered in Chapter 15. The measurement of a financial liability with a demand feature is also covered in Chapter 15 at section 10.5. IFRS 9 contains certain requirements addressing fair value measurements of financial instruments and these are covered in sections 2.3 to 2.5 below. What you need to know • Subsequent to initial recognition, financial assets and liabilities are accounted for based on their classification at: (a) amortised cost, (b) fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI) Or (c) fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL). • Amortised cost is determined using the EIR method. This calculation includes all fees and points that are an integral part of the EIR, as well as transaction costs, and all other premiums and discounts. The EIR method amortises all such fees, points, costs, premiums or discounts over the expected life of the instrument rather than the contractual life. • For a fixed rate instrument, changes to estimated cash flows that do not result in derecognition of the instrument are accounted for by discounting the revised cash flows using the original EIR and recognising a catch-up adjustment in profit or loss. For a floating rate instrument, such revisions to estimated cashflows are accounted for by discounting the revised cash flows using a revised EIR with respect to floating features (such as the risk-free rate and, or, the credit spread). • When a financial instrument is modified, and the modification does not result in derecognition, fees incurred or paid in connection with the modification are added to the carrying value of the instrument. IFRS 9 explicitly addresses certain issues, but in other cases judgement needs to be applied to determine the appropriate treatment. • For FVOCI debt instruments, interest income, foreign exchange gains or losses and impairments (calculated based on the amortised cost of the instrument) are recognised immediately in profit or loss. All other changes in fair value are recognised in other comprehensive income (OCI). Fair value changes that have been recognised in OCI are recycled to profit or loss upon derecognition of the debt instrument. • Equity instruments are normally measured at FVTPL. However, for equity instruments designated at FVOCI, gains and losses are recognised in OCI, while dividends are recognised in profit or loss, unless the dividend clearly represents a recovery of part of the cost of the investment. Unlike for FVOCI debt instruments, gains or losses recognised in OCI for equity instruments are never reclassified from equity to profit or loss. Consequently, there is no need to review such investments for impairment. • IAS 21 The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates applies to foreign currency denominated financial instruments. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement 2.1 Debt financial assets measured at amortised cost Financial assets that are measured at amortised cost require the use of the effective interest method and are subject to the IFRS 9 impairment rules. [IFRS 9.5.2.1, IFRS 9.5.2.2]. Gains and losses are recognised in profit or loss when the instrument is derecognised or impaired, through the amortisation process and, if applicable, through modification or reclassification. [IFRS 9.5.7.2]. The effective interest method of accounting is dealt with in section 3 below, foreign currency translation in section 4 below, reclassification of financial assets in section 2.7 below, modification of financial assets in section 3.8 below and impairment is addressed in Chapter 47. 2.2 Financial liabilities measured at amortised cost Liabilities that are measured at amortised cost require the use of the effective interest method with gains or losses recognised in profit or loss when the instrument is derecognised, as well as through the amortisation process and, if applicable, through modification if the modification does not result in derecognition. [IFRS 9.5.3.1, IFRS 9.5.7.2]. The effective interest method of accounting is dealt with in section 3 below, foreign currency retranslation is discussed in section 4 below and modification of financial liabilities is covered in section 3.8 below. 2.3 Debt financial assets measured at fair value through other comprehensive income For financial assets that are debt instruments measured at fair value through other comprehensive income (see Chapter 44 at section 1.1), the IASB decided that both amortised cost and fair value information are relevant because debt instruments held by entities in this measurement category are held for both the collection of contractual cash flows and the realisation of fair values. [IFRS 9.BC4.150]. After initial recognition, investments in debt instruments that are classified as measured at fair value through other comprehensive income are measured at fair value in the statement of financial position (with no deduction for sale or disposal costs). The amounts recognised in profit or loss are the same as the amounts that would have been recognised in profit or loss if the investment had been recorded at amortised cost. [IFRS 9.5.7.11, IFRS 9.B5.7.1A]. For example, the following amounts are recognised in profit or loss: • Impairment gains and losses (see Chapter 47) derived using the same methodology that is applied to financial assets measured at amortised cost. [IFRS 9.5.2.2, IFRS 9.5.5.2]. • Foreign exchange gains and losses (see section 4 below) calculated based on the amortised cost of the debt instruments. [IFRS 9.B5.7.2, IFRS 9.B5.7.2A]. • Interest revenue calculated using the effective interest method (see section 3 below). [IFRS 9.5.4.1]. And • Modification gains or losses for debt instruments that are modified (see section 3.8 below and # Chapter 47 at section 8). [IFRS 9.5.4.3, IFRS 9.5.7.10, IFRS 9.5.7.11]. Furthermore, the subsequent measurement of debt instruments at fair value through other comprehensive income involves the following: • Other fair value gains and losses are recognised in OCI. [IFRS 9.B5.7.1A, IFRS 9.5.7.1(d)]. And • When the debt instruments are derecognised, the cumulative gains or losses previously recognised in OCI are reclassified (i.e., recycled) from equity to profit or loss as a reclassification adjustment. **[IFRS 9.5.7.10].** It follows that the amount recognised in OCI (prior to derecognition) is the difference between the total change in fair value, and the amounts recognised in profit or loss (excluding any amounts received in cash, e.g., the coupon on a bond). [IFRS 9.B5.2.1]. The standard does not explain what happens if the fair value of a financial liability becomes positive, but it is safe to assume that it becomes a financial asset and not a negative liability. Gains and losses arising from remeasuring a financial asset or financial liability at fair value, that is not in a hedging relationship, are normally recognised in profit or loss. [IFRS 9.5.7.1]. However, there is an exception for non-derivative financial liabilities that are designated as measured at fair value through profit or loss. For these liabilities, the element of the gain or loss attributable to changes in credit risk (see section 2.4.1 below) is recognised in other comprehensive income (with the remainder of the fair value change recognised in profit or loss). [IFRS 9.5.7.7]. These amounts presented in other comprehensive income are not subsequently transferred to profit or loss. However, the cumulative gain or loss may be transferred within equity. [IFRS 9.B5.7.9]. This exception does not apply to loan commitments or financial guarantee contracts, nor does it apply if it would create or enlarge an accounting mismatch in profit or loss (see section 2.4.2 below). [IFRS 9.5.7.8, IFRS 9.5.7.9]. In these cases, all changes in the fair value of the liability (including the effects of changes in the credit risk) are recognised in profit or loss. [IFRS 9.B5.7.8]. 2.4.1 Liabilities at fair value through profit or loss: calculating the gain or loss attributable to changes in credit risk IFRS 7 Financial Instruments: Disclosures defines credit risk as: [IFRS 7 Appendix A] Extract from IFRS 7 Appendix A Defined terms credit risk The risk that one party to a financial instrument will cause a financial loss for the other party by failing to discharge an obligation. Credit risk is also part of non-performance risk as defined in IFRS 13 (see Chapter 15 at section 10.3). The change in fair value of a financial liability that is attributable to credit risk relates to the risk that the issuer will fail to pay that liability. It may not solely relate to the creditworthiness of the issuer but may be influenced by other factors, such as collateral. For example, if an entity issues a collateralised liability and a non-collateralised liability that are otherwise identical, the credit risk of those two liabilities will be different, even though they are issued by the same entity. The credit risk on the collateralised liability will be less than the credit risk of the non-collateralised liability. In fact, the credit risk for a collateralised liability may be close to zero. [IFRS 9.B5.7.13]. It is important to distinguish between the terms ‘credit risk’ and ‘the risk of default’ as referred to in the impairment requirements of the standard (see Chapter 47 at section 6.1), since the latter does not include the benefit of collateral. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement For these purposes, credit risk is different from asset-specific performance risk. IFRS 9.B5.7.14 notes that “…Asset-specific performance risk is not related to the risk that an entity will fail to discharge a particular obligation but instead it is related to the risk that a single asset or a group of assets will perform poorly (or not at all)”. [IFRS 9.B5.7.14]. For example, consider the following two situations: [IFRS 9.B5.7.15] (a) A liability with a unit-linking feature whereby the amount due to investors is contractually determined on the basis of the performance of specified assets. The effect of that unit-linking feature on the fair value of the liability is asset-specific performance risk, not credit risk. (b) A liability issued by a structured entity with the following characteristics: • The structured entity is legally isolated so the assets in the structured entity are ring-fenced solely for the benefit of its investors, even in the event of bankruptcy. • The structured entity enters into no other transactions and the assets in the structured entity cannot be hypothecated. And • Amounts are due to the structured entity’s investors only if the ring-fenced assets generate cash flows. Thus, in both cases, changes in the fair value of the liability primarily reflect changes in the fair value of the assets. The effect of the performance of the assets on the fair value of the liability is asset-specific performance risk, not credit risk. Unless an alternative method more faithfully represents the change in fair value of a financial liability that is attributable to credit risk, the standard states that this amount shall be determined as the amount of change in the fair value of the liability that is not attributable to changes in market conditions that give rise to what it defines as ‘market risk’. [IFRS 9.B5.7.16]. Examples of changes in market conditions that give rise to market risk include changes in a benchmark interest rate, the price of another entity’s financial instrument, a commodity price, foreign exchange rate or index of prices or rates. [IFRS 9.B5.7.17]. The standard says that if the only significant relevant changes in market conditions for a financial liability are changes in ‘an observed (benchmark) interest rate’, the amount to be recognised in other comprehensive income can be estimated as follows: [IFRS 9.B5.7.18] (a) First, to arrive at an instrument-specific component of the internal rate of return; the liability’s internal rate of return at the start of the period is computed using the fair value and contractual cash flows at that time and the observed (benchmark) interest rate at the start of the period is deducted from this. (b) Next, the present value of the cash flows associated with the liability is calculated using the liability’s contractual cash flows at the end of the period and a discount rate equal to the sum of the observed (benchmark) interest rate at the end of the period and the instrument-specific component of the internal rate of return at the start of the period as determined in (a). And (c) The difference between the fair value of the liability at the end of the period and the amount determined in (b) is the change in fair value that is not attributable to changes in the observed (benchmark) interest rate and this is the amount to be presented in other comprehensive income. It should be noted that ‘market risk’ is defined to include movements in ‘a benchmark rate’. The latter term is not itself defined but typically it would encompass both risk free rates, such as AAA rated government bond rates or overnight rates, and interbank offer rates (IBOR) such as 3-month EURIBOR, which include an element of credit risk. It would therefore appear that the standard is ambivalent as to whether the amount of change in fair value that is attributable to changes in credit risk of a liability is measured by reference to risk free rates, or by comparison to the credit risk already present in an IBOR. Using the former, the amount will reflect all changes in credit risk of the liability, whereas using the latter, it will only reflect a portion of the changes in credit risk to the liability. Further, the change in credit risk will differ depending on which tenor is selected such as 3-month EURIBOR, 6-month EURIBOR or 12-month EURIBOR. From 2022, most benchmark rates are risk free rates (RFRs) based on actual transactions (see Supplement: IBOR Reform for more details). It would follow that the change in the amount attributable to credit risk reflects all changes in credit risk if using the new RFRs as benchmarks. This method is illustrated in the following Illustration, adapted from that provided in the Illustrative Examples attached to the standard. [IFRS 9.IE1, IFRS 9.IE2, IFRS 9.IE3, IFRS 9.IE4, IFRS 9.IE5]. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement Illustration 2-1: Estimating the change in fair value of an instrument attributable to its credit risk On 1 January 20X1 Company J issues a 10-year bond with a par value of €150,000 and an annual fixed coupon rate of 8%, which is consistent with market rates for bonds with similar characteristics. J uses 3-month EURIBOR as its observable (benchmark) interest rate. At the date of inception of the bond, 3-month EURIBOR is 5%. At the end of the first year: • 3-Month EURIBOR has decreased to 4.75%. And • The fair value of the bond is €153,811 which is consistent with an interest rate of 7.6% (i.e., the remaining cash flows on the bond, €12,000 per year for nine years and €150,000 at the end of nine years, discounted at 7.6% equals €153,811). For simplicity, this Illustration assumes a flat yield curve, that all changes in interest rates result from a parallel shift in the yield curve, and that the changes in 3-month EURIBOR are assumed to be the only relevant changes in market conditions. The amount of change in the fair value of the bond that is not attributable to changes in market conditions that give rise to market risk is estimated as follows: Step (a) The bond’s internal rate of return at the start of the period is 8%. Because the observed (benchmark) interest rate (3-month EURIBOR) is 5%, the instrument-specific component of the internal rate of return is deemed to be 3%. Step (b) The contractual cash flows of the instrument at the end of the period are: • Interest: €12,000 [€150,000 × 8%] per year for each of years 20X2 (year 2) to 20Y0 (year 10). • Principal: €150,000 in 20Y0 (year 10). The discount rate to be used to calculate the present value of the bond is thus 7.75%, which is the 4.75% end of period 3-month EURIBOR rate, plus the 3% instrument-specific component calculated as at the start of the period, which gives a notional present value of €152,367 (€12,000 × (1 - 1.0775-9) ÷ 0.0775 + €150,000 × 1.0775-9), on the assumption that there has been no change in the instrument-specific component. Step (c) The market price of the liability at the end of the period (which will reflect the real instrument-specific component at the end of the period within the 7.6% yield) is €153,811. Therefore, J accounts for €1,444 (€153,811 - €152,367) as the increase in fair value of the bond that is not attributable to changes in market conditions that give rise to market risk. This method assumes that changes in fair value other than those arising from changes in the instrument’s credit risk or from changes in the ‘observed (benchmark) interest rate’ are not significant. It would not be appropriate to use this method if changes in fair value arising from other factors are significant. In such cases, an alternative method should be used that more faithfully measures the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk. For example, if the instrument in the Illustration contained an embedded derivative, the change in fair value of the embedded derivative must be excluded in determining the amount to be presented in other comprehensive income. [IFRS 9.B5.7.19, IFRS 9.B5.7.16(b)]. This ensures that only fair value changes attributable to credit risk are reflected in other comprehensive income and excludes the fair value changes attributable to the embedded derivative. The above method will also produce an amount which includes any changes in the liquidity spread charged by market participants, over say 3-month EURIBOR, since such changes are not considered to be attributable to changes in market conditions that give rise to market risk. This method is applied in practice as the effect of a liquidity spread cannot normally be isolated from that of the credit spread. As with all estimates of fair value, the measurement method used for determining the portion of the change in the liability’s fair value that is attributable to changes in its credit risk should make maximum use of observable market inputs. [IFRS 9.B5.7.20]. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement 2.4.2 Liabilities at fair value through profit or loss: assessing whether an accounting mismatch is created or enlarged If a financial liability is designated as at fair value through profit or loss, it must be determined whether presenting the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk in OCI would create or enlarge an accounting mismatch in profit or loss. The IASB expects this to be a rare occurrence. An accounting mismatch would be created or enlarged if presenting changes in credit risk in OCI would result in a greater mismatch in profit or loss, than if those amounts had been presented in profit or loss. [IFRS 9.B5.7.5, IFRS 9.BC5.39]. In making that determination, an assessment should be made as to whether the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk are expected to be offset in profit or loss by a change in the fair value of another financial instrument measured at fair value through profit or loss. Such an expectation should be based on an economic relationship between the characteristics of the liability and the characteristics of the other financial instrument. [IFRS 9.B5.7.6]. The determination is made at initial recognition and is not reassessed. For practical purposes, all the assets and liabilities giving rise to an accounting mismatch do not need to be entered into at exactly the same time - a reasonable delay is permitted provided that any remaining transactions are expected to occur. An entity’s methodology for making the determination of whether an accounting mismatch is created or enlarged must be applied consistently for similar types of transactions. IFRS 7 requires an entity to provide qualitative disclosures in the notes to the financial statements about its methodology for making the accounting mismatch determination - see Chapter 50 at section 4.4.2. [IFRS 9.B5.7.7]. If an accounting mismatch would be created or enlarged, the entity is required to present all changes in fair value (including the effects of changes in the credit risk of the liability) in profit or loss. In the absence of an accounting mismatch being created or enlarged, the entity is required to present the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk in OCI. [IFRS 9.B5.7.8]. The following Illustration describes a situation in which an accounting mismatch would be created in profit or loss if the effects of changes in the credit risk of the liability were presented in OCI. [IFRS 9.B5.7.10]. Illustration 2-2: Liabilities at fair value through profit or loss: accounting mismatch in profit or loss A mortgage bank provides loans to customers and these are funded by issuing bonds with matching characteristics (e.g., amount outstanding, repayment profile, term and currency) in the market. The loans contain a prepayment feature which allows the mortgage customer to prepay its loan (i.e., satisfy its obligation to the bank) by buying the corresponding bond (which were originally used by the bank to fund the loans) at fair value in the market and delivering that bond to the mortgage bank. As a result of that contractual prepayment right, if the credit quality of the bond worsens (resulting in the fair value of the mortgage bank’s liability decreasing), the fair value of the mortgage bank’s loan asset also decreases. The change in the fair value of the loan asset reflects the mortgage customer’s contractual right to prepay the mortgage loan by buying the underlying bond at fair value (which, in this Illustration, has decreased) and delivering the bond to the mortgage bank. Therefore, the effects of changes in the credit risk of the liability (the bond) will be offset in profit or loss by a corresponding change in the fair value of a financial asset (the loan). If the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk were presented in OCI there would be an accounting mismatch in profit or loss. Therefore, the mortgage bank is required to present all changes in fair value of the liability (including the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk) in profit or loss. In Illustration 2-2 above, there is a contractual link between the effects of changes in the credit risk of the liability and changes in the fair value of the financial asset (i.e., as a result of the mortgage customer’s contractual right to prepay the loan by buying the bond at fair value and delivering the bond to the mortgage bank). The standard states that an accounting mismatch may also occur in the absence of a contractual link, but does not provide any examples of when this might be the case. [IFRS 9.B5.7.11]. However, the standard makes it clear that a mismatch that arises solely as a result of the measurement method does not affect the determination of whether presenting the effects of changes in the liability’s credit risk in OCI would create or enlarge an accounting mismatch. For instance, an entity may not be able to isolate changes in a liability’s credit risk from changes in liquidity risk. Consequently, when the entity presents the combined effect of both factors in OCI, a mismatch may occur because changes in liquidity risk are included in the fair value measurement movement of the entity’s financial assets presented in profit or loss. However, such a mismatch is caused by measurement imprecision, not an offsetting relationship, and, therefore, does not affect the determination of an accounting mismatch. [IFRS 9.B5.7.12]. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement The following Illustration presents another situation in which an accounting mismatch is not due to an economic relationship (in the sense intended by the IASB) between the movement in the fair value of the financial assets and the movement in the fair value of the financial liabilities related to the liabilities’ own credit risk. Illustration 2-3: Liabilities at fair value through profit or loss: no accounting mismatch in profit or loss A bank issues structured products (consisting of a host debt contract and one or more embedded derivatives) in the market. These financial liabilities are designated as at fair value through profit or loss because they are managed on a fair value basis. The bank invests the funds received in highly rated bonds, which are also designated as at fair value through profit or loss, and derivatives whose terms mirror those of the embedded derivatives in the structured products. In periods when market credit spreads are volatile, it is expected that the effect of changes in credit spreads on the fair value of the structured products will be mainly compensated for by the effect of an offsetting credit spread movements on the bonds. This is because the credit quality of the bank is similar to the credit quality of the highly rated bonds in which the bank has invested. Unlike Illustration 2-2 above, there is no direct link between the bank’s own credit risk of the structured financial liabilities and the credit risk of the financial assets. The credit quality of the financial assets may be similar to that of the financial liabilities and these may move in a correlated manner as they are affected by similar movements in the general price of credit and other market risks, but the fair value of the financial assets will not move directly in response to changes in the bank’s own credit risk. Therefore, the requirements for an economic relationship are not met and all fair value changes attributable to changes in the credit risk of the financial liabilities should be taken to OCI. 2.5 Investments in equity instruments designated at fair value through other comprehensive income After initial recognition, investments in equity instruments not held for trading that are designated as measured at fair value through other comprehensive income (see Chapter 44 at section 1.2) are measured at fair value, with no deduction for sale or disposal costs. [IFRS 9.5.7.5]. With the exception of dividends, the associated gains and losses (including any related foreign exchange component) are recognised in OCI. These investments are not subject to impairment testing. Amounts presented in OCI are not subsequently transferred to profit or loss, although the cumulative gain or loss may be transferred within equity. **[IFRS 9.B5.7.1, IFRS 9.B5.7.3].** Extract from IFRS 9 Appendix A Defined terms dividends Distributions of profits to holders of equity instruments in proportion to their holdings of a particular class of capital. [IFRS 9 Appendix A]. Dividends from these investments are recognised in profit or loss when the right to receive payment is established, the amount of the dividend can be measured reliably and it is probable that economic benefits will flow to the entity. [IFRS 9.5.7.1A, IFRS 9.5.7.6]. However, if the dividend clearly represents a recovery of part of the cost of the investment, it is not recognised in profit and loss (see Chapter 9 at section 2.4.1.C). [IFRS 9.B5.7.1]. In the Basis for Conclusions to IFRS 9 the IASB distinguish between dividends that represent a return of investment, and those dividends that are a return on the investment. [IFRS 9.BC5.25(a)]. However, determining when a dividend does or does not clearly represent a recovery of the cost of the investment can be highly judgemental, and a thorough understanding of the particular facts and circumstances is needed. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement 2.6 Unquoted equity instruments and related derivatives IFRS 9 requires all investments in equity instruments and contracts on those instruments to be measured at fair value (see Chapter 15). However, it is recognised that in limited circumstances, cost may be an appropriate estimate of fair value. That may be the case if insufficient recent information is available to determine fair value, or if there is a wide range of possible fair value measurements and cost represents the best estimate of fair value within that range. Frequently asked questions Question 2-1: How should fees received (or paid) upfront to secure a loan commitment be accounted for in the Statement of Comprehensive Income? Fees paid to obtain a loan commitment from an issuer may be paid up-front, over the life of the commitment, or in arrears on the undrawn amount (sometimes called non-utilisation fees). The fees may be paid to obtain a term loan, a line-of-credit, or a revolving debt arrangement. The accounting treatment of all types of fees received or paid depends on whether: • It is probable (more likely than not) at inception that the loan commitment will be utilised in whole or in part. And • Once utilised, the loan commitment expires (that is, if the loan is repaid no further amount can be drawn). Usually, the second criterion is met when the commitment includes a specified repayment schedule. Therefore, this criterion is not met for a revolving debt arrangement or a line-of-credit when the borrower can borrow and repay funds at the borrower’s option numerous times during the life of the loan commitment (see Question 2-4 below). When both criteria above are met, and it is probable that the total loan commitment will be utilised, the fees payable for the loan commitment are recognised by both the issuer and the borrower over the life of the loan using the EIR method. [IFRS 9.B5.4.2(b), IFRS 9.B5.4.3(b)]. As the fees are integral to the EIR they are apportioned consistently with the amounts borrowed and recognised over the resulting loan’s expected life using the EIR method (see section 3.1 below). The exception to this is when the borrower or issuer will account for draw downs on the facility at fair value through profit or loss. In this case, the fees would be recognised in profit or loss when the draw down occurs. Although the guidance in IFRS 9.B5.4.2(b) and IFRS 9.B5.4.3(b) is in the context of the accounting for commitment fees received by the issuer, this guidance can also be applied to the accounting by the borrower. Furthermore, the deferral of the commitment fees paid by the borrower when it is probable there will be a drawdown of a specific loan is also consistent with the definition of an asset in the IASB’s Conceptual Framework because there are potential economic benefits. [CF 4.3, CF 4.4, CF 4.14, CF 4.16]. Question 2-2: How should fees received (or paid) to secure a loan commitment be accounted for when management initially expects only a portion of the loan commitment to be utilised? If both criteria outlined in Question 2-1 above have been met for a portion of the loan commitment only, then the fees are apportioned between the amount expected to be utilised, and the amount not expected to be utilised. The fees related to the amount expected to be utilised are recognised over the resulting loans’ expected life using the EIR method in line with the reasoning outlined in Question 2-1 above. [IFRS 9.B5.4.2(b)]. The remaining portion of the fees is expensed by the borrower over the commitment period, for example on a straight-line basis. This is on the basis that the commitment fees paid by the borrower meet the definition of a prepayment made to the lender for liquidity services made available to the borrower. **[IAS 38.70].** Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement The issuer accounts for the remaining portion of the fee income under IFRS 15 as compensation for the service of providing the facility and recognises it when (or as) it fulfils its performance obligation in making the facility available. [IFRS 9.B5.4.3(b)]. This is on the basis, that the issuer has a performance obligation to stand ready to make the facility available to the borrower (i.e., a promise to provide a service of making a certain amount of cash available to the borrower during the commitment period). [IFRS 15.26(e)]. For each performance obligation identified, it needs to be determined whether it is satisfied over time or at a point in time, and is accounted for accordingly (see Chapter 28). [IFRS 15.32, IFRS 15.35(a)]. Question 2-3: How should fees received (or paid) to secure a loan commitment be accounted for when management does not expect any of the loan commitment to be utilised? In line with the reasoning outlined in Question 2-2 above, if management do not expect any of the loan commitment to be utilised, the fees are expensed by the borrower over the commitment period, for example on a straight-line basis. The issuer accounts for the fee income under IFRS 15 as compensation for the service of providing the facility and recognises it when (or as) it fulfils its performance obligation in making the facility available. Question 2-4: How should fees received (or paid) to secure a loan commitment be accounted for when the loan commitment does not expire after being utilised? Some loan commitments, once drawn, do not expire. For example, access bond and credit card arrangements normally provide a loan facility which can be drawn down, repaid and then drawn again without any additional commitment fees being charged. When this is the case, the fees are not integral to any particular financial asset or liability resulting from draw downs on the loan commitment, and therefore, are not part of any EIR. Consequently, the fees are expensed by the borrower over the commitment period, for example on a straight-line basis. The issuer accounts for the fee income under IFRS 15 as compensation for the service of providing the facility and recognises it when (or as) it fulfils its performance obligation in making the facility available (see Chapter 28). [IFRS 15.35(a)]. Frequently asked questions Question 3-1: Does ‘subsequent reporting period’ refer only to annual periods or does it also extend to interim reporting periods? ‘Reporting period’ refers to a period for which financial statements are produced in accordance with IFRS accounting standards. In some instances, the standards specifically refer to ‘annual reporting period’ (e.g., IFRS 9.7.2.15 and IFRS 9.7.2.33), which indicates that the more general references to ‘reporting period’ extend beyond annual reporting periods. Paragraph 4 of IAS 34 Interim Financial Reporting defines interim period as a ‘financial reporting period shorter than a full financial year’. [IAS 34.4]. Therefore, both interim and annual reporting periods for which financial statements are prepared in compliance with IFRS (full or IAS 34) are ‘reporting periods’. ‘Subsequent reporting period’ therefore refers to the next interim or annual reporting period, whichever comes sooner. In line with the discussions above, it should be noted that if an entity produces quarterly financial statements that are not in compliance with IFRS (full or IAS 34), then the period cannot be considered a ‘reporting period’. Question 3-2: IAS 34.28 requires that the frequency of an entity’s reporting shall not affect the measurement of its annual results. If interim reporting periods are ‘reporting periods’, are the IFRS 9 requirements to give effect to changes between gross and net interest calculation in the ‘subsequent reporting period’ inconsistent with the requirement in IAS 34.28, and in that case, which standard’s guidance should be followed? There is an internal conflict between the requirements of IFRS 9 and IAS 34.28. How we see it In our view, in the absence of further clarity, the specific requirements in IFRS 9 to give effect to changes between the gross and net interest calculation in the ‘subsequent reporting period’ take precedence over the more general requirements of IAS 34.28 that the frequency of an entity’s reporting shall not affect the measurement of its annual results. Question 3-3: Are entities allowed to perform the impairment assessments, with the resulting changes to the interest calculation, more frequently than their interim reporting periods, for example, monthly? The IASB believes that, conceptually, the credit impairment of financial assets should be assessed on an ongoing basis, thus altering the interest revenue determination as the underlying economics change. However, it was noted that such an approach would be unduly onerous for preparers. Therefore, the *Reference: IASB introduced a practical expedient that the assessment of whether a financial asset is credit impaired* be required at the reporting date, with changes in the interest calculation effected from the beginning of the following reporting period. [IFRS 9.BC5.78]. This seems to prevent entities from having to update their staging and interest calculations on an ongoing basis. However, if entities voluntarily perform their staging assessment, and changes to interest calculations, more frequently than their reporting periods, this is not contrary to the objectives of IFRS 9, provided that the approach is applied consistently. How we see it An entity may assess whether a financial asset has become (or ceased to be) credit impaired more frequently than at its reporting dates. If it does so, any resulting changes between gross and net interest income presentation will occur more frequently than its next reporting period. The timing of these assessments is an accounting policy choice and should be evenly spaced and consistently applied. For further guidance on ‘reporting period’ in the context of reclassifications of financial assets and hedge accounting, see Chapter 44 at section 8.1 and Chapter 49 at section 6.4, respectively. Financial instruments: Subsequent measurement 3.7 Financial liabilities 3.7.1 Amortised cost financial liabilities with embedded features Under IFRS 9, most complex financial assets are accounted for at fair value through profit or loss. The treatment of financial liabilities is, however, unchanged from that under IAS 39. The application of the effective interest method to liabilities with embedded derivatives that are deemed closely related to the host, or other embedded features not deemed to meet the definition of a derivative, is not always straightforward or intuitive. Specifically, it is not always clear how to deal with changes in the estimated cash flows of the instrument and in any given situation one needs to assess which is the most appropriate approach: • The general requirements for changes in cash flows set out in IFRS 9.B5.4.6, equivalent to the previous IAS 39 AG8 approach, assuming a fixed original EIR (see section 3.4.1 above). Or • Specific requirements for floating-rate instruments under IFRS 9.B5.4.5, equivalent to the previous IAS 39 AG7 approach (see section 3.3 above). To illustrate the differences between the two approaches, consider an entity that issues a debt instrument for its par value of CU10m which is repayable in ten years’ time on which an annual coupon is payable comprising two elements: a fixed amount of 2.5% of the par value and a variable amount equating to 0.01% of the entity’s annual revenues. The instrument is not designated at fair value through profit or loss and it is judged that the embedded feature is not a derivative as outlined in Illustration 1-5 in Chapter 42 at section 1.1.3. The requirements under IFRS 9.B5.4.5 and IFRS 9.B5.4.6 could give rise to significantly different accounting treatments. In the latter case, the issuer would need to estimate the amount of payments to be made over the life of the bond (which will depend on its estimated revenues for the next ten years) in order to determine the EIR to be applied. Any changes to these estimates would result in a catch-up adjustment, based on the rate as estimated on origination, to profit or loss and the carrying amount of the bond which, potentially, could give rise to significant volatility. In the former case the annual coupon would simply be accrued each year and changes in estimated revenues of future periods would have no impact on the accounting treatment until the applicable year. In 2009, the IFRS IC was asked for guidance on how an issuer should account for a financial liability that contains participation rights by which the instrument holder shares in the net income and losses of the issuer. The holder receives a percentage of the issuer’s net income and is allocated a proportional share of the issuer’s losses. Losses are applied to the nominal value of the instrument to be repaid on maturity. Losses allocated to the holder in one period can be offset by profits in subsequent periods. The submission was asking how the issuer should account for such instruments in periods in which the issuer records a loss and allocates a portion of that loss to the participating financial instruments. Two possible views were submitted to IFRS IC: • In the first view the requirements in IAS 39.AG8 (equivalent to IFRS 9.B5.4.6) do not apply. Rather, the requirements for derecognition should be applied and a gain equal to the allocated loss should be recognised in the current period (with an offsetting reduction in the carrying amount of the instrument). • In a second view, the requirements in IAS 39.AG8 (equivalent to IFRS 9.B5.4.6) apply. Current period losses are one factor that the issuer would consider when it evaluates whether it needs to revise its estimated future cash flows and adjust the carrying amount of the participating financial instrument. There are situations where the ability to exercise these options may be contingent on some future event. The Illustration below shows how an entity may need to revise its estimate of payments in the event that an entity breaches a debt covenant and the loan becomes repayable on demand. Illustration 3-11: Loan repayable on demand due to breach of a covenant Entity X (the borrower) borrows CU1,000 from Bank Y (the lender) at the beginning of 20X1. The loan attracts interest at a floating rate and is repayable in five years’ time (i.e., at the end of 20X5). The borrower incurs transaction costs amounting to CU50. In the event that there is a breach of covenant, the financial liability becomes repayable on demand at the option of the lender. Key assumptions: • The loan is measured at amortised cost. • At the end of the reporting period 20X3, Entity X has breached its debt covenant. • The value of unamortised transaction costs at the end of 20X3 is CU20. Analysis: The loan contract provides Bank Y (the lender) with an option to demand repayment of the loan in the event that Entity X (the borrower) breaches a covenant. In determining the period over which the transaction costs should be amortised, Entity X would have had to assess the probability of the early repayment option being triggered and exercised at initial recognition. This would require an assessment of: a) The likelihood of the option becoming exercisable (taking into account the probability of Entity X breaching covenants). And b) The likelihood of the bank exercising its option in that instance. This estimate could be determined using a most likely outcome approach or a probability-weighted approach, depending on the specific facts and circumstances. Any subsequent revision of the estimates of cash flows would need to be accounted for in accordance with IFRS 9.B5.4.6. As a result of Entity X breaching its covenant at the end of 20X3 and the liability becoming repayable on demand at the option of the Bank Y, Entity X, as part of its re-estimation of the cash flows, would need to update its assessment of whether the bank is expected to exercise its option to demand repayment. The period over which the transaction costs are to be amortised would need to be updated accordingly. The gross carrying amount of the financial liability would need to be updated for any change in such estimate, with any consequential adjustment being recognised in profit or loss. [IFRS 9.B5.4.6]. In some cases, the entity may not have sufficient information to determine whether the lender will demand repayment of the loan, in which case it may be appropriate to accelerate the amortisation of the remaining unamortised transaction costs. The assumptions that the borrower makes regarding whether the bank will demand repayment, should be consistent with the assumptions used for assessing the entity’s ability to continue as a going concern. How we see it In the Illustration above, the loan (financial liability) is measured initially at fair value and subsequently at amortised cost. If the loan was subsequently measured at fair value, then the entity would have to consider IFRS 13.47 which requires that the fair value of a financial liability with a demand feature, is not less than the amount payable on demand, discounted from the first date that the amount could be required to be paid. However, as illustrated above, where a loan that is subsequently measured at amortised cost becomes repayable on demand at some point after initial recognition, e.g., as result of a covenant breach, then the principles of IFRS 13.47 would not be applicable. Therefore, the amortisation of the remaining unamortised transaction costs would not necessarily need to be accelerated such that the loan reflects the amount that could be demanded by the bank. Rather in such a case, same as before the covenant breach, the amortised cost loan would need to be remeasured taking into account the entity’s revised estimate of cash flows. This estimate would involve the use of judgement or assumptions based on the latest available, reliable information, including the status of negotiations with the bank and how this impacts the probability and timing of the bank demanding repayment. The gross carrying amount of the financial asset is recalculated as the present value of the renegotiated or modified contractual cash flows that are discounted at the financial asset’s original effective interest rate (or credit-adjusted effective interest rate for purchased or originated credit-impaired financial assets - see section 3.1.2 above). Any costs or fees incurred adjust the carrying amount of the modified financial asset and are amortised over the remaining term of the modified financial asset (see section 3.8.2 below). [IFRS 9.5.4.3]. This results in a revision of the original EIR (see section 3.8.2 below). The accounting for modifications of financial assets that do not result in derecognition is similar to changes in estimates (see section 3.4.1 above), i.e., the original EIR is retained and there is a catch-up adjustment to profit or loss for the change in expected cash flows discounted at the original EIR. [IFRS 9.B5.4.6]. For example, this would be applicable where a borrower is granted a concession to delay interest payments if the entity concludes that the change in the terms is not a substantial modification and therefore does not result in the derecognition of the original loan and the recognition of a new loan. In October 2017, the Board added wording to the Basis for Conclusions of IFRS 9 as part of the amendment for prepayment features with negative compensation (see Chapter 44 at section 5.4.4.A). This addition states that the requirements in IFRS 9 for adjusting the amortised cost of a financial liability when a modification (or exchange) does not result in the derecognition of the financial liability are consistent with the requirements for adjusting the gross carrying amount of a financial asset when a modification does not result in the derecognition of the financial asset. It adds that the requirements in IFRS 9 therefore provide an adequate basis for an entity to account for modifications and exchanges of financial liabilities that do not result in derecognition. [IFRS 9.BC4.252, IFRS 9.BC4.253]. Therefore IFRS 9 requires modifications of financial liabilities that do not result in derecognition to be accounted for similarly to modifications of financial assets. The amortised cost of the financial liability should be recalculated by computing the present value of estimated future contractual cash flows that are discounted at the financial instrument’s original EIR. Any consequent adjustment should be recognised immediately in profit or loss. Any costs or fees incurred adjust the carrying amount of the liability and are amortised over the remaining term of the modified liability. [IFRS 9.B3.3.6]. This results in a revision of the original EIR (see section 3.8.2 below for more details on the treatment of modification fees, and the examples in Chapter 48 at section 6.2.5 and section 6.2.6). In certain instances judgement may need to be applied to identify whether the substance of a payment made by a borrower to the lender represents additional interest or payment of principal to be accounted for as a modification of cash flows (in line with IFRS 9.BC4.253) rather than a transaction cost (in line with IFRS 9.B3.3.6) (see also Chapter 48 at section 6.2.5 to 6.2.7). The accounting treatment is clear in some of the fact patterns whilst an element of interpretation is required in others. Illustration 3-12: Changes in credit spread Fact Pattern 1: A ratchet loan with a rate reset Bank A issues a ratchet loan whereby the credit spread is increased in accordance with a scale of predetermined rates, on the occurrence of one or more predetermined events that are linked to the borrower’s financial covenants (e.g., debt and equity or interest coverage ratios). These clauses are included to avoid the need to renegotiate the loan when the credit risk of the borrower changes and are considered to meet the standard’s requirements for the loan to be recorded at amortised cost for the holder. Analysis: The credit spread is reset to reflect changes in credit risk and credit spreads are a component of a market interest rate as indicated by the January 2016 IFRS IC agenda decision (see section 3.1 above). Consequently, it would be appropriate to apply the guidance in B5.4.5. and account for the changes in the EIR prospectively on an accrual basis. [IFRS 9.B5.4.5]. How we see it For a vanilla floating rate loan (e.g., at EURIBOR plus 2%), it is widely accepted that resetting only the benchmark component of the rate would be regarded as a change in EIR in accordance with IFRS 9.B5.4.5. Similarly, in our view, resetting only the credit spread component when the reset is predetermined at inception can be regarded as a change in EIR as it is considered a component of the market interest rate. Although the amount of the reset is specified at inception rather than being the market credit spread at the reset date, this may be accounted for as a reset of the EIR provided that the predetermined rate changes reasonably approximate the market rates for the different credit qualities as observed at inception. Consequently, the statement of financial position measurement of a foreign currency financial instrument is determined as follows: • First, it is recorded and measured in the foreign currency in which it is denominated, whether it is carried at fair value, cost, or amortised cost. • Second, that amount is retranslated to the entity’s functional currency using the closing rate. **[IAS 21.23].** Profit and loss items associated with financial instruments, e.g., dividends receivable, interest payable or receivable and impairments, are recorded at the spot rate ruling when they arise (although average rates may be used when they represent an appropriate approximation to spot rates throughout the period). The reporting of exchange differences in profit or loss or in other comprehensive income depends on whether the instrument is a monetary item (e.g., debt instruments) or a non-monetary item (e.g., an equity investment), and whether it is designated as part of a foreign currency cash flow hedge. (This excludes the translation of foreign entities which is addressed in section 4.2 below.) Foreign exchange differences arising on retranslating monetary items, including debt instruments measured at fair value through other comprehensive income, are generally recognised in profit or loss. However, those exchange differences must be recognised in other comprehensive income for instruments designated as a hedging instrument in a cash flow hedge or in a hedge of a net investment in a foreign operation (except to the extent that there is hedge ineffectiveness). Exchange differences must also be recorded in other comprehensive income for a fair value hedge of an equity instrument for which an entity has elected to present changes in fair value in other comprehensive income (see section 2.5 above and # Chapter 49). [IFRS 9.B5.7.2, IFRS 9.B5.7.2A]. Any changes in the carrying amount of a non-monetary item are recognised in profit or loss or in other comprehensive income in accordance with IFRS 9. For example, for an instrument that is measured at fair value through profit or loss, all subsequent fair value changes, including the effect of foreign exchange, are recognised in profit or loss. Similarly, for an investment in an equity instrument where an entity has made an irrevocable election to present in other comprehensive income subsequent changes in the fair value of the investment, the entire change in the carrying amount, including the effect of changes in foreign currency rates, is presented in other comprehensive income. [IFRS 9.5.7.5, IFRS 9.B5.7.3, IFRS 9.IG E.3.4]. In cases where some portion of the change in carrying amount is recognised in profit or loss and some in other comprehensive income, e.g., if the amortised cost of a foreign currency bond measured at fair value through other comprehensive income has increased in the foreign currency (resulting in a gain in profit or loss) but its fair value has decreased in the foreign currency (resulting in a loss recognised in other comprehensive income), those two components cannot be offset for the purposes of determining gains or losses that should be recognised in profit or loss or in other comprehensive income. [IFRS 9.IG E.3.4]. - **中文:** (a) 首先，为得出内含报酬率中工具特定组成部分，需使用期初公允价值及合同现金流量计算负债期初内含报酬率，并从中扣除期初已观察（基准）利率。 (b) 其次，使用期末负债合同现金流量，以及等于期末已观察（基准）利率与按(a)确定的期初内含报酬率中工具特定组成部分之和的折现率，计算负债相关现金流量的现值。 (c) 期末负债公允价值与按(b)确定的金额之间的差额，即为不属于已观察（基准）利率变动导致的公允价值变动，该金额应计入其他综合收益。

**中文:** 需要注意的是，“市场风险”被定义为包含“基准利率”的变动。后者本身并未被定义，但通常涵盖无风险利率（如AAA级政府债券利率或隔夜利率）以及包含一定信用风险成分的银行间拆借利率（IBOR），例如3个月期欧元银行间拆借利率（EURIBOR）。因此，该准则似乎对于因负债信用风险变动导致的公允价值变动金额，是参照无风险利率计量，还是通过与IBOR中已存在的信用风险进行比较来计量，持模棱两可的态度。若采用前者，该金额将反映负债信用风险的所有变动；而采用后者，则仅反映负债信用风险变动的一部分。此外，信用风险的变动将因所选期限的不同而有所差异，例如3个月期EURIBOR、6个月期EURIBOR或12个月期EURIBOR。自2022年起，大多数基准利率均为基于实际交易的无风险利率（RFR）（详见补充材料：IBOR改革）。由此可推断，若以新的无风险利率作为基准，则归属于信用风险的变动金额将反映所有信用风险变动。以下示例改编自准则所附的示例说明，展示了这一方法。[IFRS 9.IE1, IFRS 9.IE2, IFRS 9.IE3, IFRS 9.IE4, IFRS 9.IE5] 金融工具：后续计量 示例2-1：估计归属于信用风险的金融工具公允价值变动 20X1年1月1日，J公司发行了一笔面值为150,000欧元、年固定票面利率为8%的10年期债券，该利率与具有类似特征债券的市场利率一致。J公司使用3个月期EURIBOR作为其可观察（基准）利率。在债券初始确认日，3个月期EURIBOR为5%。第一年末： • 3个月期EURIBOR已下降至4.75%。

**中文:** 国际会计准则理事会（IASB）预计这种情况很少发生。如果将信用风险变动计入其他综合收益（OCI）所导致的损益错配程度，大于将该金额计入损益所产生的错配，则会产生或扩大会计错配。[IFRS 9.B5.7.5, IFRS 9.BC5.39]。在作出该判断时，应评估负债信用风险变动的影响是否预期会被另一项以公允价值计量且其变动计入损益的金融工具的公允价值变动在损益中抵消。此类预期应基于负债特征与另一金融工具特征之间的经济关系。[IFRS 9.B5.7.6]。该判断在初始确认时作出，且不得重新评估。为实务操作之目的，产生会计错配的所有资产和负债无需在同一时点确认——只要预期剩余交易将发生，允许存在合理的时间延迟。主体用于判断会计错配是否产生或扩大的方法，必须对类似类型的交易一致应用。IFRS 7要求主体在财务报表附注中对其判断会计错配的方法进行定性披露——参见第50章第4.4.2节。[IFRS 9.B5.7.7]。若会计错配将产生或扩大，主体须将所有公允价值变动（包括负债信用风险变动的影响）计入损益。若未产生或扩大会计错配，主体须将负债信用风险变动的影响计入其他综合收益。[IFRS 9.B5.7.8]。

**中文:** 以下示例描述了一种情形：若将负债信用风险变动的影响计入其他综合收益，则会在损益中产生会计错配。[IFRS 9.B5.7.10] 示例2-2：以公允价值计量且其变动计入当期损益的负债：损益中的会计错配 某抵押银行向客户发放贷款，并通过在市场上发行具有匹配特征（如未偿还金额、还款计划、期限及币种）的债券进行融资。这些贷款包含提前还款条款，允许抵押贷款客户通过按公允价值在市场上购买相应债券（该债券最初由银行用于为贷款融资）并将其交付给抵押银行，从而提前偿还贷款（即履行其对银行的义务）。基于该合同提前还款权，若债券信用质量恶化（导致抵押银行负债的公允价值下降），抵押银行贷款资产的公允价值也会随之下降。贷款资产公允价值的变动反映了抵押贷款客户通过按公允价值购买标的债券（在本示例中该债券公允价值已下降）并将其交付给抵押银行以提前偿还抵押贷款的合同权利。因此，负债（债券）信用风险变动的影响将在损益中由金融资产（贷款）公允价值的相应变动所抵销。若将负债信用风险变动的影响计入其他综合收益，则会在损益中产生会计错配。因此，抵押银行须将负债公允价值的所有变动（包括负债信用风险变动的影响）均计入损益。

**中文:** 在上述示例2-2中，负债信用风险变动的影响与金融资产公允价值变动的影响之间存在合同关联（即，由于抵押贷款客户拥有合同权利，可通过按公允价值购买债券并将该债券交付给抵押银行来提前偿还贷款）。该准则指出，即使不存在合同关联，也可能发生会计错配，但未提供任何此类情形的示例。[IFRS 9.B5.7.11] 然而，该准则明确说明，仅因计量方法产生的错配，不影响确定将负债信用风险变动的影响计入其他综合收益是否会形成或扩大会计错配。例如，主体可能无法将负债信用风险变动与流动性风险变动区分开来。因此，当主体将两者的综合影响计入其他综合收益时，可能会产生错配，因为流动性风险变动已包含在主体计入损益的金融资产公允价值计量变动中。但此类错配源于计量不精确，而非抵销关系，因此不影响会计错配的判定。[IFRS 9.B5.7.12] 金融工具：后续计量 以下示例展示了另一种情形，即金融资产公允价值变动与金融负债公允价值变动（涉及负债自身信用风险）之间的会计错配并非源于经济关系（按国际会计准则理事会的意图理解）。

**中文:** 示例2-3：按公允价值计入损益的负债：损益中无会计错配 某银行在市场上发行结构性产品（由主债务合同及一项或多项嵌入衍生工具构成）。

**中文:** 这些金融负债被指定为以公允价值计量且其变动计入当期损益，因其按公允价值基础进行管理。银行将所收资金投资于高评级债券（同样被指定为以公允价值计量且其变动计入当期损益）以及条款与结构性产品内嵌衍生工具相匹配的衍生工具。在市场信用利差波动期间，预计信用利差变动对结构性产品公允价值的影响，将主要通过债券上抵消性信用利差变动的效应得到补偿。这是因为银行的信用质量与其所投资的高评级债券的信用质量相似。与上述示例2-2不同，银行自身结构性金融负债的信用风险与金融资产的信用风险之间并无直接关联。金融资产的信用质量可能与金融负债相似，且两者可能因受一般信用价格及其他市场风险的类似变动影响而呈相关变动，但金融资产的公允价值不会因银行自身信用风险的变化而直接变动。因此，经济关系的要求未得到满足，所有因金融负债信用风险变动产生的公允价值变动均应计入其他综合收益。 2.5 指定为以公允价值计量且其变动计入其他综合收益的权益工具投资 初始确认后，非交易性且被指定为以公允价值计量且其变动计入其他综合收益的权益工具投资（见第44章第1.2节），应按公允价值计量，且不扣除销售或处置成本。[IFRS 9.5.7.5] 除股利外，相关利得和损失（包括任何相关汇兑成分）均在其他综合收益中确认。

**中文:** 在后一种情况下，发行人需要估计债券存续期内需支付的款项金额（该金额取决于其未来十年的预计收入），以确定适用的实际利率。对这些估计的任何变更将导致基于初始估计利率的追溯调整，影响损益及债券账面价值，并可能引发重大波动。在前一种情况下，每年只需简单计提年度票息，未来期间预计收入的变化在适用年度之前不会对会计处理产生影响。2009年，国际财务报告准则解释委员会被问及，发行人应如何核算包含参与权的金融负债——此类权利使工具持有人分享发行人的净损益。持有人按比例获取发行人净收益，并承担相应比例的损失。损失从到期应偿还的工具名义价值中扣除。某一期间分配给持有人的损失可由后续期间的利润抵补。该问题询问：当发行人发生亏损并将部分亏损分配给参与型金融工具时，应如何进行会计处理。向国际财务报告准则解释委员会提交了两种可能观点： • 第一种观点认为，不应适用《国际会计准则第39号》AG8段（等同于《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.6段）的规定，而应采用终止确认要求，在当期确认等于分配损失金额的利得（同时冲减工具账面价值）。 • 第二种观点认为，应适用《国际会计准则第39号》AG8段（等同于《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.6段）的规定。

**中文:** 当期亏损是发行人在评估是否需要修正其预计未来现金流量并调整参与型金融工具账面价值时考虑的因素之一。国际财务报告准则解释委员会指出，国际会计准则第39号应用指南第AG6段和AG8段（对应《国际财务报告准则第9号》第B5.4.4段和B5.4.6段）为采用实际利率法按摊余成本计量金融负债提供了相关应用指引。解释委员会同时指出，由于该负债尚未终止确认，因此不宜类推适用国际会计准则第39号中的终止确认指引。故当期不应确认与已分配损失等额的利得。相反，当发行人评估是否需要修正预计未来现金流量并调整参与型金融工具账面价值时，应将当期亏损与未来预期一并考虑。需注意，解释委员会在审议该问题时未重新考虑来函所述假设，即该金融负债：(a) 不包含任何嵌入式衍生工具；(b) 采用实际利率法按摊余成本计量；(c) 不符合浮动利率工具的定义。8 《国际财务报告准则解释委员会最新动态》，2009年5月，载于国际财务报告准则基金会网站。金融工具：后续计量 我们的观点 我们认为，在此类情形下，通常不宜适用《国际财务报告准则第9号》第B5.4.5段的要求，主要原因在于主体的收入并非反映市场利率变动的浮动利率。然而，《国际财务报告准则第9号》并未就计算实际利率时如何估计预期现金流量提供明确指引。因此，实际利率应通过估计最终产生的现金流量来确定。这可能涉及运用基于最新可靠信息的判断或假设。

**中文:** 预期现金流量可采用最可能结果法或概率加权法进行估计。所使用的估计技术应保持一致性，除非会计估计所依据的情况发生变化，或因获取新信息、出现新进展或积累更多经验而需调整。在确定采用何种估计技术（即最可能结果法或概率加权法）时，主体可参考《国际会计准则第37号——准备、或有负债和或有资产》中关于确定最佳估计数的原则（见第27章第4.1节），以及《国际财务报告解释公告第23号——所得税处理的不确定性》中的原则（该解释公告要求主体采用能更好预测不确定性解决方式的方法，见第29章第9.4节）。对于某些金融负债，其现金流量的重新估计仅反映市场利率变动，但《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.5段的应用可能并不恰当。例如"反向浮动利率债券"，其票息按固定利率减去欧元银行间同业拆借利率（下限为零）支付。对于某些金融负债，可考虑同时采用《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.5段和B5.4.6段的方法。例如上述情形中，负债包含参与特征及浮动利率要素时，实际利率应为当前浮动利率与初始预期收益假设的组合。预期收益的修订适用《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.6段，而剩余利息票息部分则采用浮动利率（另见上文第3.3节及下文第3.8.1节中需同时采用《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.5段和B5.4.6段的其他情形说明）。

**中文:** 除判断IFRS 9.B5.4.5或IFRS 9.B5.4.6是否适用外，还存在进一步复杂性，涉及初始预期现金流量估计以及（如需）修订现金流量时的重新估计。此外，在区分部分终止确认与合同修改、以及核算因修改原合同产生的费用和成本时，亦存在难点。这些复杂性在很大程度上已在IFRS 9实施后审议（PIR）中提出（见下文第3.8.4节）。 3.7.2 具有合同联动特征的摊余成本金融负债 另一复杂领域涉及证券化结构中的金融负债会计处理：特殊目的实体（SPE）投资于贷款组合，并通过向投资者发行上市无追索权票据筹集资金。SPE对上市票据的合同义务与贷款表现（扣除利差后）挂钩。此类合同联动特征（即贷款产生的任何损失将由票据持有人承担）将作为上市票据负债的一部分进行核算。因此，票据的实际利率（EIR）将反映因基础贷款资产信用损失导致的预期现金短缺估计（因其影响票据的合同付款要求）[IFRS 9.BCZ5.67]。 关于计算EIR时如何估计预期现金流量，IFRS 9未提供明确指引。因此，EIR应通过估计最终产生的现金流量来确定。这可能涉及基于最新可用可靠信息运用判断或假设[IAS 8.32]。预期现金流量可采用最可能结果法或概率加权法进行估计。

**中文:** 在确定使用何种估计技术（即最可能结果或概率加权法）时，主体可参考《国际会计准则第37号》中关于确定最佳估计的原则（见第27章第4.1节），以及《国际财务报告解释公告第23号》中的原则——该解释公告要求主体采用能更好预测不确定性解决方式的方法（见第29章第9.4节）。所使用的估计技术应保持一致性，除非会计估计所依据的情况发生变化，或因新信息、新进展或更多经验导致变更。[《国际会计准则第8号》第34段] 因联动特征对已列报票据的估计合同现金流量进行的任何修订，应作为对票据持有人负债摊余成本（按实际利率折现）的调整进行会计处理，并计入损益（详见第47章第7.7节）。[《国际财务报告准则第9号》B5.4.6段]

**中文:** **金融工具：后续计量 3.7.3 具有或有需求特征的摊余成本金融负债** 如上述第3.4节所述，实际利率的定义明确要求主体在确定金融工具的实际利率时，需考虑提前还款权、赎回权及类似选择权。在某些情况下，行使这些选择权的能力可能取决于未来事件的发生。下图展示了当主体违反债务契约且贷款变为可随时要求偿还时，主体可能需要如何修订其付款估计。

**中文:** **图示3-11：因违反契约而可随时要求偿还的贷款** 主体X（借款人）于20X1年初从银行Y（贷款人）借入CU1,000。该贷款按浮动利率计息，期限为五年（即于20X5年末到期）。借款人发生交易成本CU50。

**中文:** 若发生契约违约，金融负债将根据贷款人选择而成为即期应付。关键假设：• 该贷款按摊余成本计量。• 在20X3年度报告期末，实体X已违反其债务契约。• 20X3年末未摊销交易成本的价值为CU20。分析：贷款合同赋予银行Y（贷款人）在实体X（借款人）违反契约时要求立即偿还贷款的选择权。在确定交易成本应摊销的期间时，实体X需在初始确认时评估提前还款选择权被触发及行使的可能性。这需要评估：a) 选择权变为可行使的可能性（考虑实体X违反契约的概率）；b) 银行在该情况下行使选择权的可能性。该估计可采用最可能结果法或概率加权法确定，具体取决于特定事实和情况。后续对现金流量估计的任何修订均需根据IFRS 9.B5.4.6进行会计处理。由于实体X在20X3年末违反契约，且负债根据银行Y的选择成为即期应付，实体X在重新估计现金流量时，需更新对银行是否预期行使还款选择权的评估。交易成本摊销期间需相应更新。金融负债的账面总额需根据此类估计的任何变动进行调整，相关调整计入损益。[IFRS 9.B5.4.6]

**中文:** 在某些情况下，主体可能没有足够的信息来确定贷款人是否会要求偿还贷款，此时加速摊销剩余未摊销交易成本可能是适当的。借款人就银行是否会要求还款所做的假设，应与评估主体持续经营能力时所采用的假设保持一致。我们的观点 在上述示例中，贷款（金融负债）初始按公允价值计量，后续按摊余成本计量。若该贷款后续按公允价值计量，则主体需考虑《国际财务报告准则第13号》第47段的规定，该规定要求具有即期特征的金融负债的公允价值不得低于可要求即期支付金额（自可要求支付首日起折现）。然而，如上所述，若后续按摊余成本计量的贷款在初始确认后某个时点（例如因违反契约）变为可要求即期偿还，则《国际财务报告准则第13号》第47段的原则将不适用。因此，剩余未摊销交易成本的摊销未必需要加速至使贷款反映银行可要求偿还的金额。相反，在此情况下，与违反契约前相同，摊余成本贷款需根据主体对现金流量的修订估计进行重新计量。该估计需运用判断或基于最新可用可靠信息（包括与银行谈判的进展及其对银行要求还款概率和时间的影响）的假设。

**中文:** 金融工具：后续计量 上述示例说明了当主体违反契约条款导致贷款变为按要求偿还时，如何修订现金流量时间估计。在确定使用的估计技术（即最可能结果法或概率加权法）时，主体可参考《国际会计准则第37号》中关于确定最佳估计的原则（见第27章第4.1节），以及《国际财务报告解释公告第23号》中要求主体采用能更好预测不确定性解决方式的方法（见第29章第9.4节）。主体还需根据贷款变为按要求偿还的情况更新流动性风险披露，详见第50章第5.4节。 负债在财务状况表中应列报为流动负债还是非流动负债的评估，与计量考虑因素相互独立。在上述示例中，鉴于违约发生在报告期末之前，且截至期末未完成贷款条款的重新谈判，该贷款应归类为流动负债。无论主体是否预期银行要求还款，或是否预期贷款条款将重新谈判，此分类均适用。详见第3章第3.1.4节。

**中文:** 3.8 修改后的金融资产与金融负债 3.8.1 不导致终止确认的修改的会计处理 当金融资产的合同现金流量被重新谈判或以其他方式修改，且该修改未导致该金融资产终止确认时（见第48章第3.5.1节），主体应重新计算金融资产的账面总额，并在损益中确认修改利得或损失。

**中文:** 金融资产的账面总额重新计算为按该金融资产原始实际利率（或购入或源生信用减值金融资产的信用调整后实际利率——见上文第3.1.2节）折现的重新谈判或修改后合同现金流量的现值。产生的任何成本或费用将调整修改后金融资产的账面金额，并在该修改后金融资产的剩余期限内摊销（见下文第3.8.2节）。[IFRS 9.5.4.3] 这导致对原始实际利率的修订（见下文第3.8.2节）。未导致终止确认的金融资产修改的会计处理与估计变更类似（见上文第3.4.1节），即保留原始实际利率，并对按原始实际利率折现的预期现金流量变动进行损益的追溯调整。[IFRS 9.B5.4.6] 例如，当借款人获得利息支付延迟的优惠，且主体认定条款变更并非实质性修改，因此不导致原贷款终止确认及新贷款确认时，适用该处理。2017年10月，理事会作为负补偿提前还款特征修订的一部分（见第44章第5.4.4.A节），在IFRS 9结论基础中增加了措辞。该措辞指出，当修改（或交换）未导致金融负债终止确认时，IFRS 9中调整金融负债摊余成本的要求，与当修改未导致金融资产终止确认时调整金融资产账面总额的要求一致。

**中文:** 该准则进一步指出，IFRS 9中的规定为实体核算未导致终止确认的金融负债修改和交换提供了充分依据[IFRS 9.BC4.252, IFRS 9.BC4.253]。因此，IFRS 9要求对未导致终止确认的金融负债修改采用与金融资产修改类似的会计处理方法。金融负债的摊余成本应通过按金融工具原始实际利率折现的预计未来合同现金流量现值重新计算，由此产生的任何调整应立即计入损益。发生的任何成本或费用应调整负债的账面金额，并在修改后负债的剩余期限内摊销[IFRS 9.B3.3.6]。这将导致原始实际利率的修订（有关修改费用处理的更多详情，请参见下文第3.8.2节，以及第48章第6.2.5节和第6.2.6节的示例）。在某些情况下，可能需要运用判断来识别借款人向贷款人支付的款项实质上是额外利息还是本金偿还，从而应作为现金流量修改（符合IFRS 9.BC4.253）而非交易成本（符合IFRS 9.B3.3.6）进行核算（另见第48章第6.2.5节至第6.2.7节）。金融工具：后续计量 当金融工具条款发生修改时，应用准则中关于修改的指引会引发对"原始实际利率"实际含义的疑问，特别是当原始合同条款引入某种形式的利率可变性时。

**中文:** 这引发了更普遍的问题：假设实际利率包含可变组成部分，何时适合根据IFRS 9.B5.4.5对实际利率采用预期变更，而非采用固定实际利率。以下示例重点关注条款变更是否构成修改，或者是否适合应用IFRS 9.B5.4.5。部分事实模式下的会计处理较为明确，而其他情况则需要一定程度的解读。这些示例从贷款人角度阐述会计处理，但同样适用于金融资产和金融负债，因为如上所述，两者适用相同的修改要求。

**中文:** 示例3-12：信用利差变动 事实模式1：具有利率重置的棘轮贷款 银行A发放了一笔棘轮贷款，其中信用利差根据预定利率表，在发生一个或多个与借款人财务契约（例如债务与权益比率或利息覆盖比率）相关的预定事件时上调。加入这些条款是为了避免在借款人信用风险变化时重新谈判贷款，并被认为符合准则对持有方以摊余成本记录贷款的要求。

**中文:** 分析：信用利差重置以反映信用风险变化，而信用利差是市场利率的组成部分，如2016年1月国际财务报告准则解释委员会议程决议所示（见上文第3.1节）。因此，适合应用B5.4.5中的指引，并按权责发生制对实际利率的变动进行预期会计处理。[IFRS 9.B5.4.5]

**中文:** 我们的观点 对于普通浮动利率贷款（例如，欧元银行间同业拆借利率加2%），普遍认为仅重置利率的基准组成部分应视为根据IFRS 9.B5.4.5对实际利率的变更。

**中文:** 同理，我们认为，若初始确认时已预设仅重置信用利差部分，则该重置可视为市场利率组成部分的变动，从而构成实际利率的调整。尽管重置金额在初始确认时已明确（而非基于重置日的市场信用利差），但只要预设利率变动与初始确认时观察到的不同信用质量对应的市场利率合理近似，即可按实际利率重置进行会计处理。

**中文:** **事实模式2：通过提高信用利差重新协商贷款契约** B公司持有一笔按EURIBOR加2%计息的定期贷款（假设无交易成本、费用、折价或溢价，实际利率同为EURIBOR加2%）。B公司即将违反某项契约条款，且通常无法在违约时提前还款，因此与贷款人重新协商，通过将贷款适用的信用利差提高至3%来调整契约条款。修订后的信用利差反映了贷款条款的变更及B公司当前的信用风险。

**中文:** **分析：** 利率变动完全源于条款重新协商，且该变动在初始确认时未在合同中约定。除非原始条款明确规定信用利差将根据特定事件按预设利率重置，否则该变动应根据《国际财务报告准则第9号》第5.4.3条作为合同修改处理。这意味着需保留原实际利率（EURIBOR加2%），并基于按该利率折现的利差变动计算并确认利得或损失。[IFRS 9.5.4.3]

**中文:** **示例3-13：修改——问题债务重组** B公司取得一笔固定利率定期贷款。数年后，公司陷入财务困境，与贷款人重新协商以减少或递延部分到期还款额。

**中文:** 贷款条款的变更不被视为导致实质性修改，因此不应对整笔贷款进行终止确认。此次重新谈判也不作为部分终止确认处理（见第48章第3.4节和第3.5.1节）。分析：由于付款变更并非因市场利率变动所致，且该变更在合同初始时未作规定，因此应根据《国际财务报告准则第9号》第5.4.3条将其视为一项修改。这意味着保留原始固定利率实际利率法，从而基于按该利率折现的合同付款变动计算并确认利得或损失。**[《国际财务报告准则第9号》第5.4.3条]。**

**中文:** 金融工具：后续计量 示例3-14：修改——固定利率贷款的重新谈判 事实模式1：不可提前偿还的固定利率贷款重新谈判，涉及部分利率重置 公司C持有一笔固定利率贷款已有数年，在此期间市场利率已下降。该贷款条款未赋予公司任何提前偿还能力。公司C与银行重新谈判贷款，以降低未来利息支付，反映利率中基准利率组成部分的部分重置。贷款的信用风险或信用利差未发生变化，且条款无其他变更。 分析：利率变动完全源于条款的重新谈判，且不存在允许公司C提前偿还并以修订后利率重新签订新贷款的现有提前还款选择权。因此，该变更应根据《国际财务报告准则第9号》第5.4.3条视为一项修改。这意味着保留原始固定利率实际利率法，从而基于按原始固定实际利率折现的利率变动计算并确认利得或损失。[《国际财务报告准则第9号》第5.4.3条]。

**中文:** 某贷款条款被修改，按初始确认时确定的原始实际利率折现后的修订合同现金流量为CU5,000（两年后应收CU6,050，按年利率10%折现）。若银行因修改发生外部成本CU50（例如，用于抵押品估值及法律服务），则原始实际利率10%将修订为约9%，即按此折现率将两年后应收CU6,050折现至现值CU5,050。

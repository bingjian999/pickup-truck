[IAS 20.1]. The distinction between government grants and other forms of government assistance is important because the standard’s accounting requirements only apply to the former. Government grants are a specific form of government assistance and relate to the transfers of resources to an entity in return for past or future compliance with certain conditions relating to the entity’s operating activities. [IAS 20.3]. The purpose of government grants, which may be called subsidies, subventions or premiums, [IAS 20.6], and other forms of government assistance is often to encourage a private sector entity to take a course of action that it would not normally have taken if the assistance had not been provided. [IAS 20.4]. Government grants are also used to provide financial support as was abundantly illustrated during the coronavirus pandemic when governments all over the world introduced measures to provide financial assistance to businesses in an effort to mitigate the significant impact of the pandemic on their economies. The main accounting issue that arises from government grants is how to deal with the benefit that the grant represents. IAS 20 adopts an income approach, whereby grants are recognised in profit or loss in the same periods as the entity recognises as expenses the costs that the grants are intended to compensate. [IAS 20.12]. This chapter covers the requirements of IAS 20 regarding recognition, measurement and presentation of government grants and looks at specific examples of government grants including low interest and forgivable loans. It also covers disclosure requirements for grants and other forms of government assistance. What you need to know • Grants are not recognised until there is reasonable assurance that the entity is eligible as a result of a past event. • IAS 20 adopts an income approach to the recognition of government grants, regardless of whether the grant is received in cash, as a reduction in a liability to the government or in the form of a transfer of a non-monetary asset. • Grants relating to specific expenses are recognised in profit or loss in the same periods as those expenses, and grants relating to depreciable assets are recognised in profit or loss in the same periods as the related depreciation expense. • Grants relating to assets must be presented in the statement of financial position either by setting up the grant as deferred income or by deducting the grant in arriving at the carrying amount of the asset. • Grants relating to income must be presented in the income statement either separately or by deduction in reporting the related expense. • A grant received in the form of a non-monetary asset can be recognised at fair value or, alternatively, at a nominal amount. • The nature of significant other forms of government assistance that do not meet the definition of a government grant must be disclosed. The only substantive amendment since issue was in 2008, requiring entities to quantify the benefit of a government loan at a below-market rate of interest. [IAS 20.10A, IAS 20.43]. IAS 20 defines government grants in terms of ”transfers of resources to an entity in return for meeting certain conditions relating to the operating activities of the entity”. [IAS 20.3]. SIC-10 Government Assistance - No Specific Relation to Operating Activities was issued in 1998 to clarify that IAS 20 applies even if the only condition is a requirement to operate in certain regions or industry sectors (see section 2.2.2 below). Government grants related to biological assets are excluded from the scope of IAS 20 and are dealt with in IAS 41 Agriculture, which has different accounting requirements (see section 5 below and Chapter 38 at section 3.3). [IAS 20.2(d)]. The standard pre-dates the IASB’s Conceptual Framework and, as the IASB itself has noted, is inconsistent with it,1 resulting in the recognition in the statement of financial position of deferred credits that do not meet the Framework’s definitions of liabilities and allowing alternatives to initial measurement that could result in an asset being understated by reference to the Framework. IAS 20 is the only standard left that takes an income approach instead of the balance sheet approach for the recognition of income and expense applied in the rest of the IFRS accounting standards. The standard recognises that an entity may receive other forms of government assistance which cannot reasonably have a value placed upon them. Rather than prescribe how these must be accounted for, it requires disclosure about such assistance. [IAS 20.34, IAS 20.36]. However, since the issuance of IFRS 13 Fair Value Measurement, the challenge of placing a value on forms of assistance has reduced (see section 3.8 below). For example, IAS 20 cites free technical or marketing advice and the provision of guarantees as examples of government assistance that cannot reasonably have a value placed on them and hence are excluded from the definition of grants. [IAS 20.35]. [IAS 20.3, IAS 20.38]. 2.2 Government grants 2.2.1 Definitions Government grants are a specific form of government assistance. [IAS 20.3]. This ruling was made to avoid any suggestion that such forms of assistance were not governed by IAS 20 and could be credited directly to equity. 2.2.3 Determining the recipient of a government grant It may not always be clear who the recipient of a government grant is due to the involvement of multiple parties. In certain cases, an entity might receive cash from the government but is merely acting as a ‘facilitator’ to ensure the benefit of the cash is passed on to the intended beneficiary of the government support. In cases where there are multiple parties, it is important to have a full understanding of the terms of the relevant government support scheme to determine which party should be recognising a government grant. Illustration 2-1: Recipient of a government grant A government has a scheme in place whereby they will provide CU1,000 of financial support to commercial electricity users over 12 months. This support will be provided by way of part settlement of their energy bills. The government provides cash to energy suppliers, and this must be used only for the partial settlement of customer bills. The terms of the scheme state that it does not reduce the amount the energy supplier is able to charge its electricity customers. Any cash remaining after the 12 months must be returned to the government. In this case, who is the recipient of the government grant, is it the supplier or the electricity users? Although the energy supplier receives a resource (cash) from the government, it is required to pass that resource on to its customer. The energy supplier still has a right to supply energy at the price in the customer contract, so the provision of support does not affect the supplier’s contract with its customer. It is merely, the settlement party that has changed, i.e., the energy bill is now part settled by the customer and part settled by the government. This is consistent with the discussion in the Basis for Conclusions on paragraph BC187 in IFRS 15 Revenue from Contracts with Customers which clarifies that the amounts to which the entity has rights under the present contract can be paid by any party. It is therefore appropriate, in this case, to consider the customer (not the supplier) as the grant recipient. The supplier is merely facilitating the provision of support to the customer and recognises a liability upon receiving cash from the government that it must use for the partial settlement of customer bills. 2.3 Scope exclusions 2.3.1 General considerations IAS 20 does not deal with: (a) Accounting for government grants if the entity prepares financial information that reflects the effects of changing prices, whether as financial statements or in supplementary information of a similar nature (b) Government assistance in the form of benefits that are available in determining taxable profit or loss or are determined or limited on the basis of income tax liability, e.g., income tax holidays, investment tax credits, accelerated depreciation allowances and reduced income tax rates (c) Government participation in the ownership of the entity And (d) Government grants covered by IAS 41 [IAS 20.2] The accounting treatment of government assistance either provided by way of a reduction in taxable profit or loss, or determined or limited according to an entity’s income tax liability, is discussed in the context of investment tax credits in section 2.3.2 below and in Chapter 29 at section 4.3. In the case of exclusion (c) above, for an entity in which the government is a shareholder, it will be necessary to determine whether any assistance being provided to the entity is being given by the government acting in its capacity as a shareholder. If so, such assistance will not be within the scope of IAS 20 and will be accounted for in equity, in common with other shareholder transactions (see Chapter 3 at section 3.3). The contract includes a grant relating to the development of a vaccine and an obligation to deliver a specific quantity of vaccines, with an option to purchase additional vaccines at a discount. How does the entity allocate the contract between IFRS 15 and IAS 20? IFRS 15 applies to all contracts with customers, except when certain other standards apply. [IFRS 15.5]. When these other standards apply, entities are required to consider firstly the other standard before applying IFRS 15. [IFRS 15.7]. IAS 20 is not included in this list of other standards and accordingly the requirements of IFRS 15.7 do not apply for such contracts (this is discussed in detail in Chapter 28 at section 2.5). Government grants Recognition and measurement 3.1 General requirements of IAS 20 IAS 20 requires that: [IAS 20.7] Extract from IAS 20 Government grants, including non-monetary grants at fair value, shall not be recognised until there is reasonable assurance that: (a) the entity will comply with the conditions attaching to them; and (b) the grants will be received. The standard does not define ‘reasonable assurance’, which raises the question of whether or not it means the same as ‘probable’ (or ‘more likely than not’). [IAS 37.15]. The phrase ‘reasonable assurance’ is generally interpreted as being a high threshold which implies a significantly higher probability than ‘more likely than not’. Therefore, an entity cannot recognise government grants before it is significantly more likely than probable that the entity will comply with the conditions attached to them and that the grants will be received. In assessing the likelihood of compliance with conditions attached to the grant, it is acknowledged the conditions may relate to future performance and other future events. For a grant related to an asset, a grant cannot be recognised before the asset is acquired, regardless of how likely the acquisition of the asset is. If the grant is also conditional on maintaining a number of jobs to operate the asset, the grant must be recognised as soon as the asset is acquired as long as there is (and continues to be throughout the period during which the grant can be claimed back by government) reasonable assurance that the jobs will be maintained as required. The standard notes that receiving a grant does not of itself provide conclusive evidence that the conditions attaching to the grant have been or will be fulfilled. [IAS 20.8]. This is particularly true since there sometimes exists a supranational legal framework to comply with in addition to the eligibility criteria in local jurisdictions. For example, in the EU, Member State aids also need to comply with EU laws, e.g., in the context of the coronavirus pandemic. A grant may need to be recognised before it is received (in cash or otherwise) or even before the entity formally files a request to receive the grant (i.e., as soon as it becomes eligible to receive the grant). The grant receivable would not generally be a financial asset as it does not arise from contractual rights but rather arises due to government legislation. The assessment about the existence of a ‘reasonable assurance’ must be made before the initial recognition of any grant income as well as subsequently, on a continuous basis, until the end of the period during which any grant received could be claimed back by the government. Such assessment requires the entity to understand fully the exact conditions that apply before it can make a valid judgement on whether it is entitled to the grant, and to what extent. The Illustration below considers two examples. Illustration 3-1: Recognition of a government grant (a) A government has announced that, as a result of the coronavirus pandemic, it is introducing a scheme whereby entities will receive financial support for their employee costs. This support is available to all companies, there are no performance conditions and once the entity submits details of all their employees, there is a routine verification process to confirm the identity of the applicant and the grant is automatically paid. In this Illustration, there are no conditions attached and the scheme is very simple by design so that the outcome is known by the entity with certainty. Hence, assessing whether there is ‘reasonable assurance’ is relatively straight-forward. (b) A government, with the aim of driving investment in artificial intelligence, has announced a scheme whereby they will provide entities in this industry financial support for the next two years but only for very specific cost types. The application process is complex and requires applicants to submit detailed cost information and forecasts alongside the specific amount of funding they will require to cover both past and future costs. Once the submission is made, there is an extensive approval process by an independent reviewer, which may require further information to be submitted by the entity as the approval process progresses. As this is a new scheme, there is no past experience of approvals that the industry can consider. In this Illustration, once due care has been applied by the entity to identify eligible projects and costs as set out in the scheme rules, and provide appropriate documentation, it will normally be reasonably assured that the entity will comply with the conditions attaching to the grant. However, caution may need to be exercised when measuring the amount expected to be received. Government grants When the terms and conditions of a grant have been communicated before the end of a reporting period, then in determining whether it is reasonably assured that the entity will comply with the conditions attached to a grant and that the grant will be received as per IAS 20.7, information obtained after the reporting period, (e.g., confirmation of the entity receiving the grant), may provide evidence of an adjusting subsequent event. Conversely, no ‘reasonable assurance’ can exist if the terms and conditions of a grant have not been communicated before the end of a reporting period. How we see it In our view, entitlement to a grant does not exist before the terms of the grant have been established by the government and clearly communicated publicly, or confirmed to the entity for entity-specific grants. If a new law or regulation introduces a government grant after the end of the reporting period, it will represent a non-adjusting subsequent event. Government grants 3.2 Non-monetary grants A government grant in the form of a transfer of a non-monetary asset, such as land or other resources, which is intended for use by the entity, is usually recognised at the fair value of that asset. [IAS 20.23]. Fair value is defined in IFRS 13 and applies when another IFRS accounting standard requires or permits fair value measurement, including IAS 20. [IFRS 13.5, IAS 20.45]. “Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date”. [IAS 20.3, IFRS 13.9]. The requirements of IFRS 13 are discussed in Chapter 15. An alternative of recognising such assets, and the related grant, at a nominal amount is permitted, effectively leading to no recognition and a pure disclosure approach. [IAS 20.23]. This alternative is available even if the fair value of the asset differs materially from the nominal amount. Under IAS 8 an entity must select an accounting policy and apply it consistently to all non-monetary government grants. [IAS 8.13]. Despite IAS 20 permitting two approaches to measuring non-monetary grants, it is acknowledged that regulators in some jurisdictions may have a preference for one approach over the other. 3.3 Forgivable loans A forgivable loan from government, the repayment of which will be waived under certain prescribed conditions, [IAS 20.3], is to be treated as a government grant when there is reasonable assurance that the entity will meet the terms for forgiveness of the loan. [IAS 20.10]. Illustration 3-4: Government grant by way of forgivable loan An entity participates in a government-sponsored research and development programme under which it is entitled to receive a government grant of up to 50% of the costs incurred for a particular project. The government grant is interest-bearing and fully repayable based on a percentage (‘royalty’) of the sales revenue of any products developed. Although the repayment period is not limited, no repayment is required if there are no sales of the products. The entity must account for this type of government grant as a forgivable loan, as follows: • Initially recognise the cash received as a liability. • Apply the principles underlying the effective interest rate method in subsequent periods, which would involve estimating the amount and timing of future cash flows. • Review at each reporting date whether there is reasonable assurance that the entity will meet the terms for forgiveness of part, or all of the loan based on expected future royalty repayments. If this is the case, then IAS 20.10 would require derecognition of part or all of the liability initially recorded with a corresponding profit in the income statement. And • If the entity subsequently revises its estimates of future sales upwards, it adjusts the amount of the liability and recognises a corresponding loss in the income statement for any amounts previously included in profit. However, an arrangement only meets the definition of a forgivable loan if its terms provide for circumstances where repayment would be waived (i.e., forgiven), without any other form of settlement. In Illustration 3-4 above, the entity either repays some or all of the loan if the project generates any sales, or, if no sales are made, its liability is waived in full, with no further recourse to the government. Grants must not be credited directly to equity. [IAS 20.15]. Income recognition on a receipts basis, which is not in accordance with the accruals accounting assumption, is only acceptable if there is no basis for allocating a grant to periods other than the one in which it is received. [IAS 20.16]. IAS 20 rejects a ‘capital approach’, under which a grant is recognised outside profit or loss (typically credited directly to equity), [IAS 20.13], in favour of the ‘income approach’, under which grants are taken to income over one or more periods, because: (a) Government grants are receipts from a source other than shareholders. As such, they are not credited directly to equity but must be recognised in profit or loss in appropriate periods. (b) Government grants are rarely gratuitous. An entity earns them through compliance with their conditions and meeting the envisaged obligations. They must therefore be recognised in profit or loss over the periods in which the entity recognises the associated costs which the grant is intended to compensate. (c) As income and other taxes are expenses, it is logical to deal also with government grants, which are an extension of fiscal policies, in profit or loss. [IAS 20.15]. IAS 20 envisages that in most cases, the periods over which an entity recognises the costs or expenses related to the government grant are readily ascertainable and thus grants in recognition of specific expenses are recognised as income in the same period as the relevant expense. [IAS 20.17]. Grants related to depreciable assets are recognised as income over the periods in which, and in proportion to, depreciation on those assets is charged. [IAS 20.17]. Grants related to non-depreciable assets may also require the fulfilment of certain obligations, in which case they would be recognised as income over the periods in which the costs of meeting the obligations are incurred. For example, a grant of land may be conditional upon the erection of a building on the site, and it may be appropriate to recognise it as income over the life of the building. [IAS 20.18]. IAS 20 acknowledges that grants may be received as part of a package of financial or fiscal aids to which a number of conditions are attached. In such cases, the standard indicates that care is needed in identifying the conditions giving rise to the costs and expenses which determine the periods over which the grant will be recognised as income. It may also be appropriate to allocate part of the grant on one basis and part on another. [IAS 20.19]. Where a grant relates to expenses or losses already incurred or becomes receivable for the purpose of giving immediate financial support to the entity with no future related costs, the grant must be recognised in income when it becomes receivable, and the entity must disclose its effects to ensure that these are clearly understood. [IAS 20.20, IAS 20.21, IAS 20.22]. The reference to ‘expenses or losses already incurred’ relates to expenses already recognised in the income statement. Where an entity has already incurred costs but they have been capitalised, then the grant received will be recognised in profit or loss as the depreciation expense on the asset is recognised. [IAS 20.17]. Frequently asked questions Question 4-1: Assuming an entity elects not to deduct the government grant from the related expense, can an entity present government grants related to income as revenue? When grants related to income are not deducted from the related expense, IAS 20 is not prescriptive, stating that such grants are presented “… either separately, or under a general heading such as ‘Other income’”. [IAS 20.29]. A question, therefore, arises as to whether it may be appropriate to present income from grants as revenue on the face of the income statement either within one revenue line item with appropriate disaggregation in the notes to the financial statements or shown as a separate line item on the face of the income statement (i.e., Revenue from grants) with inclusion in a revenue subtotal. This view is consistent with the Basis for Conclusions on IFRS 15, which says that an entity can present interest income as revenue only when interest income represents income from an entity’s ordinary activities (discussed in Chapter 28 at section 5.5.5). [IFRS 15.BC247]. It is important to distinguish between those transactions which arise in the course of an entity’s ordinary activities, and therefore may be presented as revenue, and transactions that are incidental to the main revenue-generating activities, but do not generate revenue themselves. How we see it IFRS 15 defines revenue as income arising in the course of an entity’s ordinary activities. Determining whether a government grant is income arising in the course of an entity’s ordinary activities is a matter of judgement after considering all the facts and circumstances relevant to an entity. Facts to consider relevant to this judgement include consideration of the business model of the entity as well as the frequency and purpose of grants received. Accordingly, some grants may be presented as revenue while others, that are determined not to arise in the course of an entity’s ordinary activities, cannot be presented as revenue. However, any grant revenue recognised that is outside the scope of IFRS 15 will need to be presented either separately from revenue from contracts with customers within the income statement or disclosed separately within the notes. This is because entities are required to present in the statement of comprehensive income, or disclose within the notes, the amount of revenue recognised from contracts with customers separately from other sources of revenue. The presentation approach must be applied consistently to all similar grants and appropriately disclosed. Grants that have the same characteristics must follow the same presentational approach on a consistent basis. In its current financial year, it has also received government support to compensate for the salaries of furloughed employees due to the coronavirus pandemic. Company A judges the R&D credits and the furlough grant to have different characteristics. However, neither IAS 20 nor IAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows (see Chapter 36 at section 4.4.15) provide unambiguous guidance on where in the statement of cash flows to present the cash inflows from the receipt of government grants. The IFRS IC discussed the presentation of cash flows for several fact patterns including government grants related to assets in July 2012.5 The issue was further discussed in March 2013. The IFRS IC observed that the primary principle behind the classification of cash flows in IAS 7 is that cash flows are classified on the basis of the nature of the activity in a manner that is most appropriate to the business of the entity in accordance with IAS 7.11 and with the definitions of operating, investing and financing activities in IAS 7.6. In the course of the discussions held, staff initially suggested that grants were providing a source of financing to the entity, and consequently cash received would be part of the entity’s financing activities. In a later meeting, however, it reached a different conclusion that cash received from a government grant is presented as an operating cash flow instead, on the basis that although cash inflows from a grant provide the entity with financing for the designated asset/activity, a grant does not represent a borrowing activity because there is no expectation that the resource will be returned to the provider of finance; or that the provider of finance will be appropriately compensated. Eventually, the IFRS IC concluded that clarifying the application of the primary classification principle in IAS 7 was a matter too broad for it to address and discontinued its work.6 Consequently, until and unless the IASB addresses this issue as part of its upcoming project on the cash flow statement, there is likely to be diversity in practice for the presentation of all grants in the statement of cash flows, whether related to assets or to income. Cash flows received from grants could be considered to be financing by nature because they provide a source of financing to the entity. Alternatively, cash flows received from grants (or only those from grants related to income) could be presented as operating cash flows by default since they do not seem to meet the definition of financing activities and cash flows received from grants related to assets could be presented as investing cash flows as they relate to an investing activity. Further discussion on cash flow classification can be found in Chapter 36 at section 4. How we see it Selecting the most appropriate presentation will be a matter of judgement. Once adopted, the presentation principles must be applied consistently and clear disclosure of the principles and the impact thereof on the cash flow statement must be provided in accordance with IAS 1. IAS 20 does makes clear, however, that cash flows from government grants must be disclosed as separate items in the cash flow statement, even as regards grants related to assets, when such grants are deducted from the related assets for the purpose of presentation in the statement of financial position. [IAS 20.28]. Lastly, cash flows from refundable advances must be presented as financing cash flows, whereas their transformation into a grant, if and when the entity meets the conditions for forgiveness of the loan, is a non-cash transaction. **中文:** 初始确认时，类似五年期贷款的市场年利率为10%。该贷款的初始公允价值为未来还款额按类似贷款市场利率10%折现五年的现值，即811货币单位。政府给予公司A的激励价值为189货币单位，即收到的总对价1,000货币单位与贷款初始公允价值811货币单位之间的差额。该差额作为政府补助处理。

**中文:** 在本示例中，该补助补偿了公司A本应承担的较高市场利率利息费用。公司A在贷款期限内按与利息计提相同的基础确认补助收益，从而使收益与利息成本相匹配。

**中文:** 3.6.3 补助受益期间 IAS 20提醒，在识别导致确定补助赚取期间的成本和费用条件时需谨慎。[IAS 20.19] 必须满足的资格条件并不一定是补助受益期间的最终证据。例如，若资产在特定期间内停止用于合格用途，某些补助可能需要偿还；尽管存在此条件，补助仍可能需在资产整个使用寿命期间确认，而非仅在合格期间内确认。

**中文:** 3.6.4 将补助分解为组成部分 收到的补助可能与一揽子活动相关，其各组成部分具有不同的成本和条件。在此类情况下，补助所对应的组成部分通常未被明确识别或量化。可能适宜对不同组成部分采用不同处理基础，而非对整项补助采用单一会计处理方法，[IAS 20.19] 但这并非总是切实可行。例如，补助可能基于以下条件发放：主体在特定领域进行批准的资本支出，并在约定期间内雇佣规定数量的当地员工。一般而言，若可能作出此类解释，特别是当补助的收取取决于长期资产的购置成本时，最直接的确认方式是将补助与长期资产挂钩。然而，除非有明确相反迹象，否则方可采用此方法。

**中文:** 政府补助 我们的观点 评估适当的分配方法要求主体识别补助拟补偿的成本，并需基于对事实和情况的仔细分析进行判断。

**中文:** 最终确定何种处理方式更为恰当，不仅需反映协议条款，还应考虑可能揭示贷款目的的其他事实。将补助在各项组成部分之间进行分配通常需要运用判断，主体可能对某些特征的重视程度高于其他特征。在此类情况下，若影响重大，财务报表必须明确说明在应用该准则时所作出的判断，并披露相应的财务影响。3.7 政府补助的偿还 确认后需偿还的政府补助应作为会计估计变更进行会计处理。主体必须审慎评估初始确认补助时所需的"合理保证"是否存在，以便若补助后续需偿还时，这并非对前期差错的更正，而是因情况变化或新信息所致的结果。[IAS 8.34] 与收益相关的补助偿还，应首先冲减相关的未摊销递延收益，超出部分立即确认为费用。[IAS 20.32] 与资产相关的补助偿还，应通过增加相关资产的账面价值或减少相关的未摊销递延收益予以确认。若无该补助本应截至当期累计确认的额外折旧，应立即计入损益。[IAS 20.32] IAS 20强调，导致与资产相关的补助需偿还的情况，可能要求考虑该资产是否发生减值。[IAS 20.33]

**中文:** 3.8 政府援助 摘自《国际会计准则第20号》 第3段中政府补助的定义排除了某些无法合理确定其价值的政府援助形式，以及无法与企业正常交易活动区分的政府交易。**[《国际会计准则第20号》第34段]。**

**中文:** 自1984年《国际会计准则第20号》首次生效以来，[《国际会计准则第20号》第41段]，公允价值在财务报告中的应用以及用于确定可靠公允价值计量方法的技术范围均有所扩大。2011年发布的《国际财务报告准则第13号》定义了公允价值，提供了基于原则的框架以计量国际财务报告准则下的公允价值，并要求披露这些公允价值计量的相关信息。[《国际财务报告准则第13号》第1段]。《国际财务报告准则第13号》为选择适当的估值技术提供了指引，因此，目前可以预期无法合理确定价值的政府援助情况相对罕见（参见第15章）。《国际会计准则第20号》将政府采购政策作为无法与企业正常交易活动区分的援助示例。在这种情况下，“利益的存在可能毋庸置疑，但任何试图将交易活动与政府援助分离的做法都可能具有随意性”。[《国际会计准则第20号》第35段]。因此，该准则要求披露重要的政府援助。[《国际会计准则第20号》第36段、第39段(b)项]。

**中文:** 因此，问题在于：将补助收入作为收益列报于利润表表内是否恰当——可将其纳入单一收入项目并在财务报表附注中适当分解，或作为单独项目（即“补助收入”）列示于利润表表内并计入收入小计。这一观点与《国际财务报告准则第15号》结论基础一致，其中指出：只有当利息收入源于主体日常活动产生的收入时，主体方可将其作为收入列报（详见第28章第5.5.5节）[IFRS 15.BC247]。区分以下两类交易至关重要：一类产生于主体日常活动过程中，因此可作为收入列报；另一类虽与主要创收活动相关但本身不产生收入，属于附带交易。我们认为，《国际财务报告准则第15号》将收入定义为主体日常活动过程中产生的收益。判断政府补助是否属于主体日常活动过程中产生的收益，需在综合考虑主体所有相关事实和情况后进行专业判断。影响该判断的相关事实包括：主体的业务模式、所获补助的频率及目的。据此，部分补助可作为收入列报，而经判断不属于主体日常活动过程中产生的补助则不得作为收入列报。但任何确认的补助收入若超出《国际财务报告准则第15号》范围，则需在利润表中与客户合同收入分开列报，或在附注中单独披露。

**中文:** 在讨论过程中，工作人员最初认为政府补助为实体提供了融资来源，因此收到的现金应属于实体融资活动的一部分。然而，在后续会议中，工作人员得出了不同结论：政府补助收到的现金应作为经营活动现金流量列报，理由是尽管补助产生的现金流入为实体指定资产/活动提供了融资，但补助并不代表借款活动，因为不存在资源需返还给资金提供方或资金提供方将获得适当补偿的预期。最终，国际财务报告准则解释委员会（IFRS IC）得出结论，澄清《国际会计准则第7号》（IAS 7）主要分类原则的应用问题过于宽泛，超出其处理范围，因此终止了相关工作。6 因此，除非国际会计准则理事会（IASB）在其即将开展的现金流量表项目中解决此问题，否则所有补助（无论与资产相关还是与收益相关）在现金流量表中的列报实务中很可能存在差异。从性质上看，补助收到的现金流量可被视为融资活动，因其为实体提供了融资来源。或者，补助（或仅与收益相关的补助）收到的现金流量可默认作为经营活动现金流量列报，因其似乎不符合融资活动的定义；而与资产相关的补助收到的现金流量则可作为投资活动现金流量列报，因其与投资活动相关。关于现金流量分类的进一步讨论，请参见第36章第4节。我们的观点 选择最恰当的列报方式需依赖专业判断。
